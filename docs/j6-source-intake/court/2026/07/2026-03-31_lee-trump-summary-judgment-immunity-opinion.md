## Material development

On March 31, 2026, Judge Amit P. Mehta issued a 79-page memorandum opinion and order in consolidated civil cases arising from January 6. The court largely denied Donald J. Trump's motion for summary judgment on official-acts immunity, while granting immunity for specific conduct that the court classified as official acts.

The court also denied Trump's motion to reconsider the earlier denial of his First Amendment dismissal argument, but certified the First Amendment question for interlocutory review. It granted the plaintiffs' motion to strike the United States' Westfall Act certification, granted judicial notice only to the extent relied upon, and denied Trump's objection to the Select Committee report to the extent the court cited facts it believed could be converted into admissible evidence.

## Conduct the court treated as official

The opinion states that the conduct protected by official-acts immunity included:

- the Oval Office meeting with Michigan state legislators;
- the January 6 tweets sent at approximately 2:24 p.m. and 6:01 p.m.; and
- the January 6 Rose Garden remarks.

The opinion states that other challenged conduct, including the Ellipse speech, most contacts with state and local officials, nearly all other tweets, and various additional activities, was not protected by official-acts immunity at the summary-judgment stage.

## What the ruling did not decide

- It did not establish civil liability or damages.
- It did not finally resolve the consolidated cases.
- It did not determine the truth of every allegation in the complaints.
- It did not make a moral-character judgment about Donald Trump or any January 6 defendant.
- It did not convert allegations, advocacy, or disputed evidence into final adjudicated facts beyond the court's procedural holdings.
- Later district-court and appellate proceedings may modify the legal treatment of specific conduct.
