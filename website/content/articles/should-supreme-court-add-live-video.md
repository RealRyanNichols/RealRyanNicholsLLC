The Supreme Court opened a new term this morning.

The official Court website said the justices would convene for a public session at 10 a.m. Eastern. It also said the audio feed would be livestreamed and the recording would be available later in the day.

That is real access. A person in East Texas does not have to stand on a Washington sidewalk to hear the questions asked in the highest court in the country.

But hearing is not seeing.

The Court’s public pages describe limited courtroom seating, same-day transcripts, and live audio. They do not offer the public a live video feed of oral argument.

So on the first Monday in October, the question is worth asking again: should the Supreme Court add a camera?

Facts and official links in this article were checked at 4:02 a.m. Central on October 5, 2026.

## What the public can access now

The Court says a term begins by statute on the first Monday in October. Its [procedures page](https://www.supremecourt.gov/about/procedures.aspx) explains that the justices enter the courtroom at 10 a.m. when the Court is in session.

The [oral-arguments page](https://www.supremecourt.gov/oral_arguments/oral_arguments.aspx) says arguments are generally scheduled on Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday mornings from the first Monday in October through April. It also says a live audio feed is streamed and posted later.

The [availability page for transcripts and recordings](https://www.supremecourt.gov/oral_arguments/availabilityoforalargumenttranscripts.aspx) says same-day transcripts have been available free on the Court’s website since the October 2006 term, and all oral-argument audio has been available there since the October 2010 term.

In-person access exists, too, but seats are limited. The Court’s [courtroom-seating page](https://www.supremecourt.gov/oral_arguments/courtroomseating.aspx) says arguments are open to the public and describes a pilot lottery alongside first-come seating.

Those are meaningful steps. The question is whether they are the final step.

## The strongest case for video

The Court decides questions that reach every state, business, school, police department, newsroom, and household.

When a justice asks a lawyer to define the limiting principle of an argument, the words matter. So do pace, hesitation, interruption, the use of documents, and the physical rhythm of the exchange.

Video can make a difficult institution easier to understand. It can show that oral argument is not a television trial and not a campaign rally. It is a structured test of legal positions.

The public already pays attention through secondhand clips, live blogs, quotations, and commentary. A full official feed could give people the primary source before somebody else cuts it into the most inflammatory fifteen seconds.

There is also a geographic fairness argument. A person who lives near Washington can compete for a seat. A teacher in Longview, a student in Marshall, or a small-business owner in Jefferson cannot casually do that. Live audio closes part of the distance. Video would close more.

Public institutions earn trust by letting people see the work.

## The strongest case against video

A camera changes a room.

Lawyers may shape a line for an audience outside the Court. Justices may be clipped out of context. A difficult hypothetical could circulate as if it were a ruling. A facial expression caught between questions could become a partisan meme before the argument ends.

The current audio system already gives the public the full words without turning every participant into a television figure.

There is also a difference between transparency and performance. Oral argument is not the whole case. The briefs carry the factual record and legal authorities. The final opinion explains the judgment. Video might pull attention toward the most visible hour and away from the thousands of pages that actually define the dispute.

Security and courtroom administration matter as well. A permanent video system would need fixed rules about angles, documents, counsel tables, spectators, technical failure, archiving, and reuse.

The strongest caution is simple: do not damage the Court’s working process just to make it look more open.

## Ryan’s Take

Add the camera.

Use a fixed institutional feed. No reaction shots. No panning to spectators. No producer hunting for drama. Keep the frame on the lectern and bench. Post the full video with the transcript and audio, not clipped highlights.

Then let people see it.

The fear that somebody will misuse a clip is real. It is also not a reason to keep the primary source smaller than it has to be. Bad edits are answered with the complete record.

The Court has already crossed the central line by livestreaming audio. The public can hear every question in real time. Adding a restrained official picture would not turn the argument into entertainment unless the Court designed it that way.

This site keeps returning to the same principle. [Important public information should be inspectable](/posts/should-texas-publish-data-center-water-use). [Labels should make synthetic political media easier to judge](/posts/should-texas-label-every-ai-political-ad). Even a historic unanimous vote becomes clearer when the public can see [how the door finally opened](/posts/the-vote-was-99-to-0-the-door-had-never-opened-before).

Access should lead back to the whole record.

One fixed feed. Full arguments. Same-day archive. No theatrical production.

Would live video make the Supreme Court more accountable, or would it make oral argument more performative?
