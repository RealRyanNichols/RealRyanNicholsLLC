On January 20, 2025, President Donald J. Trump granted clemency to nearly 1,600 people convicted in connection with the events of January 6, 2021.

I was among those whose charges were addressed under that action.

My federal charges were dismissed with prejudice.

I have been living with that outcome for 17 months now — watching the coverage, watching the political arguments, watching the same people who called me a traitor for years try to reframe the pardons in ways that fit their original narrative instead of the actual record.

This article is my attempt to put the record straight.

## What the pardons actually did

The coverage of the January 20 pardons has been selective from the beginning.

Here is what actually happened:

Trump granted clemency to nearly 1,600 defendants. He commuted the sentences of 14 to "time served," making them eligible for immediate release. He pardoned the rest, clearing their federal convictions.

As of the date of this article, the DOJ has continued to move to dismiss cases for some defendants. On May 22, 2026, the government moved to dismiss indictments against eight defendants. U.S. District Judge Amit P. Mehta rejected that motion, said the government had not provided an adequate reason, and gave the DOJ until June 5, 2026 to do better.

That is a federal judge telling the Justice Department that simply moving to dismiss January 6 cases without adequate justification is not acceptable procedure. That story has received very little coverage compared to the headlines about re-arrests.

## The re-arrest headlines — what they include and what they leave out

Yes, some people who were pardoned have been arrested again for **new offenses** committed after the pardon.

I want to be precise about that: a pardon covers past conduct. It does not cover future conduct. If someone who was pardoned committed a new crime after their pardon, they are being prosecuted for that new crime. That is how pardons work. That is how the law works.

The coverage often presents this as evidence that the pardons themselves were wrong. That is a different argument. And it is an argument that applies to every pardon ever granted, not just J6 pardons. When you pardon someone, you are not guaranteeing their future behavior. You are making a judgment about their past case.

People disagree about whether the J6 pardons were the right call. That is a legitimate debate.

What is not legitimate is treating new offenses committed after a pardon as evidence that the original pardoned conduct deserved punishment — without being transparent about the distinction.

## The "traitor" label does not go away

One of the things I have documented on this site over the past several months is the persistence of the "traitor" label against J6 defendants even after the pardons.

I documented it in [this article](/posts/every-day-they-tell-me-to-die). I documented it in [the receipts wall](/posts/the-receipts-wall-every-threat-x-let-stay-up). I documented it in [the master exhibit index](/posts/master-exhibit-index).

The pattern is:

1. Call a J6 defendant a traitor.
2. Attach prison, death, or "no rights" language to that label.
3. Repeat regardless of what courts have decided.

That pattern did not stop when I was pardoned. It did not stop when my charges were dismissed with prejudice. It keeps going because the label is politically useful to the people using it — and because the legal outcome of the case is irrelevant to them.

I want to be clear about something: I understand that people genuinely disagree about January 6. I understand that some people believe the events of that day were more serious than the pardons acknowledged. Those are real views held by real people.

What I am documenting is different. I am documenting the people who use the "traitor" label as permission to wish harm on me specifically — in public, on a platform, under posts about my time in solitary confinement.

That is not political disagreement. That is something else. And the record shows it.

## What the Rebecca Lavrenz story actually shows

One detail from the pardon coverage that I think deserves more attention:

Rebecca Lavrenz — known on social media as the "J6 Praying Grandma" — **declined her pardon**.

She said she planned to appeal her case and get her criminal record cleared through the courts, not through executive clemency.

Think about what that means. She believed her case was strong enough on the legal merits that she did not need a pardon. She wanted a full legal vindication, not a political act.

That is not the story of someone who was guilty and got away with it. That is the story of someone who thought the justice system, properly applied, would reach the right result — and chose to pursue that instead of accepting clemency.

That story got buried under the re-arrest headlines.

## Where things stand on June 4, 2026

Here is the current state of the record as I understand it:

- Nearly 1,600 defendants were granted clemency on January 20, 2025.
- Some had sentences commuted; most had convictions pardoned.
- Some have been arrested for new offenses committed after their pardons.
- The DOJ continues to move to dismiss cases for remaining defendants.
- At least one federal judge has pushed back on those dismissal motions, demanding adequate justification.
- The DOJ has a deadline of June 5, 2026 to respond to Judge Mehta's demand.
- Some defendants, like Lavrenz, declined pardons and are pursuing legal appeals.

None of that is a simple story. The coverage treats it as one.
