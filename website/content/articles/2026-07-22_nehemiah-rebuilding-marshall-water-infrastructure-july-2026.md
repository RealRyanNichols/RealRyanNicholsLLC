*By the Real Ryan Nichols Editorial Team*  
*Current-news facts last verified: July 22, 2026*

Nehemiah did not begin rebuilding Jerusalem's wall by pretending the damage was smaller than it was.

He listened to a painful report. He mourned and prayed. He sought authority and resources. Then, after reaching Jerusalem, he inspected the broken wall at night before announcing a plan.

Only then did he tell the people, **“Let us rise up and build.”**

That order matters: grief, prayer, inspection, planning, shared work, endurance.

Marshall, Texas, is dealing with a very different kind of broken infrastructure. Three major water-line failures in February and March exposed the vulnerability of an aging system. This week, the City Council is scheduled to consider easements for a possible Spring Street transmission-line replacement and a state grant application.

The Bible does not determine the right pipe size, easement price, construction route, or funding package. It does offer a faithful posture for public rebuilding: tell the truth about what is broken, examine it carefully, organize the work, and keep serving when the repair is difficult.

## Nehemiah's story in plain language

The story appears in [Nehemiah 1–4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Nehemiah%201-4&version=KJV).

Nehemiah was a Jewish official serving King Artaxerxes in Susa during Persian rule, after some Jewish exiles had returned to Judah. Jerusalem's temple had been rebuilt, but the city's wall and gates remained damaged.

When visitors from Judah arrived, Nehemiah asked how the people and Jerusalem were doing. The answer was blunt: the survivors were in distress, Jerusalem's wall was broken, and its gates had been burned.

Nehemiah sat down, wept, mourned, fasted, and prayed. His prayer in chapter 1 does not blame everyone else. He confesses the sins of his people and his own family, remembers God's promises, and asks for favor before the king.

### Prayer led to a responsible request

When the king noticed Nehemiah's sadness, Nehemiah explained the condition of Jerusalem and asked permission to go rebuild it. He also requested letters for safe passage and timber for the gates and his lodging.

Faith did not replace planning. Nehemiah prayed and then made a specific request for the permission and materials the work required.

### He inspected before he announced

After reaching Jerusalem, Nehemiah waited three days. Then he went out at night with a small group and examined the damaged walls and gates.

He did not begin with a speech. He first learned the condition of the thing he had come to repair.

After the inspection, he told the local leaders what he had seen, described the king's support, and invited them to rebuild together.

### The work was shared

Nehemiah 3 names families, tradespeople, priests, rulers, and residents repairing different sections. Not everyone performed the same task, but the work fit together.

The chapter also records that some people refused to help. Scripture does not sanitize the project into a story where every leader behaved well.

### Progress brought opposition

In Nehemiah 4, opponents mocked the work, threatened the builders, and tried to stop the project. Nehemiah prayed, posted guards, reorganized the people, and continued.

The lesson is not that every modern infrastructure disagreement is evil opposition. Public projects deserve scrutiny. Cost, engineering, property rights, safety, and accountability are legitimate questions.

The enduring lesson is that worthwhile rebuilding usually requires both courage and careful attention.

## The Marshall water facts—separate from the Bible story

The following comes from city records and the Texas Water Development Board. These are current civic facts, not details from Nehemiah's story.

### Three major lines failed in two months

A [May 28 City of Marshall update](https://www.marshalltexas.net/m/newsflash/Home/Detail/347) identified three major water-line failures in February and March 2026:

- a 16-inch line at U.S. 59 South, with a reported repair contract of **$345,520**;
- the 24-inch Spring Street transmission line, with a reported emergency repair contract of **$316,700**; and
- a 16-inch line at U.S. 80 East, with a reported repair contract of **$131,298**.

The March 17 Spring Street failure was the most disruptive. The city's [initial emergency notice](https://www.marshalltexas.net/m/newsflash/home/detail/320) said the break affected a major transmission line and caused low or no pressure across Marshall. Officials declared a local disaster, closed city and county offices, and Marshall ISD canceled classes.

The city issued a boil-water notice on March 18. A [March 20 recovery update](https://www.marshalltexas.net/m/newsflash/Home/Detail/325) said water service had been restored late Thursday, while officials continued refilling the system and sampling water.

### The wider system is old

Marshall's official [“Investing Today for Tomorrow” page](https://www.marshalltexas.net/647/Investing-Today-for-Tomorrow) says a 2025 review identified more than **$56 million** in critical water and wastewater improvements over six years.

The city says some water-distribution mains are more than 70 years old and wastewater equipment ranges from 25 to 50 years old. The council approved a six-year utility-rate plan in September 2025, effective January 1, 2026, to help fund system work.

Those figures describe the broader water and wastewater program. They are not a published price tag for the proposed Spring Street replacement.

### Two easements are on Thursday's agenda

The [July 23 council packet](https://marshalltx.portal.civicclerk.com/event/768/files) schedules a regular meeting for **6:00 p.m. Thursday, July 23**, at Jean Birmingham Council Chambers, 401 S. Alamo Boulevard.

Agenda items 10.A and 10.B ask the council whether to authorize the city manager to negotiate two easements for the **potential Water Supply Infrastructure Grant Spring Street Transmission Line Replacement Project**.

City staff estimates one permanent easement at approximately 0.428 acres and the second at approximately 0.573 acres. The packet lists both budget costs as “to be determined.” Authorization to negotiate is not the same thing as a final purchase agreement, construction award, or completed project.

### A grant application is on Friday's agenda

The [July 24 special-called agenda](https://marshalltx.portal.civicclerk.com/event/1859/files) schedules a meeting for **4:00 p.m. Friday, July 24**, in the same council chambers.

The single action item is whether to authorize filing a Water Supply and Infrastructure Grant application with the Texas Water Development Board and designate the city manager to submit required documents.

The [state WSIG program](https://www.twdb.texas.gov/financial/programs/WSIG/index.asp) is funded by a one-time **$1.038 billion** legislative appropriation for water-supply and water-infrastructure projects. The current application deadline is **11:59 p.m. Central on July 30, 2026**.

Applying does not guarantee an award. The amount Marshall may request, the amount the state may award, and the conditions attached to any award were not stated in the one-page special-meeting agenda reviewed for this article.

## What remains uncertain

As of July 22, several important questions remain open:

1. Will the council authorize one or both easement negotiations?
2. What prices and terms, if any, will be accepted?
3. Will the council authorize the WSIG application Friday?
4. How much grant funding will Marshall request, and how much might the state award?
5. What will the final line route, engineering scope, project cost, and construction schedule be?
6. How would a grant affect the amount Marshall must finance through utility revenue or other sources?
7. What short-term service risks will remain while a replacement project is designed and built?

Those are not reasons to dismiss the proposal. They are the details citizens should follow as the public record develops.

## What Nehemiah's example can teach without forcing it

Nehemiah is not a mascot for a city project. His story does not command residents to approve every proposal or oppose every expense.

It does challenge several unhelpful habits.

### Do not minimize the damage

Nehemiah accepted the report about Jerusalem's condition. Marshall's three major failures and its own infrastructure assessment are reasons for sober attention, not panic and not denial.

### Inspect before promising

Nehemiah examined the wall before announcing the plan. Modern rebuilding likewise requires engineering records, maps, condition assessments, cost estimates, funding terms, and a realistic schedule.

### Give people a real part in the work

Nehemiah 3 is full of names and assigned sections. Citizens cannot install a transmission main themselves, but they can read agendas, ask precise questions, conserve water during emergencies, report leaks, check on vulnerable neighbors, and hold officials to the public commitments they make.

### Expect endurance, not a quick victory speech

Emergency repair can restore service. Long-term replacement takes design, property access, money, procurement, construction, testing, and public communication.

The faithful measure is not whether leaders produce the best slogan. It is whether the system becomes safer, the records remain transparent, and the work is carried through.

## Ryan's Take

Marshall's water problem is not abstract. When a transmission line fails, families lose pressure, schools close, businesses adapt, hospitals and first responders plan around uncertainty, and residents have to decide whether the water is safe to use.

The right response is neither blind trust nor automatic cynicism.

Pray for wisdom. Read the documents. Ask what the project will cost, what the grant will cover, what remains unfunded, and how progress will be reported. Give engineers and crews room to do hard work, while expecting leaders to explain decisions in plain language.

Nehemiah's strength was not loudness. It was his willingness to face ruin honestly, inspect it carefully, organize people responsibly, and keep rebuilding.

That is a worthy standard for public service now.

## A constructive action before the votes

Marshall residents can take one concrete step: read the two agenda records and submit one focused question before or during the meetings.

The posted agendas say citizens who wish to speak are requested to complete a public-comment form and ordinarily receive three minutes. Call the City Secretary's Office at **903-935-4446** to confirm current participation procedures or request an accessibility accommodation.

Useful questions include:

- What exact section of Spring Street transmission line would the project replace?
- What is the current planning-level cost estimate?
- How much WSIG funding will the city request?
- What local match, debt, or rate-supported funding would remain?
- What milestones will be published if the grant is awarded?
- What contingency plan protects service during design and construction?

Ask for records and dates, not rumors. Disagree without attacking the people who maintain the system or the neighbors affected by the route.

## Reflection questions

2. Do I treat prayer as an escape from planning, or as preparation to plan wisely?
3. What public document can I read before repeating an opinion?
