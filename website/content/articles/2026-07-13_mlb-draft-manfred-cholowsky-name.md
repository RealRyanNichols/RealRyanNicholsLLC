Roch Cholowsky has been the presumptive #1 overall pick in the MLB Draft for over a year.

UCLA shortstop. .320/.452/.636 with 21 home runs as a junior. Every scout, every mock draft, every front office in baseball knew his name was coming off the board first months before the White Sox actually called it.

Commissioner Rob Manfred still couldn't say it right.

https://x.com/JacobRudner/status/2075998898351493630

That is the whole clip. A commissioner, a name card, a kid who has waited his entire life for this exact thirty seconds — and the guy at the podium stumbles over four syllables he has literally had a year's notice to rehearse.

## Why This Isn't Really About Manfred

I don't think Rob Manfred is a villain here. I think he's a symptom.

Look at how MLB actually treated Day 1 of its own draft this year: shoved into a broadcast window from 1:00 to 2:30 p.m., squeezed around the demands of All-Star weekend's celebrity golf event. The league that produced the number-one pick in its sport gave that moment less airtime than a exhibition round of golf.

If the event itself is a scheduling afterthought, is it really a shock that the person reading the names off the card didn't bother to nail the pronunciation of the headline name?

You can't build a signature moment for a 21-year-old kid and then treat the moment like a rounding error. Cholowsky worked his whole life for that walk to the podium. He deserved a league that acted like it mattered as much to them as it did to him.

## The Bigger Pattern

This is the same conversation I keep coming back to in different arenas: **effort is a form of respect.**

Learning to say someone's name correctly costs you nothing but attention. Skipping that step because you're rushed, distracted, or simply didn't think it was worth the prep time tells the person in front of you exactly where they rank on your priority list.

Cholowsky handled it with grace — by every account, he laughed it off, hugged his family, and got on with the business of being a first overall pick. That's on him, and it's admirable. But the standard shouldn't be "the kid was gracious about it." The standard should be "the commissioner did his homework."

MLB has spent years trying to convince a younger audience that the draft is must-watch content, that its prospects are stars in the making. You don't get to make that pitch and then hand the mic to someone who treats the moment like a chore.
