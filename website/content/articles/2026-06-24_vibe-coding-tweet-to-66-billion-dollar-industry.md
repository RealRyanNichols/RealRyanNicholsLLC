In early 2025, Andrej Karpathy — the AI researcher, former OpenAI founding member, former Tesla AI lead — posted something casual on X.

He described a new way he had been writing software: not carefully designing systems, not reading documentation, not debugging line by line. Just describing what he wanted to an AI, running whatever it gave him, and trusting the vibe.

He called it "vibe coding."

The post was not a manifesto. It was not a product announcement. It was not a research paper. It was a passing observation from a brilliant person describing what he'd been doing on a weekend.

The internet latched on immediately.

[The original vibe coding post on X →](https://x.com/i/trending/1886917395303788586)

## What Happened Next

That single phrase is now doing more cultural work than most full whitepapers.

Collins Dictionary named "vibe coding" its Word of the Year. MIT Technology Review listed it among the 10 Breakthrough Technologies of 2026. GitHub reports that 46% of all new code being written today is AI-generated. Among Y Combinator's Winter 2025 cohort, one in four startups reported codebases that were over 91% written by AI.

The industry that emerged around the tools, the platforms, and the methodology Karpathy described in a throwaway post is now valued at $6.6 billion.

Let that sit for a moment.

A casual observation on X — the kind that gets posted and forgotten a thousand times a day — named, catalyzed, and gave vocabulary to a shift that has rewritten how software gets built in less than eighteen months.

## What Vibe Coding Actually Is

Here is the honest version, not the hype.

Vibe coding is not just "using AI to write code." Developers have been using GitHub Copilot and similar tools for years. The shift is more specific than that.

Traditional coding: you understand the system you're building, you design it, you write it, you test it, you debug it. The code reflects your mental model.

Vibe coding: you describe what you want. The AI generates it. You run it. If it works, you keep going. If it doesn't, you describe the problem to the AI and let it fix itself. You are the product manager. The AI is the entire engineering team.

The best demos of this — the posts that define what Ammaar Reshi at Google AI Studio calls "the aesthetic standard of the new generation" — show someone go from idea to deployed app in under ten minutes. No IDE experience required. No computer science degree required. No mental model of the underlying system required.

That is genuinely new.

## Why This Matters Beyond Tech

I am not a software developer. I do not write production code. But I have been watching this shift and I think it matters far beyond who it affects in Silicon Valley.

The history of technology is largely a history of what becomes possible when the barrier drops.

The printing press made publishing possible for people who weren't scribes. Desktop publishing made design possible for people who weren't graphic designers. YouTube made video distribution possible for people who weren't broadcasters. Social media made mass communication possible for people who weren't journalists.

Every one of those shifts produced winners, losers, and a massive restructuring of who has power over the medium.

Vibe coding is doing that to software.

If you no longer need to know how to code to build functioning software, then the number of people who can build things just expanded by orders of magnitude. That creates new businesses, new tools, new competitors, and new problems — all at the same time.

The 92% of US developers who now use AI coding tools daily are not being replaced yet. But they are being repositioned. The ones who are thriving are the ones who understand how to direct AI systems well. The ones who are struggling are the ones whose value was in writing syntax.

That shift will not slow down.

## The Part No One Wants to Talk About

Here is the uncomfortable part of the vibe coding story.

Developer trust in AI-generated code has dropped. In 2023, 77% of developers expressed favorable opinions toward AI coding tools. In 2026, that number is 60%. Only a third of developers say they trust AI-generated code for accuracy.

Think about what that means.

We have 46% of all new code being AI-generated, written by a workforce where only a third trusts the output.

That is a trust deficit baked into the foundation of new software being built right now. The apps you use, the platforms you log into, the systems that handle your data — a growing percentage of the code running them was generated by a model that the people deploying it don't fully trust.

I am not saying it's wrong to use these tools. I am saying the gap between adoption speed and verification quality is real, and it deserves more attention than it's getting.

Vibe coding is powerful. The vibe doesn't debug itself.

## One Phrase Changed an Industry

The thing I keep coming back to is the sheer improbability of the origin.

A researcher posting on X, describing a casual weekend workflow, coined a phrase that became the vocabulary for an entire industrial shift. The post was not engineered for virality. It was not a marketing campaign. It was not a white paper or a product launch.

It was a vibe.

There is something worth examining in that. The ideas that define technological moments often don't arrive as formal announcements. They arrive as casual observations that happen to land at the right moment — when enough people are already experiencing the thing and just needed someone to name it.

Karpathy named it. The rest followed.

That is how language actually works. And it is why I think paying attention to what's being said casually on X — not just the press releases and the official announcements — is one of the most valuable things you can do to understand where the world is actually going.
