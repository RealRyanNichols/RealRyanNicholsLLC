On June 14, Trump and Iran announced a deal. The war — which started when the U.S. and Israel launched airstrikes on Iran in late February, killing Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei — was over. The Strait of Hormuz, closed since March 4, would reopen. Oil would flow again. Twenty thousand sailors stuck in the Persian Gulf could finally go home.

Six days later, Iran closed it again.

On Saturday, June 20, Iran's military command declared the Strait of Hormuz "closed," citing continued Israeli attacks on Lebanon and what they called U.S. "bad faith" in upholding the ceasefire terms. Bloomberg confirmed the announcement. CNBC called it a "shadow over nuclear talks." And CNN ran the headline that says everything you need to know: *"Iran claims the Strait of Hormuz is closed again. Few ships were leaving in the first place."*

## What's actually at stake here

Let me give you the numbers, because they matter.

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway — at its tightest point, just 21 miles wide — connecting the Persian Gulf to the open ocean. About 20% of the world's oil supply passes through it. Nearly one-third of all globally traded liquefied natural gas moves through that channel. When Iran "closes" it, whether technically or practically, it sends shockwaves through energy markets worldwide.

Right now, nearly **500 ships, including 220 oil tankers**, have been stuck inside the Persian Gulf since late February. That's not a statistic. That's 20,000 real people — crew members from dozens of countries — who have been trapped at sea for months, some reportedly charged toll fees as high as $2 million per ship just to pass.

The world's energy system has been running on backup plans, rerouted routes, and reserve stockpiles for nearly four months. That cannot continue indefinitely.

## The deal that wasn't

Here's what makes this closure feel different from the ones that came before it.

On June 17, the U.S. and Iran signed a Memorandum of Understanding to reopen the strait and end the war. Trump called it a win. Iran's president appeared to agree. Shipping companies and insurance markets started doing the math on when they could safely return.

Then Israel continued striking targets in South Lebanon.

Iran said that violated the MOU. The U.S. Central Command pushed back, saying commercial traffic had actually *increased* on Saturday and that Iran does not control the strait regardless of what it claims. "Iran does not control the Strait of Hormuz," CENTCOM said in a statement. "Traffic continues to flow, and U.S. forces are monitoring the situation to ensure this remains the case."

So now we have two competing narratives. Iran says the strait is closed. The U.S. says it isn't. And 500 ships with thousands of sailors are caught in the middle of that argument.

## What this tells us about "peace deals" in 2026

I want to be careful here. I'm not a foreign policy analyst. I'm someone who has watched powerful institutions announce one thing publicly and do another privately — and I've learned to trust the pattern more than the press release.

The pattern here is not encouraging.

A deal was announced with fanfare. Four days later, the key provision of that deal was reportedly violated. Now both sides are publicly blaming each other, a new round of negotiations is scheduled in Switzerland, and 20,000 people are still trapped on ships in the Persian Gulf.

The talks in Switzerland are supposed to advance the interim agreement. But the interim agreement itself appears to be unraveling before the ink is dry. Iran's announcement of the second closure came *on the eve of those talks* — the timing is not an accident. It's leverage.

This is how pressure campaigns work. You make an agreement, then you violate the agreement just enough to create uncertainty, then you walk into the next negotiation with more chips than you had before.

## The Fortune headline said it better than I can

Fortune ran this headline on June 20: *"Shipping companies will decide when the Strait of Hormuz is truly open — not the U.S. or Iran — and the latest deal is already sowing confusion."*

That's the real answer to all of this. Governments can sign MOUs. Militaries can issue statements. But the people who actually have to decide whether to sail a tanker through a contested strait are insurance underwriters, shipping company executives, and the crews themselves.

Until those people are confident it's safe, the closure is functionally real — regardless of what any government says.

Right now, they are not confident.
