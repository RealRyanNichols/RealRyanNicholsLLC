Five and a half years after January 6, 2021, the last federal criminal case tied to that day is closed.

Earlier this month, U.S. District Judge Amit Mehta dismissed the seditious conspiracy case against Oath Keepers founder Stewart Rhodes and seven co-defendants — the request made by the Trump Justice Department, the dismissal granted, in Judge Mehta's own words, *reluctantly*. Weeks earlier, U.S. District Judge Timothy Kelly, a Trump appointee, dismissed the last remaining Proud Boys case the same way — closing it while explicitly noting that January 6 "was an attack on the Constitution's mechanism to facilitate the peaceful transfer of power from one president to the next."

Read that again. Two judges — one appointed by a Democrat, one by Trump himself — dismissed these cases because the government asked them to, and both used the moment to go on record that dismissal is not the same thing as agreement.

X noticed. Both sides of this argument lit up within hours of each ruling, and neither side is wrong about what happened. They're just answering different questions.

## What Actually Closed

Let's be precise, because precision is the first casualty in every J6 conversation.

What ended this month is the *criminal* case against the last Oath Keepers and Proud Boys defendants still facing charges — the seditious conspiracy prosecutions that were the centerpiece of DOJ's theory that January 6 was a coordinated plot, not a spontaneous riot. Rhodes had already been commuted, not exonerated, by the mass pardons and commutations issued at the start of the current administration. This dismissal goes further: it wipes out what remained of the case itself, not just the sentence.

With these dismissals, DOJ's years-long prosecution effort against January 6 defendants has run its course. Every case that was going to be tried, appealed, commuted, or pardoned has now been tried, appealed, commuted, or pardoned. There is no more docket left to close.

## What Did Not Close

Here's where I want to be exactly as careful as I ask everyone else to be with my own case.

A dismissal at the government's request is not a finding of innocence. It is not a court ruling that the underlying conduct didn't happen. Judge Kelly said so directly — the attack on the transfer of power is still, in his description, what it was. What changed is that the executive branch that controls prosecutorial discretion decided not to pursue these cases to conclusion, and under our system, that decision belongs to them.

Both things are true at once, and I'm not going to pretend they're not:

- The prosecutions are over. That is a fact.
- The dismissals do not retroactively establish that the underlying January 6 conduct in question was lawful, or that every conviction along the way was wrong. That is also a fact.

If you were hoping this closes the book on the *meaning* of January 6, it doesn't. If you were hoping it proves a conspiracy theory about "crisis actors" or federal instigation, it doesn't do that either — that's a separate, disputed claim, and I've been careful on this site to label it exactly that: a claim, not an established fact. What this closes is the criminal exposure of the specific people who were still fighting cases. Nothing more, nothing less.

## Why This Matters to Me Specifically

I don't get to write about this like a neutral observer, and I'm not going to pretend I am one. I lived through the prosecution side of this record — the charges, the plea calculus, the sentence, the years it takes out of a life. I know defendants whose cases ran the exact arc these did: charged, tried or pled, sentenced, and only years later did the outcome change because the executive branch changed its posture, not because a jury or a judge decided the facts were different.

That should make everyone uncomfortable, on every side of this. If prosecutorial discretion can end a case this size, it can also start one. The instrument that let these defendants walk is the same instrument that could just as easily be pointed somewhere else next time, by a different administration, for different reasons. I'd rather build a public record that survives whoever's in the DOJ building than a public record that only holds up under one administration's discretion.

## Where This Leaves the Rest of Us

If you've been following my [searchable J6 defendant archive](/case), you know most of the 1,500-plus people charged after January 6 were never Oath Keepers or Proud Boys leadership — they were people who walked in, walked around, and walked out, facing misdemeanor trespassing charges that got resolved years ago. This month's news is really about the last handful of the most serious cases, the ones the government considered the core of its conspiracy theory. Their closure is the final data point in a five-year story, not a new one.

The record is what it is. My job on this site has always been to show you the record — not to tell you what to feel about it.
