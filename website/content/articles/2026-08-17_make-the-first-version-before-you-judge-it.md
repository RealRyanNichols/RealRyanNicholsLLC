*By the Real Ryan Nichols Editorial Team*

You can spend an entire day improving something that does not exist.

You can pick the perfect name for the project, compare twelve tools, study how other people did it, rewrite the plan, organize the folder, and wait for the right feeling.

At the end of the day, there is still nothing another human being can read, test, use, reject, or help you fix.

That is not preparation anymore.

That is hiding in respectable clothes.

The first version does not have to prove you are brilliant. It has to give the work a body.

Make it before you judge it.

## A thought cannot receive useful feedback

An idea in your head can feel complete because your mind quietly fills every gap.

The missing sentence sounds obvious. The button works. The customer understands. The schedule holds. The parts fit. Nobody asks the question you forgot to answer.

Then you build the first rough version and reality starts talking.

The opening is too long. The instructions skip a step. The price is unclear. The board is too short. The form asks for information nobody wants to give. The task that looked simple requires three handoffs you never planned.

That is not failure.

That is the first honest information the project has given you.

You cannot get that information by thinking harder about a perfect imaginary version.

## Rough does not mean careless

There is a difference between a first version and a reckless release.

A rough draft can be incomplete and still tell the truth. A prototype can be ugly and still be safe. A private test can expose a weak assumption without exposing a customer to it.

The rule is simple.

Lower the polish, not the standard of honesty.

Do not fabricate a fact because it is only a draft. Do not skip a safety check because it is only a test. Do not promise a result you cannot deliver because you plan to fix the wording later.

Build the smallest version that can teach you something real.

That is the same discipline behind [Remove Friction Before You Add More Pressure](https://realryannichols.com/posts/remove-friction-before-you-add-more-pressure). The goal is not to make important work effortless. The goal is to remove the unnecessary resistance that keeps the necessary work imaginary.

## The 25 minute first-version protocol

Choose one thing you have planned more than once but have not made.

It can be an email, a proposal, a landing page, a budget, a workout plan, a repair list, a difficult conversation outline, or the first page of a larger project.

Set a timer for twenty-five minutes.

### Minutes 1 through 3: Define the evidence

Finish this sentence:

“When the timer ends, I will have something I can ______.”

Good answers include read, send to one trusted person, click through, measure, price, compare, or test.

“Work on the project” is not evidence.

Name the thing that will exist.

### Minutes 4 through 18: Make the ugly useful version

Write the complete bad draft.

Build the plain page.

Put the materials in the order you expect to use them.

List the numbers you know and mark the ones you still need.

Do not stop every two minutes to polish the first paragraph. Keep moving until the whole shape is visible.

If you hit a missing fact, write a visible placeholder that names what must be verified. Do not invent the answer. Do not let one missing piece erase the rest of the work.

### Minutes 19 through 22: Run the truth test

Ask four questions:

1. What is unclear?
2. What is unsupported?
3. What would make this unsafe or unusable?
4. What is the smallest correction that makes the next test worthwhile?

Do not ask whether it is impressive yet.

Ask whether it is honest enough to improve.

### Minutes 23 through 25: Name the next proof

Choose one next action.

Send it to one person. Test the link. Price the materials. Verify the source. Read it aloud. Put it beside the space it must fit.

One next proof is better than a new list of twenty improvements.

## Stop using quality as a reason to avoid reality

People who care about quality are especially vulnerable to this trap.

You know what good work looks like. You can see the distance between your first attempt and the finished version before you even begin. That distance feels like evidence that you are not ready.

It is only evidence that you can see.

Good judgment is useful after there is something to judge. Before that, it often turns into a guard standing at an empty gate.

[Keep the Small Promise After the Feeling Leaves](https://realryannichols.com/posts/keep-the-small-promise-after-the-feeling-leaves) explains why a small kept commitment matters after the emotion fades. Today the promise is not “make it perfect.” The promise is “make it real enough to learn from.”

## Improvement needs a receipt

At the end of the session, save the first version.

Do not erase it because it embarrasses you.

Date it. Keep it. Let the next version sit beside it.

That record protects you from two lies.

The first lie says you never improve.

The second says the finished work arrived whole and should have been obvious from the beginning.

Neither is true.

Progress usually looks like a series of visible corrections made by somebody willing to look at imperfect evidence.

That is why [The Plan Can Break Without You Being Broken](https://realryannichols.com/posts/the-plan-can-break-without-you-being-broken) separates a failed plan from a verdict on the person who made it. A first version is allowed to expose a bad assumption. You are allowed to replace it.

## Make the thing

You do not need permission to create a private first version.

You do not need a launch announcement. You do not need the final logo. You do not need to know how version ten will work.

You need twenty-five honest minutes and a finish line small enough to cross.

Make the first version.

Then judge what is actually there.
