This week the President of the United States said two of the country's biggest broadcast networks should lose their federal licenses.

Not for breaking a law. Not for airing something false. For choosing *not* to air something.

On July 16, ABC and NBC declined to preempt their regular primetime lineup to carry President Trump's address on election security live on broadcast television. Both networks carried it on their streaming arms instead. CBS and Fox aired it live, in full, on broadcast.

The President did not let that editorial decision go.

"In a rare move, NBC and ABC, fake news, have both said that they would not cover this speech," he said, during the address itself. Then he went further: "Fraud like this should mean a revocation of their licenses."

[Watch on X →](https://x.com/washingtonpost/status/2078121318784860555)

Read that again. A sitting president, on camera, threatened two networks with the loss of their operating licenses because of how they chose to cover him.

## I don't care who's in the White House when this happens

I want to say this plainly, because I know exactly which direction people's minds go the moment a headline has "Trump" in it: this is not about Trump specifically. I would be writing the exact same article if a Democratic president said CBS and Fox should lose their licenses for not airing *their* speech live.

I've built this entire site around one idea — that when the people holding power start deciding which speech gets amplified and which gets punished, it doesn't matter what letter is next to their name. The mechanism is the danger, not the messenger.

I've documented what it looks like when a platform decides my words cross a line that identical words from someone else don't. I've shown the DOJ settlement admitting federal agencies pressured social media companies to suppress content during the last administration. That story barely made a ripple before the news cycle moved past it.

This is the same instinct, coming from a different office, aimed at a different medium. A broadcaster made an editorial call. The President responded by threatening its federal license.

## The pushback came from people who don't agree on much else

What's notable here isn't just the threat — it's who pushed back on it.

Senator Bernie Sanders called it "true authoritarian fashion" and said flatly: "Insane." FCC Commissioner Anna Gomez — a member of the very agency that issues broadcast licenses — said it was "ridiculous to call for broadcasters to lose their license simply for making the same editorial decisions they've made under presidents of both parties," adding that "those editorial decisions are protected by the First Amendment."

A progressive senator and a sitting FCC commissioner do not agree on much. They agreed on this. That should tell you something about how far outside the normal guardrails this threat actually sits.

## Editorial decisions are not the same as fraud

Whether or not you liked the substance of the speech — and there's plenty of dispute over the accuracy of its claims about the 2026 election — the network coverage decision is a separate question entirely. ABC and NBC have made calls like this under presidents of both parties for decades. Choosing not to preempt primetime programming for a political address is not fraud. It's not obstruction. It's an editorial judgment call, the exact kind the First Amendment exists to protect from government retaliation.

The moment a President can credibly threaten a network's license over a coverage decision, every network — the ones you agree with and the ones you don't — starts making its next editorial call with that threat sitting in the back of its mind. That's not hypothetical. That's the entire point of making the threat out loud, on camera, in the speech itself.

## What I actually want out of this

I'm not asking you to defend ABC or NBC's programming choices, or to like their coverage, or to trust their motives. I'm asking you to separate two questions that keep getting collapsed into one: *do I like this network's decision* and *should the government be able to punish a network for making it.*

Those are not the same question. The first one is yours to have an opinion on. The second one should have the same answer no matter who's asking — no.

I've spent years arguing that speech enforcement shouldn't bend to who holds power at the moment. That argument doesn't get to take a break just because this time the threat lines up with what one side of my audience wants to hear.
