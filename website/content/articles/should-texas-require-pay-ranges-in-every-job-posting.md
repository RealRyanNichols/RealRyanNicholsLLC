If a company knows what it plans to pay, should the applicant have to spend an hour applying before learning the number?

That is the practical question behind pay-transparency laws.

Texas does not currently require private employers to put a salary or hourly range in every job advertisement. The Texas Workforce Commission’s August 2026 employer newsletter says compensation disclosure is increasingly expected but [remains optional in Texas](https://www.twc.texas.gov/sites/default/files/commissioners/tbt/tbt-august-2026-twc.pdf). Separate TWC guidance says [no specific law obligates private employers to post jobs in a particular way](https://efte.twc.texas.gov/job_postings_and_recruitment.html).

The state already uses ranges in its own classified compensation system. The [Texas State Auditor’s Office publishes minimum and maximum salary schedules](https://hr.sao.texas.gov/compensationsystem/salaryschedules/) tied to state job classifications.

So the idea is not foreign to Texas. The dispute is whether private employers should be required to do the same.

## The strongest case for requiring the range

The applicant is already being asked for information.

A résumé may reveal years of experience, education, certifications, past employers, and location. An application may require another round of answers before anybody talks about compensation. The employer receives detail. The applicant often receives “competitive pay.”

That imbalance wastes time.

A posted range lets a worker decide whether the job can support a household before completing the application. It helps a currently employed person decide whether an interview is worth taking. It gives a small business a chance to attract people who are actually aligned with the budget.

Transparency can also make unfair differences easier to notice. Federal law already prohibits certain forms of pay discrimination. The [Equal Employment Opportunity Commission explains](https://www.eeoc.gov/equal-paycompensation-discrimination) that the Equal Pay Act covers wages and other forms of compensation for substantially equal work, and other federal laws prohibit pay discrimination based on protected characteristics.

A range does not prove fairness. It makes the starting terms easier to inspect.

## The strongest case against a mandate

Not every job is priced like a product on a shelf.

A small employer may be willing to hire a developing candidate near the bottom of a range or redesign the role for an unusually experienced candidate near the top. A posting that says $45,000 to $85,000 may technically disclose a range while telling the applicant almost nothing.

Employers also worry about compression. A current employee may see a new-hire range and ask why years of service did not produce more distance. That conversation may be necessary, but it can create a real cost that a small shop cannot fix overnight.

There is also an enforcement question. Would the law cover ten-person businesses? Remote jobs that can be performed in Texas? Commission work? Tips? Bonuses? Benefits? Jobs posted through a staffing company?

A vague law can create paperwork without creating clarity.

## What a useful Texas rule would need

If Texas adopts a requirement, “post the pay” is not enough.

A workable rule should define a good-faith minimum and maximum that the employer reasonably expects to pay at the time of posting. It should say whether the number is hourly, annual, per project, commission-based, or another method. It should require a short note about bonuses, tips, or incentives when they make up a meaningful part of expected compensation.

It should also avoid pretending every employer has a human-resources department.

Texas could phase the rule in by employer size, publish plain-language examples, allow a short correction period for first mistakes, and penalize repeated fake ranges more seriously than an honest formatting error.

The goal should be usable information, not a new trap for a local owner who posted the right number in the wrong box.

## Ryan’s Take

Put the range on the listing.

If the employer does not know the exact final number, post the honest range and explain what moves a candidate within it. That is what a range is for.

The applicant’s time has value before the applicant becomes an employee. Asking somebody to complete a long application, arrange an interview, and tell you everything about their experience while you hide the budget is not good recruiting. It is information hoarding.

But the mandate has to be written for real businesses, not only large companies with compliance teams. Give small employers a clean template. Define commission and tip-heavy work. Punish fake ranges. Let honest mistakes be corrected.

Texas already publishes salary ranges for much of its own classified workforce. Private employers can explain why one role is flexible without requiring every applicant to guess whether “competitive” means rent money or pocket change.
