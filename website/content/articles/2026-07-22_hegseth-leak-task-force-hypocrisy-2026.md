On July 13, [Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth posted from the official @SecWar account](https://x.com/SecWar/status/2076673841913344136) that the Department of War and the Department of Justice had "created a joint task force to IDENTIFY AND PROSECUTE LEAKERS."

His exact words: "Access to confidential and secret information is a sacred trust, and those who betray that trust will be met with the full force of the law."

Read that sentence twice. Then remember who's saying it.

## The receipts nobody forgot

X did not let the announcement stand on its own for long. Within the hour, replies were stacked with the same reminder: this is the same defense secretary at the center of the 2025 "Signalgate" episode, when a group chat on the commercial app Signal — used to coordinate sensitive military planning around strikes in Yemen — ended up including The Atlantic's editor-in-chief, Jeffrey Goldberg, because someone added him to the thread.

Hegseth [dismissed the reporting as a "hoax"](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/pete-hegseths-vow-to-find-leakers-backfires-spectacularly_n_6a566010e4b0540b8c7b1305) at the time — a characterization Goldberg told CNN was simply false.

That history is exactly why "full force of the law" landed the way it did this week. It's not that people think leaks of sensitive military information don't matter. It's that a "sacred trust" standard only works if the person announcing it has held himself to it.

## Why this isn't just a gotcha

I've spent a lot of time watching how "accountability" gets used as a word. Sometimes it means a real, consistent standard applied evenly. More often, in Washington, it means a standard that points outward — at staffers, at leakers, at critics — while the person holding the microphone gets the benefit of the doubt every time.

That's not accountability. That's a chain of command deciding who the rules are for.

A task force built to "identify and prosecute" people who mishandle sensitive information is a legitimate government function when it's built on a standard that applies from the top down. It becomes theater the moment the public can point to the exact same failure sitting one office over, unaddressed.

## What accountability actually requires

If the Pentagon wants the public to take a leak task force seriously, the fastest way to earn that is simple: apply the same review to every prior leak, including the ones involving the officials now setting the policy. Publish what that review finds. Let the same "full force of the law" language mean something whether the name attached to the leak is a junior staffer or a cabinet secretary.

Anything short of that isn't a policy. It's a threat aimed in one direction only.
