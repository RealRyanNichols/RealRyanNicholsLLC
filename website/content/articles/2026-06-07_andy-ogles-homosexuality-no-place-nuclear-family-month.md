On June 1, 2026 — the first day of Pride Month — Tennessee Representative Andy Ogles posted the following on X:

> *"Homosexuality has no place in America. Happy Nuclear Family Month."*

The post was real. It was verified. It came from a sitting member of the United States Congress.

And within hours of the backlash hitting — including pushback from fellow Republicans — Ogles deleted it.

Then came the explanation: *"A member of my comms team sent this post."* He called the message "stupid" and "hurtful." He said the employee had been reprimanded.

I have a lot to say about this.

{{share}}

## Let's Start With What He Said

"Homosexuality has no place in America."

I want you to read that sentence again slowly.

Not "I disagree with the direction of Pride Month." Not "I believe in the traditional family structure." Not even a pointed policy statement about gender or marriage.

A sitting U.S. congressman told a category of American citizens that they have *no place* in their own country.

That is not a policy position. That is a declaration of erasure.

And it landed on the first day of Pride Month, paired with a celebratory "Happy Nuclear Family Month" — a designation that Tennessee Governor Bill Lee formalized this year by signing House Joint Resolution 182, which defines a nuclear family as one husband, one wife, and their children.

I understand people have different views on family structure. I respect that there is a real and legitimate debate about policy questions tied to these issues.

But "you have no place here" is not a policy debate. It is a statement about whether someone belongs in America at all.

## The "Staffer Did It" Defense

Let me be direct about this part: I do not believe it.

Not because I think Ogles is uniquely dishonest. Politicians blame staffers for controversial posts because it is a well-established escape hatch. The statement gets made. The message lands. The audience it was intended for receives it loud and clear. Then when the backlash becomes politically inconvenient — particularly when your own party starts pushing back — you delete the post and point at someone lower on the org chart.

This is not accountability. This is a technique.

The most revealing moment was not the post. It was who pushed back.

Former Representative George Santos — who is openly gay — responded to Ogles' post by saying the comment felt like a personal rejection. He said he was "saddened" to hear someone he considered a friend suggest there was "no place" for him simply because of who he loves.

That hit differently. Because Santos was not speaking abstractly. He was talking about what it feels like to be told — by a colleague, on a national platform — that your existence is incompatible with the country you call home.

## The Nuclear Family Month Framework

I want to engage with the actual idea here, because I think the framing matters.

Declaring June "Nuclear Family Month" is, on its surface, a statement of values. I understand that. Plenty of Americans believe deeply in the traditional family structure. That belief deserves respect in a pluralistic society.

But the execution chose a very specific opponent. Designating June — which has been Pride Month for decades — as "Nuclear Family Month" is not a neutral celebration of one family structure. It is a counter-programming move. It is designed to send a signal to a specific audience that their identity is in opposition to the American family ideal.

You do not have to celebrate Pride Month. No one is required to hang a rainbow flag. But the act of rebranding an entire month specifically to counterprogram against an existing observance tells you exactly what the move is about. It is not about celebrating nuclear families. It is about telling LGBTQ+ Americans that their Pride Month is being contested — that their visibility is something to be pushed back against, not accommodated.

That is different from simply honoring families. And the Ogles tweet made the subtext into text.

## What I Have Seen in My Own Life

I have been on X long enough to know what coordinated campaigns to erase certain people's voices look like. I have experienced it. I have documented it.

What I have never done — what I *will* never do — is tell someone that they have no place in America because of who they are.

Not because I am a saint. Because I know what it costs a person to be told that their existence is incompatible with the place they grew up, paid taxes in, loved, and fought for.

I have LGBTQ+ people in my life — friends, family members — who are as American as anyone who has ever posted a flag emoji on the Fourth of July. The idea that any elected official gets to declare them placeless is something I am not willing to let sit quietly.

## The Bigger Stakes

Here is why I think this moment matters beyond the specific tweet.

When an elected official says something like this — even when they later delete it and blame a staffer — it sends a message to every LGBTQ+ American about where they stand in the eyes of their government.

A teenager in Tennessee who is gay saw that post before it was deleted. A same-sex couple watching the news cycle saw it. They did not need it to stay up. They already got the message.

And the "reprimanded staffer" explanation does not erase what was said. It does not address the reality that a congressional office — at minimum — employed someone who thought that message was appropriate to send on behalf of a sitting member of Congress.

That alone is worth a serious conversation.
