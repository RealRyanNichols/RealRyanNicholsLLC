*By Ryan Nichols*

Somewhere, a marketing department spent real money on a KFC campaign this year. I'd bet none of it moved the needle like [one guy filming himself eating popcorn chicken](https://x.com/JoeisHungryBite/status/2079344215704674683) did.

The reviewer goes by Joe Is Hungry — real name Joe Desaavedra, a Georgia-based food creator known for cinematic, almost overproduced reviews of ordinary fast food. He's posted more than 100 of these. His new review of KFC's limited-time Popcorn Chicken, a 620-calorie item, passed 17 million views on X. Some counts put the total even higher once you add other platforms.

Here's the detail that made me stop scrolling: [Desaavedra told TMZ](https://www.tmz.com/2026/07/24/joe-is-hungry-surprised-his-kfc-video-went-viral/) he thought the video was "whatever" and almost didn't post it.

## The guy who wasn't trying got the biggest swing

I've spent enough time around marketing to know how much a campaign like a fast-food product launch usually costs — the agency, the shoot, the media buy, the approvals chain. KFC didn't produce this video, didn't pay for it, and by Desaavedra's own account, its own creator didn't expect it to work.

It worked anyway, and not by accident. He's posted over 100 of these reviews. The "overnight" viral moment was actually the hundred-and-first rep of a craft he'd already been building in public, one ordinary fast-food order at a time. Nobody sees the first ninety-nine. They only see the one that broke through — and assume it happened by luck.

That's not luck. That's what showing up looks like right before it pays off.

## Why this matters past one KFC video

I talk to small-business owners constantly through this site — East Texas shops, tradespeople, people building something with their own name on it. Almost every one of them believes they need to sound polished, corporate, and safe to be taken seriously. Professional lighting. Scripted copy. A brand voice that sounds like everybody else's brand voice.

Meanwhile, a guy eating popcorn chicken in a car, being honest about whether it's actually good, outperformed a national ad budget.

The lesson isn't "don't try." Desaavedra clearly puts real craft into these — the cinematic style, the pacing, the hundred reps before this one. The lesson is that people can tell the difference between something built to sell them and something built because someone actually cares about doing the thing well. One of those is instantly recognizable, and it's getting rarer, so when it shows up, it travels.

## What I'd tell any small business reading this

Stop trying to sound like a bigger company than you are. Bigger companies are, right now, trying to figure out how to sound like you — a real person, doing real work, telling the truth about it. You already have the thing they're chasing. Use it.

Post the hundred reps nobody watches. The hundred-and-first might be the one that changes everything, and you won't know which one it is until it happens.
