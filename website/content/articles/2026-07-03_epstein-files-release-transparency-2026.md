This week, a federal judge did something Washington almost never does voluntarily: he gave the government a hard deadline and a hard choice.

U.S. District Judge Emmet Sullivan ordered the Justice Department to strip the redactions from a specific set of Epstein-related records — including email exchanges referencing a "torture video" and interview records tied to decades-old abuse allegations — or show up in court and explain, on the record, why it legally cannot. The order came in a lawsuit brought by legal analyst Katie Phang, arguing the DOJ was already violating the bipartisan Epstein Files Transparency Act that required document production back in December 2025.

https://x.com/RoKhanna/status/2070309658447810937

Sullivan didn't have to guess at the DOJ's position. According to his own order, Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche's team **conceded** the department was already out of compliance with the law. Not accused. Conceded.

## I have said this same sentence about a different set of files for years

Swap "Epstein files" for "January 6 Capitol surveillance footage" and you are reading an argument I have made on this site more than once: the government holds the largest archive of evidence in a matter of intense public interest, and for years it has decided — case by case, redaction by redaction — what the public is allowed to see.

The Epstein files and the J6 footage are not the same case. I'm not conflating them. But the test I apply is identical, because it's the only test that's actually honest:

**If full release vindicates the official story, release ends every rumor overnight.** If the documents show exactly what officials have said they show, unsealing them costs the government nothing and buys back years of "what are they hiding" suspicion in a single afternoon.

**If withholding continues anyway, that's not an accident.** An agency that has already been told it's *violating a transparency law Congress passed with bipartisan votes*, and is still fighting to keep specific names and emails blacked out, is not protecting an ongoing investigation. It's protecting somebody's discomfort.

## Watch what happens next, not what gets said now

The DOJ says it will appeal. That's their right. But notice the shape of the fight: nobody in this dispute is arguing the underlying law is unclear. Blanche's own team told the judge they're behind. The argument now isn't "we didn't violate the Act" — it's "let us keep some of it dark anyway."

That's the tell. When the fight moves from "we didn't do this" to "let us keep doing part of this," the file cabinet has already told you what's in it.

## What I'm asking you to do with this

I'm not asking you to take a side on Epstein's associates before the documents are public — I don't know what's in the redacted lines any more than you do, and neither of us should pretend otherwise. I'm asking you to hold the same line I hold on January 6: **the public paid for these records, the public is named or implicated in these records, and the public does not have to accept "trust us" as an answer from an agency that already admitted it broke the law Congress wrote for exactly this situation.**

Full release. Not the parts that flatter whoever's currently in the building. All of it.
