A House Oversight subcommittee hearing titled "Rewriting American History: Examining the Smithsonian's Efforts to Reshape the Past" was not supposed to be the most-watched clip on X this week. Then Rep. Brandon Gill (R-TX) started asking questions.

[Gill posted the exchange himself](https://x.com/realBrandonGill/status/2079598019331186739): "The Smithsonian's 'MASS Action Toolkit' said that politeness is a 'defining aspect of white supremacy culture.' I asked the Director of the National Museum of American History if that means she's a white supremacist."

## What was actually said

Gill asked Dr. Anthea Hartig, director of the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History, a plain question: "Is it important to be polite?"

"Yes, sir," she answered.

Gill then read from the museum's own "Mass Action Toolkit" — a document the museum has promoted since 2020 — which lists politeness, objectivity, and the belief that "hard work is the key to success" as "aspects of whiteness & white culture in the United States." Hartig said she wasn't familiar with the exact passage and told the committee the museum "does not take sides in America's political debates."

That answer is the part that didn't survive contact with the internet.

## Why a clip like this travels

This isn't really about one document or one hearing. It's about a simple test the public keeps applying to institutions that ask for trust and funding: do your stated values survive being read out loud, in your own words, in front of a camera?

The Smithsonian is not a private museum funding its own opinions. It's a federally chartered institution funded substantially by taxpayer money, and it exists to preserve and present American history — not to rank ordinary virtues like courtesy and work ethic as markers of a "culture" to be dismantled. When a document circulated under its own name does exactly that, the burden isn't on the public to explain why it's uncomfortable. The burden is on the institution to explain why it published it.

Hartig's answer — "I'm not familiar with that passage" — is a defensible thing to say about one line in a long document. It is not, on its own, an answer to the substance of the question: does the museum stand behind material that tells millions of Americans that being polite, valuing hard work, or believing in family structure are traits of a "dominant culture" to be corrected?

## The bigger point

I don't think most people watching that clip were arguing about museum curation. They were reacting to something more basic: an institution built on the public's trust telling that same public, in writing, that ordinary decency is a problem to be solved.

Institutions get to have hard, honest conversations about history. What they don't get is a pass on defending their own paperwork when a member of Congress reads it back to them.
