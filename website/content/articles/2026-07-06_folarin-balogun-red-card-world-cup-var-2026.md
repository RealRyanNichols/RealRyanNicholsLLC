Folarin Balogun stepped on the back of Tarik Muharemović's leg in the 61st minute of the USA's win over Bosnia-Herzegovina. Referee Raphael Claus reviewed it on VAR and sent Balogun off. Under FIFA's own rules, that's a one-match ban — Balogun sits out the Round of 16 against Belgium.

Except he didn't sit out. FIFA's Disciplinary Committee suspended the ban for a "probationary period of one year" under Article 27 of its Disciplinary Code, and Balogun started against Belgium anyway. Reporting says the move came after direct outside pressure on FIFA to review the call. It's being described as the first time since 1962 that a player has been allowed to play a World Cup match immediately after picking up a red card in the previous one.

Belgium's federation said it was "astonished." I think that reaction is worth sitting with, even as a USA fan who wanted Balogun on the field.

## What Balogun actually said

To his credit, Balogun didn't pretend the contact never happened. In his first comments after the incident, he said: *"It's important for me to say, first and foremost, it was totally unintentional."* He went further, saying he thought *"a yellow card would've been fair, due to it not being intentional."*

That's a reasonable, honest read of the situation. Plenty of neutral analysts agreed the review appeared to misapply VAR protocol, which is supposed to weigh point-of-contact severity, not just how bad a slow-motion replay looks frozen on a screen. If the process was flawed, that's a legitimate grievance.

## Where it stops being about the process

Here's my problem. If the review was wrong, the remedy is an appeals process that applies the same way to every team, every time, regardless of who's asking. What actually happened was a single team getting a suspension lifted through what reporting describes as outside political pressure, in a manner FIFA has not applied to any other red card in over sixty years of World Cup soccer.

I've spent a long time writing about institutions bending the rules for whoever has the most leverage in the room, and I don't think the analysis changes just because this time the leverage worked in my team's favor. A process that only produces the "correct" outcome when enough pressure gets applied isn't a fair process — it's a favor. And favors for one side today are precedent for someone else's favor tomorrow, including a favor that goes against USA next time.

Belgium played the match believing the rules said Balogun would be out. Then the rules moved. If I were a Belgian fan, I would be furious too, and I don't think that fury is misplaced just because I'm rooting for the other team.

## Why I'm writing about it anyway

It would be easy to just enjoy the win and not think too hard about how Balogun ended up on the field. A lot of USA fans are doing exactly that this week, and I understand the impulse.

But the whole reason I've spent so much time writing about fairness in institutions — courts, agencies, referees, whoever holds the whistle — is that consistency has to survive contact with a result you like. If I only demand a fair, evenly applied process when the ruling goes against me, I'm not actually asking for fairness. I'm asking for my side to win. Those are not the same thing, and conflating them is how every institution slowly loses the public's trust.

I wanted Balogun on the field. I also think Belgium is right to be angry about how he got there. Both of those things are true at the same time.
