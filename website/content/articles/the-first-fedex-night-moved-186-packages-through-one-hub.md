On the first official night, the system looked too large for the result.

Federal Express had 389 team members and 14 aircraft. They moved 186 packages to 25 American cities.

That is fewer than 14 packages per airplane.

If you judged the company by that night's efficiency alone, the conclusion could have been brutal. Too many people. Too many planes. Too much infrastructure. Not enough packages.

But the small number was moving through a system built for a much larger future.

## The first night was April 17, 1973

FedEx's [official company history](https://www.fedex.com/en-us/about/history.html) says Federal Express began operations in Memphis, Tennessee, on April 17, 1973. Fourteen aircraft delivered 186 packages to 25 cities that night.

The company says Memphis was chosen because it was centrally located, its airport was rarely closed by bad weather, officials were willing to make improvements, and hangar space was available.

The [Smithsonian's account of Memphis](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/travel/the-soul-of-memphis-14139079/) confirms the first-night figures and notes that one of the original Dassault Falcon aircraft entered the National Air and Space Museum collection.

The striking fact is not merely that airplanes carried packages overnight. Air cargo already existed.

The operating idea was that packages from different cities could flow into one central point, be sorted together, and then flow back out toward their destinations. Instead of trying to build every possible city-to-city connection, the network created a common handoff.

The hub was not a stop added to the system. The hub was the system.

## One hub reduced the number of separate problems

Imagine trying to connect 25 cities directly to every other city. Each route becomes its own schedule, its own capacity problem, and its own chance for failure.

A central hub changes the shape of the problem. Each city needs a reliable path into the hub and a reliable path back out. Sorting rules inside the hub decide what goes where next.

That structure creates a business lesson far beyond aviation.

Most growing companies do not fail because nobody is working. They fail because the work travels through too many disconnected routes.

A lead arrives through Facebook. Another comes through a website form. A customer texts one employee. A voicemail sits on another phone. A payment lives in one system. The work order lives in somebody's memory. The owner becomes the only person who knows how to connect the pieces.

The business has activity, but it does not have a hub.

## A small first result can still test the right system

The 186-package figure is useful because it protects the story from easy mythology.

The first night was not a massive victory by volume. It was proof that the coordinated motion could happen. Aircraft could arrive. Packages could be sorted. Destinations could be assigned. The network could complete the loop.

A good early test does not have to prove the final scale. It has to expose whether the important handoffs work.

Can the lead enter once and reach the right person? Can the customer see what happens next? Can the team tell whether the follow-up occurred? Can a payment trigger delivery? Can the owner see a failure before a customer has to report it?

## The infrastructure was built before the volume arrived

Building for scale too early can waste money. Building nothing until demand becomes overwhelming can waste the opportunity.

The answer is not to imitate an airline. It is to identify the smallest system that can survive the next level of demand.

For a local service business, that might be one form that creates one customer record, sends one acknowledgment, assigns one owner, schedules one follow-up, and records one outcome.

For a school, it might be one path from inquiry to tour, enrollment, payment plan, course access, and graduate follow-up.

For a professional service firm, it might be one intake that separates urgent matters, qualified prospects, existing clients, and people who need a different resource.

The visible result may still be small. The important question is whether each additional customer can travel through the same dependable route without requiring the owner to reinvent the trip.

[Remove Friction Before You Add More Pressure](/posts/remove-friction-before-you-add-more-pressure) offers a practical way to find the places where work collapses between steps.

## A network is only as real as its handoffs

FedEx did not create value by owning isolated airplanes. The value came from timing them together, bringing the work to a common point, sorting it correctly, and completing delivery.

Your website, CRM, phone, calendar, payment processor, email system, and advertising accounts are also isolated equipment until they share a route.

Buying another tool may add capability. It does not automatically add coordination.

The work is in the handoffs.
