Texas voters are casting 2026 congressional ballots under district lines drawn after the decade had already begun.

That is legal unless a court says the particular map violates another rule. It is also a policy choice worth arguing about before every party starts treating mid-decade redistricting as normal.

Should Texas be able to redraw congressional maps between federal censuses when political control makes it possible, or should ordinary redistricting wait for new population data every ten years?

## What is in effect now

The [Texas Legislative Council’s redistricting site](https://redistricting.capitol.texas.gov/) says PlanC2333, enacted during the Legislature’s second called session in 2025, is the congressional plan in effect for the 2026 election.

The state’s [official timeline](https://redistricting.capitol.texas.gov/2020s_timeline) records that a three-judge federal court blocked the plan on November 18, 2025. The U.S. Supreme Court then stayed that order on December 4, 2025 while the appeal continues. The state timeline says PlanC2333 is again in effect pending the Supreme Court’s action.

The state’s plan dataset was updated September 11, 2026. Jefferson County has also told voters that some people are voting in new districts even though their current representatives continue providing constituent services under the prior lines through the end of the year.

Those are the operational facts as verified September 30, 2026.

The unresolved legal case includes allegations of racial discrimination and the state’s defense that the drawing was political rather than racially unlawful. The Supreme Court’s stay did not finally decide every claim on the merits.

## The strongest case for allowing a mid-decade redraw

State legislatures have constitutional authority over the times, places, and manner of congressional elections, subject to federal law and judicial review.

Supporters of mid-decade redistricting can make several serious arguments.

First, a map may need correction after a major court decision changes the legal framework. Waiting years can preserve lines drawn under assumptions that no longer apply.

Second, elections have consequences. If voters choose a legislature with a different policy agenda, redistricting authority is one power that legislature may lawfully use.

Third, a blanket once-per-decade rule can freeze a flawed map even when the population moves dramatically, communities split, or litigation exposes a defect.

Finally, political line drawing is not new. The Supreme Court has held that federal partisan-gerrymandering claims can present political questions beyond the reach of federal courts. That does not erase racial-vote-dilution or equal-protection claims, but it explains why the legal line is not simply “politics happened.”

## The strongest case for waiting until the next census

A ten-year cycle creates stability.

Candidates know their districts. Local parties build organizations. voters learn who represents them. Counties prepare precincts and voter materials. Residents develop relationships with congressional offices.

Mid-decade redistricting can turn every change in legislative control into a map war.

If Texas redraws for partisan advantage in 2025, another state can answer in 2026. A later Texas majority can answer again. The result is not neutral reform. It is a national escalation where voters choose representatives inside districts repeatedly redesigned by the representatives’ allies.

A fixed cycle also gives the process a neutral trigger: new census data. Without that trigger, the public can reasonably suspect that the real reason for the redraw is the expected partisan result.

The administrative confusion is real too. Jefferson County’s own explanation separates the district a voter uses for the 2026 ballot from the representative serving that voter until January 2027.

## Editorial analysis

Texas should be able to redraw between censuses when a final court order requires a remedy or when a clear, measurable defect makes the existing map unusable.

Pure partisan opportunity is a weaker reason.

The rule should be written for the day your opponents control the pen.

A defensible Texas standard could require one of three triggers for an ordinary mid-decade congressional redraw:

1. a final court order;
2. a documented federal-law compliance problem; or
3. approval by voters in a statewide election after the proposed map is published.

That would not end political redistricting. Nothing will. It would make the trigger more visible and harder to change with the mood of one legislative session.

The current map will shape who Texas sends to Congress. The rule we accept will shape what every future majority believes it can do.
