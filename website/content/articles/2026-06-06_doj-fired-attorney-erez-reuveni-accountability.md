Erez Reuveni was a Department of Justice attorney.

He was given a directive he believed violated his ethical obligations as a lawyer.

He refused to comply.

He was placed on administrative leave in April 2025. Then he was fired.

Months later, the Government Accountability Project and co-signing counsel representing Reuveni sent a letter to the Senate and House Judiciary Committees urging Congress to address what they called a failure of DOJ's internal oversight — the Office of Inspector General — to carry out its essential responsibilities.

And in the background, the Department of Justice has been quietly advancing a new proposed rule that would allow the DOJ to insert itself at the front end of state bar-complaint proceedings against current and former department attorneys — effectively pausing state disciplinary boards while the DOJ runs its own internal review first.

Read that carefully.

The same institution that fired a lawyer for refusing to follow orders it believed were illegal is now proposing a rule that would give itself veto power over the disciplinary process for anyone who might come forward next.

## What Actually Happened to Erez Reuveni

The case that triggered Reuveni's firing involved Kilmar Abrego Garcia.

Abrego Garcia is a Salvadoran man who was deported to El Salvador's CECOT prison — a facility the State Department has documented as having severe human rights concerns — in what courts later found was an error. Reuveni allegedly refused to comply with directives related to the case that he believed crossed a legal and ethical line.

He filed a protected whistleblower disclosure. That disclosure was submitted to the Senate Judiciary Committee in 2025.

Reuveni's case generated significant coverage in legal circles and on X — particularly among attorneys and former DOJ employees who saw it as a signal about what happens inside the department when lawyers try to hold a line that institutional pressure wants them to cross.

The pattern his case represents is not new. What is new is the scale and the audacity of the institutional response.

## The Bar Complaint Rule: How Institutions Protect Themselves

In April 2026, critics began raising alarms about a proposed DOJ rule that would restructure how misconduct allegations against current and former DOJ attorneys are handled.

Under the proposed rule, the DOJ would be able to insert itself into state bar-complaint proceedings against its attorneys — pausing state disciplinary authorities while the DOJ conducts its own internal review. The rule also includes language about "appropriate action" if a state bar declines to defer to DOJ's internal process.

The Brennan Center for Justice analyzed this rule and concluded it contradicts the McDade Amendment — a 1998 law passed by Congress specifically requiring DOJ attorneys to comply with the professional responsibility rules of the states in which they practice.

That law exists for a reason.

State bar associations are the mechanism by which the legal profession polices itself — outside the chain of command of whichever administration happens to be in power. They are supposed to be independent from the political pressures that shape how DOJ leadership makes decisions. An attorney who is punished by their employer for following the law can still face, or be protected by, the independent bar-complaint process.

If DOJ gains the ability to pause that process and run a parallel review first, it significantly narrows the window in which independent professional accountability can operate.

That is not a partisan observation. Republican and Democratic administrations have both had reason, at various points, to prefer a more insular accountability structure. This is an institutional power grab that transcends politics — and it is the kind of structural change that tends to slide through precisely because it sounds procedural and boring.

It is not boring.

## The Brennan Center Gets It Right

The Brennan Center's analysis of DOJ's broken accountability system is worth reading in full. The core argument is simple: the oversight mechanisms inside DOJ are not working as intended. The OIG — the Office of Inspector General — is structurally limited in what it can investigate. The internal disciplinary processes have gaps. And the political pressure that flows through any administration shapes how those processes operate in practice.

Reuveni's case is a data point in that larger picture. One attorney refusing one directive and being fired for it would be a workplace dispute under normal circumstances. What makes it a signal is the pattern it fits: lawyers inside the department who try to hold an ethical line that department leadership has crossed are losing their jobs and their careers.

That dynamic is not sustainable for any institution that is supposed to operate under the rule of law.

## Why This Matters to Anyone Who Has Dealt With a Broken System

I am going to be direct about why I'm writing this.

I have spent years documenting what happens when institutions — legal, governmental, bureaucratic — act in bad faith and then deploy the procedural tools available to them to prevent accountability from reaching them. I know the mechanics. I know what it looks like when a system is designed to exhaust you. I know what it looks like when the complaint process itself is controlled by the entity you're complaining about.

Reuveni's situation is a version of that same dynamic operating inside the Department of Justice itself.

When the entity you're supposed to hold accountable controls the mechanism by which accountability can be imposed — you don't have a justice system. You have a closed loop.

The proposed bar complaint rule is an attempt to close that loop a little tighter.

The people who should be loudest about this are lawyers — because their profession's independence is what's at stake. But the people who should care about it are everyone who has ever needed the DOJ or the state bar system to do its job without political interference.

That is a lot of people.

## What Needs to Happen

**Congress needs to act on the Reuveni disclosure.** The protected whistleblower disclosure was submitted to the Judiciary Committees in 2025. That disclosure should trigger hearings, document requests, and — if warranted — referrals. The Government Accountability Project has been publicly calling for exactly this. Congressional leadership needs to follow through.

**The proposed bar complaint rule needs to be stopped.** If the McDade Amendment means what the Brennan Center says it means — and the text of that law is not ambiguous — then the proposed DOJ rule directly contradicts an act of Congress. That is a matter for Congress to address, and for courts to rule on if the rule advances over objections.

**The OIG needs structural reform.** The Inspector General system at DOJ was designed to provide independent oversight. If that system is not functioning as designed — if it lacks the authority, resources, or independence to investigate the things it needs to investigate — then the structural gaps need to be documented and corrected. That is a legislative problem. Congress has the power to fix it.

**The attorneys inside DOJ who are watching this need to know someone is paying attention.** Institutional whistleblowers act when they believe the personal cost of staying silent is higher than the personal cost of coming forward. If the visible track record is that people who come forward lose their careers while the institutions they exposed face no consequences — the next person who sees something wrong will do the math and stay quiet.

That silence is how institutional misconduct compounds over decades.
