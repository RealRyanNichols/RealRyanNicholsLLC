A reporter walked up to President Trump and asked about the latest inflation number.

His response: *"No, I love it. I love the inflation."*

That clip is now one of the most-shared political moments on X this month. Tens of millions of views. Cut into highlight reels. Posted by the left as proof of incompetence. Posted by the right as evidence of media manipulation. Shared by people who have absolutely no idea what the sentence before it was, or the sentence after.

That last part is the part I want to talk about.

{{share}}

## What a viral clip does — every time

Here's what a 15-second clip on X cannot carry: tone, setup, crosstalk, body language over time, or the words that came before and after. What it can carry is a reaction. And the reaction is what gets shared.

The people posting "HE SAID HE LOVES INFLATION" are betting you won't look up the full exchange. The people posting "THE MEDIA IS DECEPTIVELY EDITING AGAIN" are making the same bet. Both sides are using the same six seconds and calling it evidence.

Was he responding sarcastically to a loaded question? Was he mid-thought when the camera cut? Was there context in the room that changed the meaning entirely? These are not hypothetical manipulations — they are standard features of how political clips travel on X in 2026.

I've had my own words cut from their paragraph and sent around the internet. I know what it looks like when a fragment becomes the story and the full statement never trends.

## Inflation is real. The clip circus is also real.

I'm not telling you inflation doesn't matter. It does. Every family paying for groceries, gas, and rent this year knows the number is real. The pain is real.

What is also real: we now live in a political environment where the clip of a politician's reaction to an economic question drives more conversation than the economic question itself. The clip hits 30 million views. The actual Consumer Price Index report gets 300,000.

That ratio tells you everything about who controls the debate — and who benefits from keeping it at the clip level.

When the clip is the story, nobody has to answer for the policy. Nobody has to defend the data. Nobody has to explain what they'd actually do differently. The clip replaces the accountability.

## What a real conversation about inflation would look like

It would start with the number. What is the current rate? How does it compare to a year ago, two years ago? What's driving it — energy, housing, food supply chains?

Then it would move to policy. What is the administration's current approach? What are the alternatives? What do economists across the spectrum say about those alternatives?

Then it would require accountability. If the rate is high, what's the causal story? What has changed under this administration? What would critics do instead — and what are the tradeoffs of those approaches?

None of that fits in a clip. None of that trends on X. But all of it matters more than six seconds of a response to a reporter's question, cut and deployed to confirm what you already believed before you pressed play.

## The clip format is winning a debate it doesn't deserve to win

I want to name this directly: in the current media environment, the politicians best at generating viral moments win — regardless of whether their policies work. The ones who get clipped most favorably build the most momentum. The policy conversation gets drowned out.

This is not a left or right problem. It is a structural problem with how political information moves in 2026. Everyone who shares a political clip without seeking the full context is contributing to a system that makes governing harder and accountability nearly impossible.

I've watched that system used against me. I've watched it used to distort things I said, things other people said, and things that happened in front of cameras. The tool works on everyone. It doesn't care about your side.

Share the clip if you want. But ask what it's missing first.
