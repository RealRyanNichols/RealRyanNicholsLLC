American money says it is legal tender.

That does not mean every private store has to take it.

The [Federal Reserve explains](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/currency_12772.htm) that no federal law requires a private business, person, or organization to accept currency or coins for goods or services. A private business can set its own payment policy unless a state law says otherwise.

That leaves Texas with a real policy question.

Should an in-person store be allowed to require a card or phone, or should every customer have the right to pay a reasonable cash amount?

## What the federal rule actually says

The phrase “legal tender” is often used as if it settles the argument.

It does not.

The Federal Reserve’s guidance distinguishes a valid offer of payment for a debt from a rule forcing every private seller to accept cash at the point of sale. A store can generally announce its payment terms before the transaction.

That means the current baseline is business choice unless another law creates a cash-acceptance requirement.

The policy debate is not about whether dollars are real money. It is about whether access to an in-person retail counter should come with a cash option.

## The strongest case for requiring cash

Cash works without a bank account, a charged phone, a card network, or a payment app.

That matters to people who are paid in cash, manage a tight budget with physical envelopes, do not qualify for conventional banking, or simply do not want every small purchase attached to a digital record.

Cash also works during some technical failures. A card processor can go down. Internet service can fail. A phone battery can die. A customer may have money in hand and still be unable to buy what is sitting on the counter.

Supporters of a requirement can make a narrow fairness argument: if a business opens a physical door to the public, it should accept the country’s physical money for ordinary purchases.

That case becomes stronger for essentials such as food, medicine, fuel, and basic household goods.

## The strongest case for letting businesses decide

Cash is not free to handle.

It has to be counted, secured, reconciled, deposited, and protected from theft. Employees can make change incorrectly. Businesses can receive counterfeit bills. A store with no cash drawer may reduce robbery risk and close faster.

Some business models are built around self-service kiosks, unattended counters, or very small staffs. Forcing those businesses to install and maintain cash systems creates a real cost.

There is also a basic property-rights argument. A seller should be able to state the terms of a voluntary transaction, and a customer who dislikes those terms can go elsewhere.

That argument is strongest when alternatives are nearby and the product is not essential.

## A broad mandate could create bad edge cases

“Every business must take cash” sounds simple until the exceptions arrive.

Should an unattended parking kiosk need a bill acceptor? Should a pop-up vendor carry change for a $100 bill? Should a business have to accept a bucket of coins? What happens during a robbery threat or when the safe is full? Does the rule apply to online orders picked up inside a store?

A workable law would need clear limits.

It could cap the bill size a merchant must accept, exempt fully unattended locations, permit temporary cash refusal during a documented safety event, and require the policy to cover ordinary in-person purchases rather than every possible transaction.

The details determine whether the rule protects access or creates theater.

## Ryan’s Take

Texas should require most in-person sellers of essential goods to accept cash for ordinary purchases, with narrow and written exceptions.

That is not the same as forcing every digital business or unattended machine to build a cash drawer.

The better rule would be limited:

1. Apply it to in-person transactions at staffed locations.
2. Focus first on food, medicine, fuel, and other basic needs.
3. Let merchants refuse unusually large bills when reasonable change is unavailable.
4. Allow temporary safety and system exceptions that are posted clearly.
5. Keep card, phone, and app payments fully available for customers who prefer them.

Cash may be used less often than it once was. That does not make the people who rely on it disappear.

A physical store should not quietly turn a working dollar into unusable money for the customer standing at the counter, especially when the purchase is something necessary.

## The question is about access and burden

The cleanest arguments on both sides are real.

Customers need a payment path that does not depend on a bank, a device, or a network. Businesses need room to control safety, cost, and operations.

The answer should not pretend one of those facts is fake.

A limited cash rule could protect access without ordering every seller to redesign every system. A careless rule could impose costs while creating confusing exceptions.
