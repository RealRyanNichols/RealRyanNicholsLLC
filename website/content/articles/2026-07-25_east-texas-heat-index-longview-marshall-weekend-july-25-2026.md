*By the Real Ryan Nichols Editorial Team*  
*Facts last checked: July 25, 2026, 9:26 a.m. Central*

There was no active National Weather Service watch, warning or advisory for the Longview point forecast at 9:26 a.m. Central on Saturday, July 25.

That is useful information. It is not permission to ignore the heat.

The same live National Weather Service forecast expected the heat index—the temperature it feels like when humidity is combined with the air temperature—to reach about **102°F in Longview and 103°F in Marshall on Saturday**. On Sunday, both point forecasts showed a heat index as high as **105°F**.

Afternoon air temperatures were also forecast to keep climbing:

| Day | Longview forecast | Marshall forecast |
|---|---:|---:|
| Saturday, July 25 | High near 97°F; heat index up to 102°F | High near 96°F; heat index up to 103°F |
| Sunday, July 26 | High near 99°F; heat index up to 105°F | High near 97°F; heat index up to 105°F |
| Monday, July 27 | High near 100°F | High near 98°F |
| Tuesday, July 28 | High near 102°F | High near 100°F |

The point forecasts also included a 20% chance of showers and thunderstorms before early Saturday afternoon. A shower could temporarily cool one neighborhood while another stays hot. It does not erase the broader multi-day heat risk.

The practical message for Gregg and Harrison counties is straightforward:

> **No active alert does not mean no risk. Plan for the forecast conditions, keep checking for changes, and act before someone becomes sick.**

## Who is most affected

High heat can affect anyone, but exposure and the ability to cool down are not evenly distributed.

People who deserve an early check-in include:

- outdoor workers and people doing yard, construction, road or farm work;
- athletes, coaches, marching groups and families at outdoor events;
- older adults and young children;
- people who do not have reliable air conditioning;
- people living alone;
- people who depend on someone else for transportation; and
- anyone who has already spent hours in the heat without enough water, rest or shade.

Vehicles are a separate danger. Never leave a child, dependent adult or pet in a parked vehicle, even for a short stop.

Employers and supervisors should not wait for a worker to ask for help. OSHA’s heat guidance calls for cool drinking water, frequent drinking before thirst, rest breaks that become longer and more frequent as heat stress rises, and a shaded or air-conditioned recovery area.

For work lasting more than two hours, OSHA says employers should also provide access to additional fluids containing electrolytes.

## Know when heat illness becomes an emergency

Heat exhaustion can include headache, nausea, dizziness, weakness, heavy sweating, thirst and reduced urine output. A person with those symptoms should be moved out of the heat, cooled and evaluated promptly.

Heat stroke is an emergency.

The CDC lists warning signs that can include:

- confusion or an altered mental state;
- slurred speech;
- loss of consciousness;
- seizures;
- hot skin or profuse sweating; and
- a very high body temperature.

If heat stroke is suspected, call **911**. Stay with the person, move them to shade or a cooler area, remove outer clothing and begin rapid cooling while emergency help is on the way.

Do not treat confusion, collapse or a seizure as something that can be fixed with a bottle of water and a ride home.

## A 15-minute heat plan to complete before noon

The best time to make a heat plan is before the hottest part of the day.

### Minute 1–3: Move the hardest work

List every outdoor task planned for the day.

Move strenuous work to the earliest or latest reasonable time. Delay anything nonessential that would place someone on a roof, pavement, in direct sun or around heat-producing equipment during the hottest hours.

If the work cannot be moved, reduce its duration and build in a replacement worker or buddy.

### Minute 4–6: Set the water, rest and shade rule

Do not use “take a break if you need one” as the entire plan.

Decide:

- where cool water will be;
- who will refill it;
- where the shaded or air-conditioned recovery location is;
- how often breaks will occur; and
- who is watching for confusion, unsteady movement or unusual behavior.

New workers and people returning after time away from heat may need a gradual increase in exposure.

### Minute 7–9: Check the people, not just the temperature

Choose two people to contact.

Ask direct questions:

- Is the air conditioner working?
- Is the home staying cool enough?
- Do you have water?
- Do you have a way to reach a cooler place if the power or air conditioning fails?
- Who will check again this afternoon?

Avoid collecting or posting private medical details. The goal is a practical safety check, not public disclosure.

### Minute 10–12: Turn on local alerts

Longview residents can register for the city’s [Smart911 emergency alerts](https://longviewtexas.gov/2864/Emergency-Alerts), which can deliver phone, text and email notifications.

Marshall residents can register for [CivicReady alerts](https://www.marshalltexas.net/591/Alerts-Notifications), including phone, text and email delivery in English or Spanish.

Those systems carry local emergency information. They do not replace the live National Weather Service forecast.

### Minute 13–15: Write the emergency rule

Put this where the household, crew or team can see it:

> **Confusion, collapse, seizure or loss of consciousness in the heat: call 911 and cool the person immediately.**

Then assign one person to make the call and one person to begin cooling. A clear rule is easier to follow under stress than a vague intention.

## Ryan’s Take

*Editorial analysis prepared by the Real Ryan Nichols Editorial Team; this is not presented as a firsthand quotation from Ryan.*

A weather alert should not be the starting gun for neighbor care.

Official alerts matter. They help the public recognize dangerous conditions and coordinate action. But a forecast can still demand responsibility before an alert appears.

If the heat index is expected to reach 102 to 105 degrees, the useful questions are not limited to “Is there a warning?”

They are:

- Who is working outside?
- Whose air conditioner is failing?
- Who cannot drive to a cooler place?
- Who is alone?
- Who will check again before late afternoon?

Resilience is not pretending the heat is harmless. It is doing ordinary work early enough that an emergency becomes less likely: moving the job, filling the cooler, scheduling the breaks, checking the neighbor and knowing when to call 911.

Faith can show up in the same practical way. Loving a neighbor may look like a phone call, a ride, a case of water or two minutes spent helping someone turn on local alerts.

Do not wait for a dramatic moment to become useful.

Make the plan while everyone is still able to make one.
