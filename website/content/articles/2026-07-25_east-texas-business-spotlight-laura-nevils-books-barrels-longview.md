*By the Real Ryan Nichols Editorial Team*  
*Last verified: July 25, 2026*

LONGVIEW, Texas — An independent bookstore has to sell books. A durable independent bookstore usually has to do more.

Books & Barrels, located at **208 N. Center Street in downtown Longview**, combines adult and children’s books, gifts, public events, local-author programming, book clubs, private gatherings, and a place where customers can sit down and stay awhile.

Local reporting identifies **Laura Nevils** as the owner and public face of the business. The store’s origin, however, was a partnership: when KLTV announced the concept in February 2020, it identified Laura Nevils, Chad Nevils, and Joanna Burrows as the three Longview partners creating it. Later coverage described Chad as Laura’s husband and Burrows as her mother.

The store opened during the summer of 2020, when the COVID-19 pandemic was disrupting normal retail, events, and downtown traffic. Four years later, CBS19 reported that Nevils told the public business had slowed and that East Texans responded with a surge of support.

That combination makes Books & Barrels worth studying. The positive story is not that independent retail is easy. It is that a local owner built a useful gathering place, made room for readers and writers, and was willing to tell the community when the business needed customers to show up.

## Who owns Books & Barrels?

The public record supports two related statements.

First, Books & Barrels began as a three-person family partnership. [KLTV’s February 10, 2020 report](https://www.kltv.com/2020/02/10/future-longview-business-will-feature-concept-that-combines-bookstore-wine-bar/) identified Laura Nevils, Chad Nevils, and Joanna Burrows as the partners developing the bookstore and wine-bar concept in downtown Longview. A publishing-industry report from Shelf Awareness likewise described Laura as one of three co-owners, working with her husband and mother.

Second, more recent East Texas coverage identifies Laura Nevils as the owner. [The East Texas Weekend’s December 2022 profile](https://www.theeasttexasweekend.com/2022/12/02/books-barrels/) and its [March 2024 follow-up](https://www.theeasttexasweekend.com/2024/03/08/books-wine-what-novel-idea/) both center Nevils in that role. CBS19 also identified her as the owner in its May 2024 report about community support for the store.

This article does not assign ownership percentages or claim to know whether every original partner’s legal interest remains unchanged. Those details were not established in the public materials reviewed. The responsible conclusion is narrower: Laura Nevils is publicly identified as the current owner, and the store was created through a documented three-partner family effort.

## A bookstore designed around atmosphere

The original concept was straightforward: create a comfortable place where people could browse or read a physical book and, if they chose, enjoy wine from the region.

That matters because a small bookstore cannot compete with the entire internet on inventory or speed. It can compete by doing what a shipping box cannot:

- learn what local readers want;
- recommend a book in plain language;
- introduce readers to East Texas authors;
- host a conversation, signing, workshop, or book club;
- give families a reason to spend time downtown; and
- create a setting people associate with discovery and belonging.

Books & Barrels’ [current official website](https://www.booksandbarrelstx.com/) emphasizes that broader role. It describes an independent bookstore carrying fiction and nonfiction for adults, young adults, and children, along with puzzles, apparel, tote bags, and other gifts. The site also promotes free public events, kid-friendly programming, book clubs, school groups, local-author signings, poetry nights, and private gatherings.

The business offers an online ordering path through Bookshop.org, but the physical store remains central to its identity.

## Opening in the hardest possible season

Early reporting said the partners hoped to open in March 2020. The pandemic disrupted that plan.

The East Texas Weekend later reported that Books & Barrels opened in the summer of 2020. That timeline is consistent with a 2021 Longview Public Library spotlight describing a May or June 2020 opening.

No article should romanticize what that meant. A new independent retailer normally needs foot traffic, events, predictable hours, and time for customers to form habits. Pandemic restrictions and public uncertainty interfered with all four.

The useful business lesson is not simply “persevere.” Perseverance without adaptation can become expensive denial.

The more practical lesson is to protect the central idea while changing how customers can participate. Books & Barrels kept the physical-store experience, but also developed recurring events, private-use options, order support, local-author activity, and an online purchasing path.

## Constructive work for readers and writers

### Making local authors easier to find

Independent authors face a basic visibility problem. Publishing a book does not guarantee shelf space, a launch audience, or the chance to explain the work to readers.

Books & Barrels advertises local authors as part of its inventory and hosts author signings and book launches. That is more constructive than simply placing a “local” label on one shelf. Events let authors meet readers, answer questions, and build the word-of-mouth that small publishers and self-published writers often need.

The City of Longview provides an authoritative example. During the Longview Public Library’s 2021 Indie Author Day, Laura Nevils presented a workshop on how local bookstores can work with local authors and what stores look for. The [city’s archived event record](https://www.longviewtexas.gov/Calendar.aspx?EID=6759&calType=0&day=2&month=11&year=2021) also shows that the event was free and included author discussions, readings, and publishing education.

That is a specific community contribution: using operating experience to help local writers understand the retail side of books.

### Giving children and families a low-pressure place to participate

The official site describes free, kid-friendly events and a children’s inventory. Earlier local coverage documented children’s story times among the store’s programming.

Not every family wants another expensive outing. A free public event built around books gives children a reason to enter a bookstore, hear a story, and see reading as a social activity rather than only a school assignment.

Event details change, so families should confirm the current schedule rather than rely on an old recurring calendar.

### Creating a downtown gathering place

A downtown business contributes in at least two ways.

The first is direct: it sells something useful and supports its own operation.

The second is indirect: it gives people another reason to visit the district, park, walk, eat, shop, attend an event, or discover a neighboring business.

Books & Barrels’ couches, group activities, author programs, and private-event options turn a retail visit into time spent downtown. That does not mean one bookstore is responsible for downtown Longview’s progress. Revitalization involves many owners, employees, property managers, city programs, events, and customers.

The accurate claim is that this bookstore adds a distinct reason for people to be there.

### Showing that asking for help can be responsible business leadership

In May 2024, [CBS19 reported](https://www.cbs19.tv/article/news/local/community-support-helps-save-locally-longview-bookstore/501-ab57ee86-f8d1-4e09-8e29-1433f81f1991) that business had slowed and that Nevils asked the community for support through social media. The response brought customers to the store.

That episode should not be turned into a permanent claim about the company’s finances. Public reporting from one moment does not establish current revenue, profit, debt, or stability.

It does demonstrate something useful: an owner can communicate a concrete need without pretending everything is perfect. Customers cannot respond to a problem they do not know exists.

The strongest version of that message is specific and actionable:

- explain what the business provides;
- explain what has changed;
- tell people exactly how they can help;
- make buying or attending easy; and
- thank the community without manufacturing urgency.

Transparency is not weakness when it gives customers a truthful choice.

## A useful guide for customers

### If you are looking for a specific book

Call before driving across East Texas. Ask whether the title is in stock and whether the store can order it if it is not.

Have the author’s name and, if possible, the ISBN. The ISBN helps distinguish between hardcover, paperback, large-print, audiobook, revised, and special editions.

If the store cannot fill the order directly, the official website links to a Bookshop.org storefront that supports independent bookselling.

### If you want to attend an event

Check the current event schedule and confirm:

- date and start time;
- whether registration is required;
- whether the program is intended for children, adults, or all ages;
- whether seating is limited;
- whether the author will sign books bought elsewhere; and
- whether food or beverages will be available.

An event listed in an older news article may no longer recur on the same day or at the same time.

### If you are a local author

Approach the store like a business partner, not simply as someone requesting a favor.

Prepare:

- the book title, format, ISBN, price, and publication date;
- a one-paragraph description;
- the intended reader;
- wholesale or consignment terms;
- how unsold copies are handled;
- a professional cover image;
- any credible local connection;
- a realistic event plan; and
- the ways you will help promote the appearance.

Do not assume a store can accept every title. Shelf space, payment processing, returns, audience fit, and staff time all matter.

### If you want to host a private event or book club

Use the store’s official contact information and ask about:

- available dates and times;
- capacity and seating;
- accessibility;
- setup and cleanup;
- outside food or refreshments;
- deposits and cancellation terms;
- beverage service;
- insurance or vendor requirements; and
- whether the store remains open to the public.

Do not rely on capacity or pricing quoted in older media coverage.

## Lessons other East Texas owners can use

### Sell the reason to visit, not only the inventory

Books are available almost everywhere. A local bookstore must answer a harder question: why should someone come inside?

Books & Barrels answers with discovery, atmosphere, events, author access, group space, and personal help.

Every local business can ask the same question. A hardware store might teach a repair class. A nursery might host seasonal planting demonstrations. A coffee shop might provide a reliable meeting space. A skilled trade might offer homeowner education.

The added experience should reinforce the core business rather than distract from it.

### Build programming that uses what you already know

Nevils’ Indie Author Day workshop grew directly from bookstore operations. It did not require inventing an unrelated service.

The most credible community education often comes from ordinary working knowledge:

- how a retailer chooses products;
- how a contractor prepares an estimate;
- how a restaurant handles a large reservation;
- how a grower plans for a season; or
- how a professional helps customers avoid common mistakes.

Useful teaching builds trust because it helps people make better decisions.

### Give people a clear way to support you

“Support local” is a broad slogan. A better request names an action:

- buy one book;
- reserve a seat;
- attend an author event;
- order a title through the store’s link;
- bring a book club;
- buy a gift; or
- tell a reader about the shop.

Specific requests are easier to act on and easier to measure.

### Keep current information current

Independent businesses lose sales when hours, addresses, event calendars, menus, and contact details conflict across platforms.

Books & Barrels’ history shows why the current official listing should control. Early coverage used a neighboring street number; the business now publishes 208 N. Center Street.

A simple monthly audit of the website, Google Business Profile, social accounts, chamber listing, and event pages can prevent a customer from driving to the wrong place or arriving when the door is closed.

## Editorial perspective

Books & Barrels is doing constructive work because it treats books as the beginning of a community activity rather than the end of a transaction.

The store gives readers a place to browse, gives families another activity, gives authors a way to meet an audience, and gives downtown Longview a business that can host conversation.

Its 2024 public call for support adds another practical lesson. A local owner does not have to perform invincibility. Honest communication can give the community a chance to decide whether something valuable stays.

That is not charity. Customers receive books, events, space, service, and discovery in return. It is a local exchange built around usefulness and trust.
