In the final hours of his presidency, Joe Biden signed a preemptive pardon for Anthony Fauci.

He didn't sign it with a pen in his hand.

He used an autopen — a mechanical device that replicates a handwritten signature — that has been used by presidents for routine correspondence for decades. And depending on who you ask, that distinction either matters enormously or not at all.

Donald Trump says the pardon is void. His administration declared it "terminated." X has been divided on this for months. Legal scholars have largely pushed back on Trump's framing. The House Oversight Committee released a full report on what they are calling "The Autopen Presidency."

Nobody has tested it in court.

And that absence — the fact that no one has actually litigated the question — is the part of this story that deserves more attention than it is getting.

## What Actually Happened

Let me lay out the undisputed facts before I get into the dispute.

Biden issued a broad preemptive pardon for Fauci with hours remaining in his presidency. The pardon was not for a specific conviction or charge — it was preemptive, covering potential future federal prosecutions. This type of pardon is unusual but not without precedent. President Ford's pardon of Nixon was preemptive.

The pardon was executed via autopen.

Trump, upon taking office, declared the autopen pardons void. His position: a presidential pardon requires the president's direct, personal signature — and an autopen doesn't count. He applied this argument broadly to Biden's final-days clemency actions, which also included pardons for family members.

The Oversight Committee's report went further, alleging that Biden was not meaningfully engaged in the signing of many of his final executive actions — that the autopen was operating with minimal presidential awareness of what was being signed.

## What Legal Experts Are Actually Saying

Here is where I have to be straight with you: the legal consensus does not favor Trump's position.

Constitutional scholars from across the political spectrum have noted that the Constitution does not specify the exact physical mechanism by which a president must execute a pardon. Autopens have been used by presidents of both parties for decades without legal challenge. There is no case law establishing that an autopen signature voids a presidential act.

Stanford Law School posted an analysis titled "Why Trump Can't 'Void' Biden's Pardons Because of Autopen." PBS News ran a fact-check reaching similar conclusions. The Democracy Docket documented the legal scholars' near-unanimous position that a pardon, once granted, cannot be undone — regardless of the signing mechanism.

Trump's legal team, as of this writing, has not made this argument before a federal court.

That is significant.

If the autopen argument were strong, you'd litigate it. Courts exist to resolve exactly these kinds of constitutional disputes. The fact that the administration has declared the pardons void without seeking a judicial ruling suggests one of two things: they know the argument won't survive court scrutiny, or they don't need the argument to succeed legally because they're using it politically.

I don't know which one it is. But I know a declared position that has never been tested is not the same as a settled legal fact.

## The Oversight Committee Report Is Worth Reading

The House Oversight Committee released what they are calling a report on "The Autopen Presidency" — the claim that Biden was so cognitively diminished in his final months that many of his executive actions were effectively signed without his meaningful knowledge or consent.

That is a serious allegation. Not a partisan attack — a serious institutional claim that, if true, would raise questions about the validity of a wide range of Biden-era executive actions.

I have not read every page of that report. I am not in a position to verify every claim in it. What I can tell you is that it is a public document, it is based on testimony and evidence that Republicans on the committee found credible enough to publish, and it deserves to be engaged with seriously rather than dismissed based on who published it.

If the report's core claim is true — that executive actions were being executed without the president's actual engagement — that is a governance crisis bigger than any single pardon.

And if the report's core claim is false or overstated, the people who published it should answer for that.

Either way, someone needs to actually find out.

## The Fauci Question Isn't Going Away

Anthony Fauci has not been charged with anything. The preemptive pardon was issued before any charges were filed. The Oversight Project has argued publicly that prosecution should happen before any statute of limitations expires, and that the pardon is invalid due to the autopen issue.

Revolver News published an analysis in May 2026 arguing that, pardon or not, there is a time-sensitive case for charging Fauci quickly — because certain claims would need to be established in court regardless of clemency validity.

I am not a lawyer. I am not telling you whether Fauci should be charged or whether the pardon is valid.

What I am saying is this: if Trump's administration genuinely believes the autopen pardon is legally void, the way to establish that is to bring charges and let a court rule on the pardon's validity when it is raised as a defense. Declaring something void through press releases and executive memos does not create legal precedent. A court ruling does.

The absence of that court fight, more than a year into the Trump administration, is telling.

## Why This Matters Beyond Fauci

I want to pull back from the specific names for a moment because the principle here is bigger than any individual.

If a president can issue preemptive pardons for allies using an autopen in the final hours of an administration — and those pardons are legally unassailable — that is a power with enormous implications. It means any outgoing president can effectively immunize a list of names from federal prosecution with no oversight, no deliberation, and potentially no personal engagement.

That is a power worth examining carefully regardless of which party is using it.

And if Trump's counter-claim is correct — that autopen pardons are void — that opens a completely different set of questions about which Biden-era actions stand and which don't.

Both possibilities have serious institutional consequences. Neither one is getting the serious public deliberation it deserves.

The people who should be forcing that deliberation are in Congress. They have the authority to hold hearings, compel testimony, and send referrals to DOJ. They have used that authority selectively, as Congress always does. What I want to know is whether anyone with actual power is willing to push this to a definitive legal resolution — or whether the autopen pardon question will stay in the political atmosphere indefinitely, used as a talking point by everyone and settled by no one.

## What I Need You to Do

**Read the primary sources.** The Oversight Committee report is public. The scholarly analyses are public. The C-SPAN record of relevant hearings is public. This is not a classified situation. The documents are available.

**Stop accepting declarations as conclusions.** "The pardon is void" is a political position. "The pardon is valid" is a legal analysis. They are not the same thing. The only thing that actually resolves the question is a court ruling — and we don't have one yet.

**Demand a legal test.** If your representatives are invoking this pardon question to make political arguments, ask them why no one has sought a judicial resolution. The answer to that question tells you something about whether anyone actually wants the truth or just the talking point.

**Share this article.** This is a story with long-term institutional implications that is getting reduced to a headline. The more people who understand what is actually being claimed — and what has and has not been tested — the better.
