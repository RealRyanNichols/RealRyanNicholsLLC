Last week, screenshots exploded across X showing what looked like a direct message conversation between Eric Trump and UFC legend Daniel Cormier.

In the alleged exchange, Trump asked Cormier point blank: *"Are any of the fights tomorrow rigged? I've been eyeing the Lopes fight and I think an upset wouldn't be too unrealistic."*

Cormier's supposed reply: *"No, none of our fights are rigged and honestly, I am appalled that you would even ask me something like that."*

The screenshots blew up. Millions of engagements. Thousands of quote-posts. Reactions from every corner of the internet — sports fans, political commentators, late-night pundits.

There was just one problem.

Both men say it never happened.

{{share}}

## What the receipts actually show

Daniel Cormier told Fox News the posts were "not real" and that his account was hacked. He went on his YouTube channel to address the situation directly, calling the screenshots fake and pointing out one simple, verifiable fact: he and Eric Trump do not follow each other on any social platform.

Eric Trump called the screenshots "fake, AI-generated" and said he has never spoken to Cormier.

ESPN reported Cormier's denial. Yahoo Sports covered the full sequence — the viral post, the delete, the fallout. Fox News ran the video response. The Daily Beast documented how Eric Trump himself got trolled in the aftermath.

Multiple major outlets confirmed: this was almost certainly fabricated content. Possibly AI-generated. Possibly a hack. Either way — not real.

And yet it spread at full speed before anyone could stop it.

## The platform did nothing that mattered

Here is what bothers me about this situation, and it should bother you too.

X has spent three years telling us it is the home of free speech and open information. It markets itself as the public square. It has fought battles over content moderation, censorship, and what it means to let information flow freely.

But when a set of viral screenshots that almost certainly never happened starts spreading across its platform — screenshots that implicate a sitting president's son in a corruption scandal involving professional sports — what did the platform do?

It surfaced it. The algorithm amplified it. Millions of people saw it before either party could respond.

I've been on the receiving end of fabricated content spreading about me. I know what it is to watch something untrue get shared as if it were gospel, and I know the lag between "viral lie" and "correction" is not even close to even. The lie travels first class. The correction rides coach — if it gets on the plane at all.

The Cormier situation is a textbook example of that dynamic.

## AI-generated disinformation is now table stakes

The thing that makes this moment different from five years ago is the quality of the fake.

We are no longer talking about blurry photoshops or obviously edited screenshots. We are talking about AI-generated content that is clean, plausible, and indistinguishable to most readers at a glance.

X knows this. The platform has written policies about AI content disclosure. It has suspended creators for posting unlabeled AI videos of armed conflict. It has an entire PR strategy built around fighting manipulation.

And yet when fabricated AI-generated DM screenshots of a presidential family member asking if sports events are rigged hit the platform, it trended before either party could issue a denial.

The policy exists. The enforcement is a different story.

## What this means for anyone with a public profile

I want to say this clearly, because it matters: **if it can happen to Cormier, it can happen to anyone.**

You do not have to be famous. You do not have to be controversial. You do not have to have enemies in high places. You just need to be someone that a bad actor decides to target with a convincing enough fake.

And right now, creating convincing fakes is cheaper and easier than it has ever been in human history.

X's answer to that is a labeling policy and a report button. I've used both. I know how well they work.

The burden of proof, as always, falls on the person being lied about — not the person spreading the lie, and not the platform making money off the engagement.

## The real question no one is asking

Everyone is focused on whether the screenshots were real.

The more important question is: **what happens when fabricated content this damaging spreads this fast with this little friction?**

A man who built a career in one of the most physically demanding sports on earth had to spend news cycles defending himself from a conversation he says never happened. A presidential family member had to issue a denial. Sports media covered it like it was real until the denials piled up.

That is the cost. Who paid it? Not the person who made the fake. Not X. Not the algorithm.

Cormier paid it. Trump paid it. And every reader who shared that screenshot without knowing it was fake became an unpaid distribution partner for whoever created the lie.
