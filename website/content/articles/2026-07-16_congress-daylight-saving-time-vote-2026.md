This week the House passed the Sunshine Protection Act, 308-117 — a bill that would end the twice-a-year clock change and lock the country onto daylight saving time year-round.

X lit up almost immediately. Not because the vote was close. Because there's a second, competing bill — the Sunshine for Our Kids Act — pushing the opposite fix: permanent *standard* time instead.

Two bills. Same problem. Opposite answers. And a timeline on the Senate side nobody should hold their breath for — Congress has tried this exact fight before and it's died before.

## Why the two camps aren't actually arguing about the same thing

The permanent-daylight-time crowd wants longer light in the evening — more daylight after work, brighter dinners, sports leagues that don't lose their fields to darkness at 5:30 in November.

The permanent-standard-time crowd — including a lot of sleep scientists — wants mornings that match how the human body actually wakes up. Standard time keeps sunrise closer to when people and kids are getting up and heading out, instead of pushing dawn back toward 8 or 9 a.m. in the dead of winter.

Both sides are right about something. That's what makes this one of the rare fights in Washington that isn't actually red versus blue. The bill sponsors on both sides cross party lines. Nobody's villain here is the other party. The villain is a decision Congress has been able to duck for over 50 years by just... not deciding.

## The angle I haven't seen anyone write

Every piece I've read on this treats it as an energy-policy fight or a golf-industry lobbying fight. Nobody's writing it as what it actually is at my kitchen table: a fight about whether my kids walk to the bus stop in the dark or come home from practice in it.

Permanent daylight time means kids getting up for school under a still-black sky for months at a stretch. Permanent standard time means practices, games, and evening plans getting swallowed by darkness before dinner's even on the table. There's no version of this that doesn't take something away from a family schedule that's already stretched thin.

I've watched this exact debate play out in my own house every single time the clocks change — the groggy mornings, the sudden pitch-black drive to practice, the two weeks it takes anyone under twelve to adjust. Whatever Congress lands on, some parent, somewhere, is going to be standing in the dark with a kid and a backpack, wondering why the people who voted on this don't have to live with the consequences at 6:45 a.m.

## What I'd actually want from Washington here

Pick one. Either one. The twice-a-year switch is the actual enemy — not daylight time, not standard time, the switching itself. Every study on this fight agrees on that much even when they disagree on which fixed time is better. Congress arguing for another decade while doing nothing is the worst outcome on the table, and it's the most likely one.
