On Friday, July 3, Taylor Swift and Travis Kelce got married at Madison Square Garden. Adam Sandler officiated. Stevie Nicks performed. Jason Kelce stood up as best man, Austin Swift as man of honor. By most accounts from people who were actually in the room, it was personal, warm, and exactly the kind of wedding two people who've spent years in public would want to have.

By Saturday morning, X had turned it into something else entirely: proof that a pop star had personally dimmed the lights on New York City.

I want to walk through what actually happened here, because it's a clean example of a pattern I write about a lot — the internet deciding the story before it checks whether the story is true.

{{share}}

## What people said happened

New York was in the middle of a brutal heatwave. Con Edison had asked residents across the city to cut back on electricity use as demand spiked to dangerous levels, and at one point close to 19,000 customers temporarily lost power.

Then the Empire State Building lit up in blue for Swift's wedding, and MSG blazed with lights for the ceremony inside. One tweet that captured the mood and spread fast: *"we are supposed to conserve energy while @taylorswift13 lights up the garden like an effing Christmas tree."*

That framing — celebrity excess causing regular New Yorkers to suffer — took off. People connected the outages directly to the wedding. Commentary about "disgusting" juxtaposition and a "tacky, childish, narcissistic spectacle" ran across outlets and spread across timelines. It fit a story a lot of people already wanted to tell: rich and famous people don't have to play by the same rules as everyone else.

## What Con Edison actually said

Con Edison put out a statement clarifying that Taylor Swift's wedding did not contribute to the citywide power outages. The outages were a heatwave-and-grid-demand problem playing out across the entire city, not a single-venue problem caused by one building's lighting for one night.

That correction did not travel nearly as far as the original accusation. It rarely does.

## Why this pattern matters more than this wedding

I've written before about what happens when the internet decides it already knows the story — Victor Wembanyama's anthem posture, the Elmo pile-on, fans turning on athletes over a single clip stripped of context. This is the same mechanism, just with a different target.

Here's the sequence every time: something ambiguous or coincidental happens near something famous. Someone posts the most damning possible interpretation. That post gets the engagement, because outrage travels faster than nuance. The correction comes later, quieter, to a fraction of the audience that saw the original claim.

By the time Con Ed's statement circulated, the narrative was already locked in for a lot of people: Taylor Swift caused a blackout so she could have a fancy wedding. That's a tidy, shareable story. It also isn't what happened.

## You're allowed to have opinions about the wedding itself

None of this means the wedding is above criticism. People are allowed to think a $10-million-plus celebrity wedding during a heatwave, with street closures on a holiday weekend, is tone-deaf optics. That's a fair conversation about judgment and timing.

But "tone-deaf optics" and "caused a blackout" are two very different accusations, and only one of them is true. Conflating them is exactly how a fair critique turns into a false one that spreads faster and sticks longer.

I'd rather people criticize what actually happened than invent a worse version because it performs better online.
