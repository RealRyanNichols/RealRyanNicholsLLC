Folarin Balogun got a red card in the U.S. win over Bosnia and Herzegovina. Under FIFA's own rules, that's automatic: one-game suspension, no appeal, no exceptions. It's been that way, more or less, since 1962.

Then President Trump called FIFA president Gianni Infantino and asked him to review it.

Balogun played against Belgium two days later.

"Thank you to FIFA for doing what was right, and reversing a great injustice." That's the post. No mention of the phone call in it — just the outcome, delivered like it was always going to go this way.

## I know exactly what a rule that bends for some people and not others looks like

I've spent years now living inside a system that tells you the process is neutral, the rules are the rules, everyone gets treated the same. I've watched that promise get broken in real time, in real cases, for real people who didn't have Howard Lutnick and Andrew Giuliani working the phones on their behalf.

That's not a metaphor. Reporting on this one names the actual people who got involved: the Commerce Secretary, the executive director of the White House's own World Cup task force, lawyers recruited specifically to make the case to FIFA. That's not a fan complaining to a hotline. That's the machinery of a government leaning on a private governing body to change a ruling that had already been made, for one team, in the middle of a tournament everyone else is also trying to win fairly.

UEFA didn't mince words. They called the reversal "unprecedented, incomprehensible and unjustifiable" and said FIFA had "crossed a red line." European Parliament members are now calling for a formal probe into Infantino over it.

Here's what gets me: nobody is disputing that the red card was correctly given. Nobody is arguing Balogun didn't do what triggered it. The rule was applied correctly and then un-applied — not because new facts came in, but because a call came in.

## This is the same story I keep writing, just with a different jersey

Nearly a thousand people faced federal charges after January 6. Sentences varied wildly for similar conduct, access to resources determined outcomes as much as the underlying facts did, and connections mattered more than anyone in charge wanted to admit out loud. I've lived the version of this story where the person on the wrong end of the phone call doesn't get to make one back.

Balogun getting to play in a soccer match is a low-stakes way to watch the exact same mechanism work in public, with cameras on it, with the parties involved not even bothering to deny that a call was made. That's almost a gift. Most of the time this kind of leverage gets applied quietly, in a room nobody's filming, to a case nobody's covering. This time it happened in front of a global audience during the biggest sporting event on earth, and the man who made the call posted about it himself.

If a red card — a clean, factual, undisputed red card — can get reversed with one phone call to the right person, ask yourself what that means for the rulings you don't get to watch happen. Ask what it means for the cases that don't have 48 million viewers.

## Belgium had the better answer anyway

The U.S. lost 4-1. Belgium didn't need the exception overturned to win — they just played. That's usually how it goes when the rules get bent for someone: the bending was never actually necessary to win, it just proved that someone with enough pull can make the outcome different regardless of whether it needed to be.

That's the part that should bother people more than the scoreline does.
