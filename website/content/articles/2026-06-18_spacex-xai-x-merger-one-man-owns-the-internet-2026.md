In February 2026, SpaceX officially acquired xAI in an all-stock deal that Bloomberg called the largest merger in human history.

The number: $1.25 trillion.

And because xAI had already acquired X — the platform formerly known as Twitter — that means one man now controls, under a single corporate umbrella:

- The dominant private rocket company on the planet, with contracts from NASA and the U.S. military
- Starlink, the satellite internet service that provides connectivity to governments, militaries, and civilians in conflict zones
- The world's most rapidly advancing AI lab, with its own large language model and image generation tools
- A social media platform with hundreds of millions of active users

The name on all of it: Elon Musk.

## Let's be clear about what this is

I want to set aside the culture war arguments for a moment — the "Elon is a free speech hero" versus "Elon is a right-wing propagandist" debates that I see play out on X every single day.

Those arguments are real and they matter. But they are distractions from a simpler structural question:

**Is it a problem when one private individual controls the communications infrastructure, AI development, and space launch capabilities of the most powerful nation on earth?**

That question is not partisan. It should concern everyone.

When X was just a social media company, the platform power debate was significant but bounded. A single company moderating speech on its platform is a big deal, but it is not unprecedented. Facebook, YouTube, and Google all wield that kind of power.

What is unprecedented is the combination.

## The orbital data center angle deserves more attention

When Musk announced the SpaceX-xAI deal, he was direct about the rationale. AI development requires massive amounts of computing power, cooling, and energy. Building that infrastructure on Earth imposes costs on local communities and the environment. The solution, Musk said, was space-based data centers powered by solar energy.

SpaceX organizes its new structure into three segments: Connectivity (Starlink), Space (launches, Starship, government contracts), and AI (xAI, Grok, X).

Read that again.

The company that launches the satellites is the same company that operates the satellite internet is the same company that runs the AI model is the same company that owns the social media platform.

If you have ever had a post removed, an account suspended, or a story buried on X, you might want to think about who controls every layer of that stack — from the data center in orbit to the feed you scroll through at night.

## This is not just a business story

Al Jazeera covered the announcement. CNBC ran the valuation. Vice covered the implications for social media.

What most of the coverage missed is the human cost dimension of this level of consolidation.

I've written before about what it feels like to be on the receiving end of platform power. When X had me suspended, when content about me was allowed to circulate while my own responses were throttled — that was a small company with enormous power making decisions about whose voice mattered.

Now multiply that power by a factor that includes rocket launches and AI-generated content and satellite internet.

The people who can be silenced, buried, or amplified by this stack are not abstract users. They are journalists. Activists. Whistleblowers. People like me who filed legal complaints and tried to document what was done to them.

Every one of those people is now dependent on infrastructure controlled by a single entity that has demonstrated — not hypothetically, but in practice — the willingness to use platform power selectively.

## I'm not saying Musk is a villain

I want to be direct about this because the reflexive response to this kind of piece is to assume it is an attack on the man.

It is not.

The problem is not Musk specifically. The problem is the structure. No individual — regardless of their politics, their competence, or their stated values — should sit at the intersection of all these systems without meaningful independent oversight.

Antitrust law exists for exactly this reason. Not because businesses are evil. Because unchecked concentration of power is dangerous regardless of who wields it.

The question regulators should be asking right now is not "do we trust Musk?" The question is "should any single person control this?"

My answer is no. I'd say that about anyone.

## What X users should understand

If you use X — and I still do, because it is where the real-time conversation happens whether we like it or not — you are now using a product that sits inside the largest private corporate empire in human history.

The algorithm that decides what you see is built by a company that also decides what content gets labeled as AI-generated, what advertisers get approved, and what data gets processed through orbital servers.

That is not a conspiracy theory. That is the business structure Musk announced on February 2, 2026. He said it out loud.

The only question is whether we are paying attention.
