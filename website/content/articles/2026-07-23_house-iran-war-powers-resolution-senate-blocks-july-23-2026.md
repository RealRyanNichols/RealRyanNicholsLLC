*By the Real Ryan Nichols Editorial Team*

The House and Senate sent opposite signals on Iran war powers Thursday.

The House voted **214–208** to approve H.Con.Res.89, a concurrent resolution directing the president to remove U.S. armed forces from hostilities against Iran unless Congress has declared war or enacted a specific authorization.

Hours later, the Senate voted **47–49** against a motion to discharge S.J.Res.180, Senator Chris Van Hollen’s parallel Iran war-powers proposal, from the Foreign Relations Committee.

Those two facts belong together. They also require a careful limit:

**The House vote did not, by itself, end U.S. military operations. The Senate vote was not final passage of its resolution. It was a failed motion to bring the measure out of committee.**

As of the Senate’s adjournment at 4:51 p.m. Eastern on July 23, Congress had not produced same-day bicameral approval of an Iran war-powers measure.

## The verified record

The [official House Clerk roll call](https://clerk.house.gov/Votes/2026282) records H.Con.Res.89 as passed at 10:24 a.m. Eastern:

- 214 representatives voted yes;
- 208 voted no;
- no member voted present; and
- nine members did not vote.

The Clerk’s party breakdown shows 210 Democrats and four Republicans voting yes. The no votes came from 207 Republicans and one independent.

The [official Senate floor log](https://www.periodicalpress.senate.gov/thursday-july-23-2026/) records a different procedural result:

- the Senate considered a motion to discharge S.J.Res.180 from the Foreign Relations Committee;
- the motion was not agreed to, 47–49; and
- the Senate adjourned without taking a final passage vote on S.J.Res.180.

The Senate is scheduled to reconvene Monday, July 27. That does not guarantee another Iran vote.

## What H.Con.Res.89 actually says

Representative Pramila Jayapal introduced H.Con.Res.89 on April 23. The [official Government Publishing Office text](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hconres89ih/html/BILLS-119hconres89ih.htm) invokes section 5(c) of the War Powers Resolution.

The operative language directs the president to remove the use of U.S. armed forces from hostilities against Iran, its government, or its military unless Congress explicitly authorizes that force through a declaration of war or a specific authorization for use of military force.

The resolution also contains three important limits.

It says it should not be read to prevent the United States from:

- defending the United States, U.S. armed forces, diplomatic facilities, or allied states from imminent attack;
- maintaining a regional troop presence for defensive purposes; or
- keeping regional forces that are not engaged in hostilities against Iran.

It also says the resolution should not disrupt intelligence collection, analysis, or sharing that the president determines is appropriate and in the national-security interest.

Finally, the text expressly says it **does not authorize military force**.

Those clauses matter because the resolution is not a command to remove every American service member from the region. It targets forces engaged in hostilities against Iran while preserving specified defensive, non-hostile, and intelligence activities.

## What the House vote changed

The House vote created a formal chamber position on H.Con.Res.89.

That is more than a press release or a nonbinding sense-of-Congress statement adopted without a recorded vote. Every representative’s position is now available in the public roll call. The resolution cleared one chamber with support from members of both major parties.

The vote also keeps the core constitutional question in public view: whether continuing hostilities against Iran should rest on a specific congressional authorization.

The Congressional Research Service explained in its [June 2025 review of Iran war-powers issues](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IN12577) that the War Powers Resolution provides expedited procedures for Congress to consider authorizing or disapproving military action. CRS also described competing institutional positions: some members argued that force against Iran required congressional authorization, while the executive branch asserted Article II authority for certain uses of force.

Thursday’s vote did not resolve that legal disagreement. It recorded where the House stood on this resolution.

## What the House vote did not change

H.Con.Res.89 is a concurrent resolution whose opening formula says, “the Senate concurring.” The House’s approval alone is not bicameral concurrence.

The vote therefore did not:

- enact a declaration of war;
- enact a new authorization for use of military force;
- produce Senate approval of H.Con.Res.89;
- require a presidential signature;
- establish that every American force must leave the region;
- eliminate the defensive exceptions written into the resolution; or
- prove that military operations stopped after the vote.

Representative Jayapal described the vote as an order to end what she calls an illegal war. That is the sponsor’s stated legal and political position. The narrower procedural fact is that her resolution passed the House and still lacked Senate concurrence at the end of Thursday’s session.

## What happened in the Senate

The Senate vote is easy to misstate.

Senators did not vote 47–49 on final passage of S.J.Res.180. They voted on whether to discharge the measure from the Foreign Relations Committee.

A successful discharge motion would have moved the resolution out of committee and opened a path for further Senate action. Because the motion failed, the Senate did not reach final passage Thursday.

The official floor log does not support saying that 49 senators voted for continued war or that 47 senators voted for immediate withdrawal in every circumstance. The vote was procedural, even though its practical effect was to block the resolution from advancing that day.

It is also not accurate to combine the House’s 214–208 vote and the Senate’s 47–49 vote as if they were votes on the same legislative vehicle. The House acted on H.Con.Res.89. The Senate acted on a motion involving S.J.Res.180.

## Why the two chambers can move differently

House and Senate war-powers measures can have similar purposes while following different legislative paths.

The House passed a concurrent resolution. In the Senate, supporters first needed to remove their joint resolution from committee. Different sponsors, chamber rules, text, timing, and procedural posture can produce different coalitions.

That distinction has practical consequences:

1. A House majority can create a public record without securing Senate action.
2. A failed Senate discharge motion can stop a proposal before senators vote on its merits at final passage.
3. A headline saying only “Congress votes on Iran war” can hide the most important procedural facts.

The best way to read a war-powers story is to ask four questions:

- Which chamber acted?
- Which resolution was before it?
- Was the vote procedural or final?
- What additional step is required before the measure has broader effect?

## A fast-moving story with a clear status line

This article reflects the official congressional record available after both chambers adjourned on **July 23, 2026**.

The status can change. The Senate could revisit procedure, act on another resolution, consider the House measure, negotiate an authorization, or take no additional action. The House could also vote on related oversight, funding, reporting, or authorization language.

Readers should treat social-media posts claiming that “Congress ended the war” or that “the Senate voted down withdrawal” as incomplete unless they identify the measure and the procedural question.

The accurate short version is:

> The House passed H.Con.Res.89 by 214–208. The Senate separately rejected, 47–49, a motion to discharge S.J.Res.180 from committee. Congress did not complete bicameral action on an Iran war-powers measure Thursday.

## Four questions citizens can ask Congress now

Citizens do not have to begin with a slogan. They can ask for specific, answerable commitments.

### 1. What legal authority supports current hostilities?

Ask the member to identify the constitutional and statutory authority the member believes applies. If the answer relies on an existing authorization for use of military force, ask for the exact statute and explanation.

### 2. Should Congress vote on a specific Iran authorization?

An authorization can define mission, geographic scope, targets, reporting duties, duration, and repeal conditions. Ask whether the member supports a direct vote and what limits the member would require.

### 3. What are the objectives and exit conditions?

“Success” should be measurable enough for Congress and the public to evaluate. Ask what military objective is being pursued, what conditions would end operations, and when Congress will receive public and classified reporting.

### 4. How will Congress account for costs and risks?

Ask for recurring public reporting on deployments, expenditures, civilian-harm assessments, force-protection risks, and any material change in the mission. Classified facts may need protection, but classification should not erase Congress’s duty to conduct oversight.

Use the House’s [Find Your Representative](https://www.house.gov/representatives/find-your-representative) page and the Senate’s [senator directory](https://www.senate.gov/senators/senators-contact.htm) to identify the officials who represent you. Be concise, include your ZIP code, name the resolution, and request a written response.

## Ryan’s Take

Congress should not hide a decision about war inside procedural fog.

People can disagree about the threat from Iran, the president’s constitutional authority, the need for military action, and the risks of withdrawal. But those disagreements are exactly why elected representatives should cast clear votes on authority, objectives, limits, cost, and duration.

The House put one position on the record Thursday. The Senate blocked a procedural path for a parallel proposal. Neither outcome is a substitute for a durable, specific policy that the public can read and Congress must defend.

The honest standard should be simple: if military force is necessary, say what authorizes it and what it is meant to accomplish. If current hostilities should end, say which forces and operations must stop and how legitimate defensive needs will be preserved.

Voters deserve more than victory language from either side. They deserve a record they can audit.
