A blank page is less intimidating when the first question is not “What should I write?”

It is “What kind of monster should I build?”

Longview Public Library is hosting a free Monster-Building Handbook writing project this afternoon for young writers ages 8 and older. The event runs from 4:30 to 5:30 p.m. at the library, 222 W. Cotton St.

The city’s [official event listing](https://www.longviewtexas.gov/Calendar.aspx?EID=10900&calType=0&day=21&month=9&year=2026) says the activity leads children through creating an original monster character and constructing a narrative around it.

That is more than a seasonal craft. It is a practical entry point into storytelling.

## What is happening today

The program is part of the library’s Discovery Kids STEAM Lab.

The confirmed details, checked on September 21, 2026, are:

- **Time:** 4:30 to 5:30 p.m.
- **Place:** Longview Public Library, 222 W. Cotton St.
- **Age:** Designed for children ages 8 and older
- **Cost:** Free
- **Contact:** 903-235-1345

The library says the project is supported by a Tigris Foundation grant through the Cosmic Writers program. The September series also included sessions on September 14 and has another listed for September 28, with future dates planned in October.

The official listing does not state whether advance registration is required or whether supplies are limited. Families should call the library before leaving if they need confirmation about space, accommodations, or participation details.

## Why monster-building works

“Write a story” can feel enormous.

Build a monster, and the writer suddenly has smaller questions to answer.

What does it look like? What does it want? What is it afraid of? Where does it live? Who misunderstands it? What happens when it enters a room?

Those questions quietly teach character, setting, motivation, conflict, and plot.

A child does not need to know those formal terms to begin using them.

The monster becomes a container for imagination. The handbook provides enough structure to keep the child moving without deciding every creative choice for them.

That balance matters. Too little structure can make a blank page feel impossible. Too much can turn writing into a worksheet with one acceptable answer.

## Who may benefit most

The program is useful for children who already fill notebooks with characters.

It may be even more useful for the child who says, “I do not know what to write.”

Some young people have stories but need permission to make them strange. Some are comfortable drawing but not writing. Some can explain a whole imaginary world out loud and freeze when asked to put one sentence on paper.

A guided, low-cost program gives them a place to test the next step.

Because the event is free, it also gives caregivers a way to try a creative activity without buying a class, a workbook, or a large set of supplies first.

## What families can do before 4:30

Ask four questions if they matter to your family:

2. Are materials provided?

4. Are accommodations available for a child who needs them?

Do not assume missing today means missing the project.

## A useful hour can begin a longer habit

The value of a library program is not limited to the page completed inside the room.

A child can bring the character home and keep asking questions.

Give the monster a problem. Write a letter from the monster. Draw the place it sleeps. Describe the sound it makes when it is trying not to be noticed. Let the child dictate while an adult types if handwriting becomes the barrier.

The point is not to produce a polished book in one afternoon.

The point is to prove that a story can be built one decision at a time.

That lesson reaches beyond writing. Complicated work becomes possible when someone learns to stop waiting for the whole answer and make the next choice.
