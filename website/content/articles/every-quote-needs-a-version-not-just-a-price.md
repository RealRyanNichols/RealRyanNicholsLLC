A quote can be accurate when it is sent and wrong when the work begins.

The price did not necessarily change by itself.

The scope moved. The customer asked for one more thing. A material changed. Somebody promised a faster date. A verbal approval replaced the document everyone could see.

Then the job reaches delivery with three people holding three different versions of the truth.

The cure is not a longer disclaimer.

It is a versioned quote.

## A price is attached to a promise

A number by itself does not explain what the business agreed to do.

The promise includes scope, quantity, timing, assumptions, exclusions, and the conditions that can change the price.

When any of those move, the business needs a new version. Otherwise the last conversation silently overwrites the record.

Ryan put the operator’s version of this idea plainly in a recent public work note: “[The second job is already priced](https://realryannichols.com/posts/the-second-job-is-already-priced).”

That only stays useful if the next person can see which job, which configuration, and which price the record meant.

## Versioning prevents the polite argument

Most quote disputes do not begin with fraud.

They begin with memory.

The customer remembers the helpful sentence from the phone call. Sales remembers the original package. Operations sees the latest email. The invoice is generated from an old field in the CRM.

Everybody may be acting honestly.

They are still working from different records.

A version number gives the conversation a shared object. Quote Q-1042, version 3, is not “the latest thing we talked about.” It is a document with a date, owner, scope, and approval.

{{callout: key | If the price changed but the record did not, the argument has already started.}}

## The six fields every quote version needs

You do not need a giant proposal system to create a durable record.

Start with six fields.

### 1. Quote ID and version

Keep the quote ID stable and increment the version. Q-1042-V1 becomes Q-1042-V2.

Do not create an unrelated record every time the customer changes one detail. The version chain should remain visible.

### 2. Scope snapshot

List the exact product, service, quantity, location, configuration, and deliverables included in that version.

If a detail affects labor or materials, do not leave it inside a text thread.

### 3. Price and expiration

Record the price, tax treatment, deposit, payment schedule, and the date through which the quote remains valid.

An expiration date is not pressure. It tells both sides when changing costs require a fresh calculation.

### 4. Assumptions and exclusions

Name what the quote depends on and what it does not include.

Access, site condition, customer-supplied measurements, permit costs, revision count, delivery distance, and response deadlines are common sources of invisible scope.

### 5. Approval evidence

Record who accepted the version, when, and through what method.

An electronic signature is useful. A clear email approval may also be enough for the workflow. The important thing is that the accepted version can be retrieved later.

### 6. Change path

State what happens when the customer asks for something different.

The answer should not be “we will figure it out.” Name who prices the change, who approves it, and whether work pauses until approval.

## The accepted version should lock

Once a customer accepts a quote, do not edit that version in place.

Create a new version or a change order.

Editing the accepted record destroys the evidence of what both sides approved. It also makes the business unable to explain why the final invoice differs from the first number.

Good systems preserve the old version, link the new one, and identify what changed.

This matters even when the change benefits the customer. A free upgrade, waived fee, or earlier date is still a change to the promise. Operations needs to see it.

## Put the version into the handoff

The quote should not die when the deal becomes a job.

The accepted quote ID and version should travel into the work order, project, production ticket, delivery record, and invoice.

That one reference lets the team answer basic questions:

- What did the customer approve?
- Which price applies?
- What changed afterward?
- Who approved the change?
- Which version should billing use?

If those answers live only in the salesperson’s inbox, the CRM is storing contacts, not running a handoff.

## Run the five-quote audit

Open the last five quotes your business sent.

For each one, ask:

1. Can I identify the exact version the customer received?
2. Can I see the scope tied to the price?
3. Can I find the approval?
4. Can I see every change without rewriting history?
5. Does delivery know which version to build?

Any “no” is a process gap worth fixing before volume makes it expensive.
