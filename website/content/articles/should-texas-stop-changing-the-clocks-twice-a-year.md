Texas lawmakers have already said what they want: stop changing the clocks and keep daylight saving time all year.

The clocks still change.

That is not because the Texas law disappeared. It is because the state wrote a condition into it that only Congress can satisfy. The result is a useful policy argument hiding inside a simple complaint about losing or gaining an hour.

Should Texas stop the twice-yearly switch? If so, should the state keep the brighter evenings of daylight saving time or the earlier morning light of standard time?

## The verified facts

As verified on August 26, 2026, Texas [H.B. 1393](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/html/HB01393F.HTM) says the state shall observe daylight saving time year-round and call it “Texas Time.” The House passed the bill 133 to 11 in April 2025. The Senate passed it 26 to 5 in May.

But the enacted bill also says it takes effect only if Congress passes a law authorizing Texas to remain on daylight saving time year-round.

The current [U.S. Department of Transportation explanation of the Uniform Time Act](https://www.transportation.gov/regulations/time-act), updated July 15, 2026, says states may exempt themselves from daylight saving time and remain on standard time. States do not have authority to choose permanent daylight saving time. States that observe daylight saving time must use the federally mandated start and end dates.

The [National Institute of Standards and Technology](https://www.nist.gov/pml/time-and-frequency-division/local-time-faqs) says the switch back to standard time occurs on the first Sunday in November at 2 a.m. local time. In 2026, that means the next scheduled clock change is November 1.

So Texas has expressed a preference, but the legal trigger for year-round daylight saving time has not been met.

## The strongest case for ending the switch

The clearest argument is simplicity.

Twice a year, households, schools, workplaces, transportation systems, and software schedules have to absorb an artificial one-hour change. Many devices update themselves, but human bodies do not switch as cleanly.

The [CDC's workplace safety guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/bulletin/2022/time-change.html) says changes in light and time can influence the circadian system and disrupt sleep. It notes evidence of shortened sleep and adverse health and safety effects after the transition to daylight saving time.

Ending the switch would remove that recurring disruption. Parents would not have to reset young children's schedules twice a year. Shift workers would not have to navigate the repeated or missing hour. Businesses operating across locations would face one fewer calendar complication.

There is also a trust argument. Texas legislators took a lopsided vote in favor of a fixed clock. People can reasonably ask why a state decision about local time must wait on Washington.

## The strongest case for keeping seasonal time

The strongest defense of the current system is that it moves daylight toward the part of the day when more people can use it.

Summer daylight saving time creates later evening light for travel, recreation, shopping, and outdoor work after the normal business day. Winter standard time moves light back toward the morning, when students commute and many workers begin their day.

A single fixed clock cannot create more daylight. It only decides where the darkness falls.

Permanent daylight saving time would mean darker winter mornings. Permanent standard time would mean earlier summer sunsets by the clock. The seasonal switch is an attempt to split the difference, even if the transition itself is inconvenient.

There is also a coordination problem. Time zones are shared infrastructure. A Texas-only approach could affect transportation, broadcasting, national business, and people who cross state lines. Uniform federal rules reduce a different kind of friction.

The same tension appears in the debate over [later school start times in Texas](/posts/should-texas-require-middle-and-high-schools-to-start-at-830-or-later). The clock is not just a number. Morning light, sleep, transportation, family schedules, and local control all collide in the same decision.

## Ryan's Take: end the switch, but name the tradeoff

**Editorial analysis:** Texas should stop changing the clocks twice a year, but “stop changing” is not a complete policy.

The state chose permanent daylight saving time because later evening light is popular and economically useful. That preference is understandable. It also carries a real cost in winter morning darkness. Supporters should say that plainly.

Permanent standard time has a cleaner path under existing federal law and aligns morning light more closely with the body's clock. It also gives up late summer light by the clock. Supporters of that option should say that plainly too.

The wrong way to settle this is to promise that one fixed time improves every part of the year for everybody. It cannot.

The right question is which cost Texas would rather carry: two annual disruptions, darker winter mornings, or earlier summer evenings.

Good policy begins when the tradeoff is visible. That is also the lesson in [Protect the First Hour Before the World Claims It](/posts/protect-the-first-hour-before-the-world-claims-it). Time cannot be expanded. It can only be assigned on purpose.

## What should happen next

Congress could authorize states to select permanent daylight saving time, which would activate the choice Texas already made. Texas could also reconsider permanent standard time, a choice current federal law allows states to make.

Either path should include a clear implementation date, coordination with neighboring states and major transportation systems, and enough lead time for schools, hospitals, employers, and software providers to adjust.

The public deserves something more precise than “keep more sunshine.” No law moves the sun. A law only moves the clock.
