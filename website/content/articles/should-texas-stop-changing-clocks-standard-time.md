Texas has already said what it wants.

In 2025, lawmakers passed a bill directing the state to use daylight saving time year-round and call it “Texas Time.” The vote was not close.

There was one condition.

Congress had to authorize states to stay on daylight saving time permanently.

As of October 4, 2026, that federal permission has not changed the rule described by the U.S. Department of Transportation. Texas still changes clocks with the rest of the participating states.

That leaves a real question.

Should Texas keep waiting for permanent daylight saving time, or stop changing the clocks by choosing permanent standard time, which federal law already allows?

## What the law says now

The [U.S. Department of Transportation’s current Uniform Time page](https://www.transportation.gov/regulations/time-act?os=os) says states may exempt themselves from observing daylight saving time. A state that does observe daylight saving time must use the federally mandated start and end dates.

The same page says states do not have authority to choose permanent daylight saving time on their own.

Texas [House Bill 1393](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/html/HB01393F.HTM), enrolled in 2025, directs Texas to observe daylight saving time year-round only if Congress enacts a law authorizing it. The official text records House passage by 133 to 11 and Senate passage by 26 to 5.

The legal structure is not symmetrical.

Texas can choose year-round standard time under current federal law. It cannot activate its chosen year-round daylight time without federal action.

## The strongest case for waiting

Permanent daylight saving time gives people more evening light by the clock.

Supporters can reasonably prefer that for after-work activity, youth sports, shopping, outdoor recreation, and the simple feeling that the usable day lasts longer. Texas lawmakers made that preference clear in the 2025 bill.

Waiting also avoids locking the state into the opposite choice just because Congress has not acted yet. If Texas adopts permanent standard time and federal law changes later, the state could end up reopening the issue, changing systems again, and asking schools, employers, transportation networks, and software providers to absorb another transition.

Uniformity matters too. Texas businesses interact with customers, suppliers, airports, television schedules, financial markets, and remote workers across state lines. A different rule from neighboring states can move confusion from two clock-change days into months of scheduling differences.

The strongest case for waiting is not love of changing clocks.

It is that Texas already chose the version it wants and should not accept the version it rejected merely because federal law offers it sooner.

## The strongest case for permanent standard time

The current compromise guarantees the one thing almost everybody says they dislike: changing the clocks twice a year.

Permanent standard time would end that immediately if Texas enacted it under the existing federal option. Morning light would arrive earlier by the clock than under permanent daylight time. People who prioritize school mornings, early shifts, farming, construction, or morning routines may see that as the safer alignment.

There is also a question of honesty.

If the real public priority is ending the switch, the state already has a legal path. If the real priority is keeping later evening light, then Texans should say they are willing to continue the switch until Congress permits that specific result.

Those are different goals.

Policy gets muddy when they are treated as the same sentence.

## Ryan’s Take

Texas should stop pretending that “end the clock change” settles the argument.

It does not.

The state has to choose which daylight pattern it wants to make permanent. Later evening light and earlier morning light cannot both occupy the same hour.

My take is that Texas should hold a fresh, plain-language debate between the two real options available to it:

1. Keep the current seasonal change while waiting for Congress to allow permanent daylight saving time.
2. Use the authority Texas already has and move to permanent standard time.

The debate should show sunrise and sunset examples for different parts of Texas across winter and summer. It should hear from schools, hospitals, transportation operators, small businesses, parents, shift workers, and people who work outside.

Then lawmakers should choose in public.

Not “Texas Time.”

Not “stop changing the clocks.”

Choose the actual morning and evening tradeoff.
