*By the Real Ryan Nichols Editorial Team*

Texas has put a checkpoint at the entrance to the app store.

Under Senate Bill 2420, the App Store Accountability Act, an app-store operator must use a commercially reasonable method to verify the age category of a person creating an account in Texas. The statute sorts users into four groups: under 13, 13 to 15, 16 to 17, and adult.

If the user is a minor, the account must be linked to a verified parent or guardian. The parent receives information about an app's age rating and must approve a minor's download or purchase.

The law took effect January 1, 2026. Its enforcement has been fought through federal court. A district judge blocked it in December 2025. The Fifth Circuit later stayed that injunction, and on July 6, 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court denied two emergency requests to lift the stay. That means Texas can enforce the law while the constitutional case continues. The Supreme Court did not decide that the law is constitutional.

That last distinction matters. The immediate rule is in force. The larger legal argument is not finished.

## What the Texas law actually requires

The enrolled text does more than ask whether somebody is over 18.

App stores must determine an age category when an account is created. Minor accounts must be affiliated with parent accounts. Developers must provide age ratings and disclose the reasons for those ratings. App stores pass age-category and consent information to developers, while both app stores and developers face restrictions on how information collected under the law may be used or disclosed.

The law also requires renewed parental consent for certain significant app changes, including some changes to data practices, age ratings, or monetization features.

Supporters see a chain of responsibility. Critics see a chain of identity and consent data touching more companies.

The law applies to app stores, not every website. Its practical reach is still wide because phones and tablets have become the front door to news, education, banking, communication, entertainment, and political participation.

## The strongest case for the law

Parents are expected to protect children in a marketplace designed to make downloading frictionless.

Many apps are free at the point of download but collect data, sell upgrades, present advertising, or open social features. A child can move from a colorful icon to a complicated agreement without understanding what was accepted.

Supporters argue that the app store is the most efficient place to establish an age signal and connect a minor to a parent. Instead of forcing each developer to build a separate age-check system, the platform that distributes the apps can create a common gate.

The parental-approval step also gives families information before the download, not after a problem appears. Age ratings and explanations can help a parent distinguish a calculator from a public chat room, even when both are one tap away.

Texas also built data-use limits into the statute. The law says information collected for compliance may not be used for another purpose, and it restricts disclosure. Supporters can reasonably argue that a regulated, limited system is better than the scattered and often invisible data collection already happening across apps.

In plain language, the case is this: children do not enter contracts, data markets, and unlimited content systems on the same footing as adults. Parents need a meaningful switch, not a settings page buried six menus deep.

## The strongest case against it

The law begins with every account holder, including adults.

An age category cannot be assigned without some method of assurance. Depending on the implementation, that can mean account history, payment information, identity documents, facial estimation, third-party verification, or another data signal. The statute says the method must be commercially reasonable, but it does not make the privacy risk disappear.

Critics also argue that the app store is not just a store. It distributes tools for speech. A minor may use apps to read news, organize a school group, contact public officials, make art, practice faith, or communicate with friends. Requiring parental approval for each download can burden access to protected expression, especially when the parent refuses for reasons unrelated to safety.

There is a family-autonomy argument too. Some parents may want a blanket approval for ordinary apps. The law chooses a transaction-by-transaction model and backs it with state enforcement.

Developers carry duties as well. Smaller companies have to assign ratings, react to consent signals, issue notices for significant changes, and protect compliance data. Large platforms can absorb that machinery more easily than a small developer can.

Finally, data minimization matters. A rule meant to protect children can create a valuable new map of who is a child, who is their parent, and which apps were approved. Legal restrictions help, but no database is made harmless by good intentions.

## Editorial analysis: protect minors without building an identity warehouse

The purpose is legitimate. The implementation deserves relentless scrutiny.

Parents should have clear, enforceable control over a minor's downloads and purchases. Developers should not be able to hide the real nature of an app behind a cheerful icon. Age ratings should explain what they are measuring.

But Texas should demand proof that compliance systems collect the least information possible. An app developer usually needs to know an age category and whether consent exists. It does not need a copy of a driver's license or the underlying evidence used to establish that category.

The best system would verify the attribute, share only the result, retain as little as possible, and give users a clear way to correct errors. Independent security testing and public reporting should be part of enforcement. A privacy promise without a deletion rule and an audit trail is only marketing.

The same ownership question appears whenever a platform becomes a gatekeeper. [If you cannot export it, you do not own it](https://realryannichols.com/posts/if-i-cannot-export-it-i-do-not-own-it). If you cannot understand or challenge the identity decision controlling your account, you do not have meaningful control there either.

Transparency should apply to the policy debate too. An emergency order is not a final constitutional ruling. A child-safety goal is not proof that every mechanism is narrow. A privacy risk is not proof that parents should receive no stronger tools.

Texas can demand responsibility from app stores and still demand restraint from the age-verification industry. Those obligations belong together.

## The question

Should Texas require app stores to verify every user's age category and obtain parental approval for minors, or should the state pursue a narrower system that does not place every account behind an age check?
