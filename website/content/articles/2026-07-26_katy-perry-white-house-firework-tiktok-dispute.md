*By the Real Ryan Nichols Editorial Team*

The official White House TikTok account posted a video over the weekend pairing footage described by multiple outlets as U.S. military strikes with Katy Perry's 2010 song "Firework," captioned "Iran has been warned." Perry responded directly on X on Saturday, July 25, and her statement is now the center of the conversation, not the video itself.

This is not a one-off. It follows a similar dispute the White House had with Sabrina Carpenter in December 2025 over unauthorized music use — a pattern worth watching, not just a single flare-up.

## What Perry actually said

Perry's post is public and verifiable at [x.com/katyperry/status/2081032309508047136](https://x.com/katyperry/status/2081032309508047136). The quoted statement, confirmed across [Variety](https://variety.com/), [TMZ](https://www.tmz.com/), [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/), [The Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/), [The Hill](https://thehill.com/) and [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/):

> "I am deeply appalled and angry to see 'Firework' used on the @WhiteHouse TikTok account as a backing track for video footage of military strikes. I did not approve this, I was not asked, and I absolutely do not condone it. I wrote this song to be an anthem of hope, healing, and inner strength for people going through their darkest personal moments. To see a message of self-worth and upliftment weaponized to soundtrack destruction and violence is a complete violation of everything my song stands for."

That's a direct, on-the-record statement from the artist herself — not a rumor, not a secondhand account. I'm quoting it in full because paraphrasing something this specific risks losing the actual claim she's making: lack of consent, and a values conflict with how the song was used.

Reporting varies on exactly what the strike footage showed beyond "military strikes" tied to Iran coverage — some outlets describe it more specifically, some more generally. I'm sticking with what the White House's own caption said ("Iran has been warned") rather than guessing at details that aren't consistently confirmed across sources.

## Why the reaction split the way it did

One camp is squarely behind Perry: an artist's work getting used without permission — especially to score violence when the song was written as the opposite — is a legitimate objection regardless of politics. Copyright and consent don't stop applying because the user is a government account.

The other camp is mocking the objection, with some commentary (Gateway Pundit-style pages among them) framing it as ironic that an artist named the song "Firework" is upset about it soundtracking things that explode.

Both reactions are talking past each other. One is about consent and control over your own work. The other is about the literal content of the lyrics. Those aren't actually the same argument, and conflating them is how these disputes turn into noise instead of a real conversation about musicians' rights versus how government accounts use viral audio.

## The pattern worth watching

Sabrina Carpenter in December, Katy Perry in July — that's twice in eight months. If this keeps happening, the real story stops being about any single artist and starts being about whether official government accounts are treating popular music as free-to-use meme material. That's a licensing and consent question that outlasts any one news cycle.
