Texas can let you renew a driver license online, update an existing voter registration online, and check whether you are registered online.

For most people registering to vote for the first time, the last step still happens on paper.

That creates a real policy question as the November election approaches: Should Texas build a system that lets eligible new voters complete the entire registration process online?

Reasonable people can disagree. Access matters. Verification matters. Security matters. So does the basic question of whether a form started online should still have to pass through a printer and mailbox.

## The verified facts

As verified on August 31, 2026, the [Texas Secretary of State](https://www.sos.state.tx.us/elections/voter/reqvr.shtml) offers an online form that an applicant can fill out, but the applicant is not registered until the form is printed and mailed to the county voter registrar.

In a January 28, 2026 [election reminder](https://www.sos.state.tx.us/about/newsreleases/2026/012926.shtml), the Secretary of State again said a voter registration application must be printed, signed, and mailed. The same notice said Texans who have moved or changed their name can update an existing registration online through Texas.gov.

There is also a limited online path tied to the Department of Public Safety. Texas has long allowed eligible people to register while completing certain driver license or identification transactions online. That is different from a general online registration portal open to every eligible applicant.

The [National Conference of State Legislatures' Texas profile](https://documents.ncsl.org/wwwncsl/Elections/state-profiles/Texas%20Profile_Mar4.pdf) lists Texas as not providing general online voter registration and reports that 42 states and the District of Columbia do.

None of that settles what Texas should do. It establishes where the state stands.

## The strongest case for fully online registration

Supporters begin with a simple point: the printer and postage do not prove eligibility.

They create an extra handoff.

A person can complete every field correctly, believe the process is finished, and still fail to register because the form was never printed, signed, mailed, delivered, or processed before the deadline. That burden can fall hardest on younger voters, people who move often, residents without printers, Texans working long hours, and people far from a registration office.

A secure online system could validate required fields before submission, provide a confirmation, reduce unreadable handwriting, and give election officials structured data instead of another paper form to enter manually.

The national comparison matters too. Most states have found a way to offer online registration while keeping paper available for people who cannot or do not want to use it.

The strongest argument is not that election systems are risk-free. It is that Texas already manages sensitive government transactions online and can design a registration portal with identity checks, logging, monitoring, and a clear paper alternative.

## The strongest case for keeping the current process

Opponents do not have to oppose voter registration to question another internet-facing election system.

Voter files contain personal information and support every later stage of election administration. A flawed identity check, software error, denial-of-service attack, phishing campaign, or compromised account could create confusion close to a deadline and put county officials under enormous pressure.

The federal [Election Assistance Commission](https://www.eac.gov/election-officials/election-security-preparedness) publishes recommended security controls for voter registration systems because the threat is real. Adding a public submission portal expands the surface that must be defended, audited, funded, and explained.

Texas also has 254 counties with different staffing and technical capacity. A statewide portal would need reliable integration, clear ownership when records do not match, accessible alternatives, and a recovery plan when the system is unavailable.

The strongest opposition is not that paper is magically secure. It is that convenience should not be introduced faster than the state can verify identities, protect data, support counties, and preserve a trustworthy record of every submission.

## Ryan's Take: build it, test it, and keep the paper door open

**Editorial analysis:** Texas should offer fully online voter registration, but only through a system built around verification and public accountability.

The state should start with applicants who can be matched to a Texas driver license or identification record. It should require strong identity checks, provide an immediate timestamped receipt, let applicants track the status of their submission, and give county registrars a clear process for resolving mismatches.

Independent security testing should happen before launch and after major changes. Texas should publish uptime, rejection, correction, and processing-time reports without exposing personal data. Paper registration should remain available and should not become a second-class option.

If the state cannot explain who owns a failed submission, how an applicant proves it was sent, and how counties recover from an outage, the system is not ready.

That is the same accountability standard argued in [Should Texas Publish Job and Wage Results for Every Workforce Grant?](/posts/should-texas-publish-job-and-wage-results-for-every-workforce-grant). Build the service, then publish enough evidence for Texans to judge whether it works.
