Ferrari just unveiled its first fully electric car.

It is called the Luce. It costs €550,000 — roughly $600,000 USD. It was designed in collaboration with Jony Ive's LoveFrom collective, the same creative agency behind some of Apple's most elegant products.

And the moment X laid eyes on it, the verdict came back fast and brutal: *"It looks like a Nissan."*

I want to sit with that for a second. Because it tells us something important — not just about a car, but about what happens when legacy brands decide their identity is a problem to be solved.

{{share}}

## What They Built

The Ferrari Luce is, by most technical descriptions, a beautiful piece of engineering. It is smooth. It is glassy. The lines are clean and restrained. Jony Ive's fingerprints are everywhere — the same minimalist philosophy that gave us the original iMac and the iPhone's early design language.

There is no question that the Luce is a *sophisticated* object.

The question is whether it is a *Ferrari*.

The answer X gave was: no.

And I think X got this one right.

## The Identity Problem Nobody Talked About Before Launch

Ferrari's brand is not just a car company. It is an emotion. It is a sound. It is a silhouette you can identify at 200 yards. The aggressive haunches, the low snarl, the feeling that this machine was made to devour pavement and intimidate everything around it.

That brand took decades to build. Multiple generations of engineers and designers poured themselves into creating something that *screamed* Ferrari before it ever left the showroom floor.

The Luce whispers.

Former Italian industry minister Carlo Calenda did not hold back. He called the car *"an aesthetic and technological insult to anyone who loves Ferrari."*

That is a direct quote. And while it is harsh, it captures exactly what hundreds of thousands of replies on X were saying in less formal terms.

When the most recognizable sports car brand in the world releases a vehicle that gets compared — repeatedly, publicly, with screenshots being shared across the platform — to a mid-range Japanese sedan, something has gone wrong.

## What I Think Happened Here

I think Ferrari made the same mistake that legacy institutions keep making: they confused *evolution* with *erasure.*

Evolution says: we are going to electrify our lineup while keeping the soul of what makes a Ferrari a Ferrari.

Erasure says: we hired a design agency known for tech minimalism to reimagine our entire visual identity for a new era.

Those are two completely different decisions. Ferrari chose the second one.

You can argue that the future of performance cars is electric. You can argue that Ferrari had no choice but to eventually go fully electric. Both of those arguments have merit. I am not anti-EV. What I am saying is that the way you enter the electric era matters enormously when your entire brand equity is built on emotional resonance.

The Porsche Taycan maintained Porsche's visual DNA while going electric. People recognized it immediately as a Porsche. That is evolution.

The Ferrari Luce, in its current form, asks you to trust that the Ferrari badge is enough.

Six hundred thousand dollars says the badge better carry some weight.

## The Jony Ive Factor

Here is the part that is most interesting to me.

Jony Ive is brilliant. His work at Apple fundamentally reshaped how we think about consumer products. He understood that objects have emotional weight, that form communicates function, that people bond with well-designed things.

But Ive's design language — clean, geometric, glass-smooth, minimal — is Apple's language. It is *tech's* language.

Ferrari's language is aggression, curves, and barely contained power.

When you hand one language to someone fluent in the other, you sometimes get a translation error.

What X is reacting to is not ignorance. It is not people being resistant to change. It is people having a visceral response to a translation error worth €550,000.

## The Broader Lesson

Every brand that has existed long enough eventually faces a version of this moment.

You built your identity on something real — something people connected with emotionally. Then the industry shifts, the technology changes, the market demands something different. And you have to decide: how much of yourself do you preserve, and how much do you sacrifice at the altar of relevance?

The brands that navigate this well tend to do it the same way: they modernize the mechanics while protecting the soul.

The brands that blow it tend to do the opposite: they protect the mechanics (or in this case, the price point and the badge) while replacing the soul with someone else's aesthetic.

A $600,000 Nissan — as the internet keeps calling it — is what happens when you get that equation backward.

Ferrari is going to sell these cars. There is no shortage of ultra-wealthy buyers who will own a Luce purely because it carries the prancing horse on the hood.

But this launch will be remembered as the moment Ferrari's loyal faithful stood up on X and collectively said: *"That's not ours."*
