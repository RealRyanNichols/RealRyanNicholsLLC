X Corp. filed suit against New York this month, naming Attorney General Letitia James, over a law with a title built for headlines: the "Stop Hiding Hate Act."

Strip the branding off and here's what the law, S895B, actually does. Signed by Governor Kathy Hochul in December 2024, it requires social media companies that pull in more than $100 million in annual revenue to file public reports twice a year on how they moderate five categories of content: hate speech, extremism, disinformation or misinformation, harassment, and foreign political interference.

X's complaint argues that requirement crosses a constitutional line — that forcing a private company to publicly account for its editorial decisions about speech is itself government interference with speech, not a neutral disclosure rule. The company also argues it conflicts with Section 230, the 1996 federal law that lets platforms moderate content as they see fit without the government dictating the process. [CBS New York covered the filing and what it targets.](https://www.cbsnews.com/newyork/news/x-sues-new-york-over-social-media-law/)

## This isn't X's first time making this argument — and it already won once

X's complaint calls New York's law a "carbon copy" of a nearly identical 2022 California law. That's not spin. It's a fair description, and it matters, because a federal appeals court already ruled on that one: in September 2024, the Ninth Circuit found California's content-moderation disclosure requirements violated the First Amendment.

That's the precedent X is walking into this fight with. Not a theory. A ruling.

## Why I think the framing matters more than the politics

I'd bet most people's gut reaction to this story splits along whether they like Elon Musk or not, and I think that reaction misses the actual question the law raises.

Set aside the specific company. Ask yourself: should a state government be able to require *any* private publisher — a newspaper, a magazine, a platform — to file biannual public reports justifying its editorial decisions about which categories of speech it allows or restricts? Not "should platforms moderate content" — they clearly do, constantly, and that's their right. The question is narrower and sharper: should the *government* get to compel a report card on how a private company exercises that editorial judgment, in categories the government itself defines?

Framed that way, this stops being an X story and becomes a press-freedom story. If a state can mandate that kind of disclosure from a social platform because it decided "hate speech" and "disinformation" are reportable categories, there's no clean line stopping a future legislature from deciding some other category of speech — political commentary, religious speech, whatever's unpopular that decade — deserves the same treatment. The compelled-disclosure mechanism doesn't care what content it gets pointed at next.

That's the part of "Stop Hiding Hate" that should give people pause regardless of what they think of X, Musk, or any platform's current moderation choices. A tool built to regulate one company's speech decisions today is a tool built to regulate speech decisions, period.

## Where this goes next

New York isn't backing down just because a lawsuit landed — the law reportedly stayed in effect while the case proceeds, the same posture California took before its version got struck down. Expect X to lean hard on that Ninth Circuit ruling as the case moves through the courts. If history repeats, this ends the way the California fight did.

I deal in records on this site, and I think this is a record worth watching closely — not because of who's suing, but because of what wins if the lawsuit fails. A state successfully compelling a platform to report on its speech decisions is a precedent that outlives this administration, this platform, and this news cycle.
