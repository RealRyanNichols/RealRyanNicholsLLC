On July 6, in the 31st minute of the United States' World Cup Round of 16 match against Belgium, Malik Tillman bent a free kick into the net at Lumen Field in Seattle.

The stadium didn't just roar. It moved the earth.

The Pacific Northwest Seismic Network — the agency that actually monitors earthquakes and volcanoes in Washington and Oregon — had a station running near the stadium as an experiment for the tournament. When Tillman's goal hit the back of the net, the needle jumped. A real, measurable "fanquake," the kind of crowd-generated seismic spike more associated with Seahawks playoff games than a soccer friendly. This wasn't a metaphor. Scientists were tracking it in real time.

For a few minutes, that goal was the biggest thing on X. A nation co-hosting its own World Cup, tied 1-1 with Belgium, on a golazo from a 22-year-old, and the ground itself said so.

Then the game kept going.

## What happened after the earthquake

Belgium answered fast — Charles De Ketelaere scored again just 52 seconds after Tillman's equalizer. A miscue from goalkeeper Matt Freese made it 3-1. Romelu Lukaku added a fourth late. Final score: Belgium 4, USA 1. The co-hosts were out of their own tournament in the round of 16.

Christian Pulisic, the closest thing the USMNT has to a face of the program, had a rough night before it ended early. He lost the ball repeatedly in the first half, got caught by a hard challenge, tried to play through it, and was subbed off with an ankle injury before the hour mark. Cameras caught him emotional on the bench, watching his team's tournament end without him on the field to try to fix it.

The internet's verdict arrived before he'd finished limping to the sideline. "Christian Pulisic with a hall of shame performance." "What is Pulisic even doing?" Carli Lloyd, on the Fox broadcast, said she was "disappointed" in him — not just for the game, but for the whole tournament.

## Two true things, thirty minutes apart

Here's what strikes me about July 6: the same country that felt one unified jolt of joy at 9:31 p.m. was hunting for someone to blame by 10:00.

Both reactions are real. The fanquake wasn't manufactured — it's the most literal proof of collective feeling I've ever seen, a stadium's emotion registering on scientific equipment. The frustration with the loss is real too. You don't get to host a World Cup and lose in the round of 16 without hearing about it.

But watch how fast the crowd went from celebrating a 22-year-old's moment of magic to piling onto a player who was visibly hurt, trying to play through it, in the biggest match of his career, on his own country's soil. Pulisic didn't get a news cycle of grace before the pile-on started. He got about 45 minutes.

I write often about what it's like to be on the other end of a pile-on — to have a moment defined by the worst 90 seconds of it rather than the whole picture. I'm not saying Pulisic's performance was above criticism. Analysis is fine. Fair critique of a bad night is fine. What I'm flagging is the speed and the totality of it — the jump from "rough half" to "hall of shame" to writing off his entire tournament, delivered before we even knew how serious the injury was.

A player can be struggling and hurting at the same time. Those two facts don't cancel each other out, and they don't deserve the same tone of contempt reserved for someone who simply doesn't care.

## What I'd want people to sit with

The fanquake is the story I want to remember from July 6th. A goal so good, celebrated by so many people at once, that geologists have the data. That's a genuinely great, unifying American sports moment, and it deserves more oxygen than it's getting.

The instinct to torch Pulisic by the second half is the story I want us to be a little more careful with next time. Ask what you know before you decide what someone deserves to hear about themselves. Wait for the medical update. Separate the performance critique from the personal one.
