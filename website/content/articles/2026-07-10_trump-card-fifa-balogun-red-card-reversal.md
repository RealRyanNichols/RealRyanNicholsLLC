On July 1, USMNT striker Folarin Balogun stepped on the foot of Bosnian defender Tarik Muharemovic and got sent off. Red card, automatic one-match suspension, done — that is how the rule has worked since FIFA started enforcing it.

Except this time it did not stay done.

President Trump called FIFA president Gianni Infantino directly. Four days later, FIFA reversed the suspension — the first time the governing body has lifted a red-card ban during a World Cup since 1962. Balogun was back on the field against Belgium.

Then Trump posted this:

https://x.com/FabrizioRomano/status/2073828193568436595

*"Thank you to FIFA for doing what was right, and reversing a great injustice!"*

Within hours, "The Trump Card" was everywhere on X — some of it admiring, most of it furious.

## What Actually Happened

US Soccer's legal team built their appeal around the use of slow-motion replay in the original ejection call — a legitimate technical argument teams make all the time. Most of those appeals fail. This one did not, and it did not fail on the strength of the legal argument alone. It failed because the President of the United States picked up the phone.

Belgium's opposition Socialist party did not mince words: *"Shame on you! When money calls the shots, the World Cup loses all credibility."* Other reactions online called it the most corrupt decision in World Cup history.

## Why This Matters Past the Scoreline

I did not sit down to write about a soccer rule. I sat down to write about what happens when the people who are supposed to enforce a rule fold the moment someone with enough power leans on them.

That is not a soccer problem. That is a *everywhere* problem.

Institutions exist to apply standards evenly — to the powerful and the powerless the same way. The minute an institution proves it will bend for one phone call from the right person, it has told everyone else exactly what its rules are actually worth: nothing, unless you cannot get someone powerful on the line.

I have spent the last several years watching institutions decide, case by case, who gets due process and who does not, who gets the benefit of the doubt and who gets the headline first and the hearing later. So forgive me if a FIFA reversal that required a presidential phone call does not read to me as a win for fairness. It reads as a receipt. Now everyone knows exactly what it costs to get a ruling reversed at the highest level of the sport: not a better legal argument, but a bigger name.

Was Balogun's red card genuinely a bad call? Maybe. Officials get calls wrong constantly, and instant replay exists because human eyes miss things. If the underlying call was wrong, it should have been overturned on the merits — through the same appeals process every other team and player is stuck with. What actually got him back on the field was not the merits. It was the phone call.
