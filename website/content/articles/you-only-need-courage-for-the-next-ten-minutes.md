Some mornings feel heavy before your feet reach the floor.

There is too much to fix. Too many calls. Too many unanswered questions. Too much distance between where you are and where you thought you would be.

Then your mind makes the day even heavier. It asks you to carry every hour at once.

You do not have to.

You do not need courage for the whole day. You need enough courage for the next ten honest minutes.

{{callout: pull | You do not have to feel ready for the whole day. You only have to stop abandoning the next ten minutes.}}

## The whole day is an unfair assignment

Fear likes to speak in totals.

It shows you the entire debt, the whole project, every strained conversation, and all the ways the plan could fail. It stacks tomorrow on top of today and then asks why you are not moving.

That is not a useful measurement.

Nobody lives an entire day in one moment. You live this breath, this decision, this call, this glass of water, this page, this mile.

The next ten minutes will not solve everything. They can do something more important: they can interrupt the freeze.

## Courage can be small and still be real

Courage is often pictured as one dramatic decision made without hesitation.

Real courage is usually less polished.

It is opening the message you have avoided. It is admitting that you do not know. It is asking one safe person to stay on the phone. It is putting the shoes on. It is writing the first sentence badly enough that the second sentence can exist.

The action does not have to look impressive. It has to be honest.

If you are exhausted, your next ten minutes may be taking medicine as directed, eating something, or moving to a safer room. If you are behind, it may be naming the missed deadline before somebody has to chase you. If you are overwhelmed, it may be making a list with only three lines.

Small is not the same as fake.

## The ten-minute courage plan

Set a timer for ten minutes. Do not use the timer to punish yourself or race. Use it to shrink the assignment.

### Minute one: name the truth

Write one sentence:

**The thing I am most tempted to avoid right now is ________.**

Be precise. “My life” is too large. “Calling the utility company” is workable. “Everything at work” is too large. “Sending the corrected estimate” is workable.

### Minutes two and three: choose the smallest honest move

Ask what would count as contact with the problem.

You may not be able to finish the application, but you can open it and find the missing document. You may not be ready for the entire conversation, but you can send a message asking for a time to talk. You may not be able to clean the whole room, but you can clear the doorway and one chair.

The move should be small enough to begin now and real enough to matter.

### Minutes four through eight: do only that move

Do not redesign your life. Do not research twelve better systems. Do not stop halfway through to judge whether you should have started sooner.

Make contact with the work.

Dial the number. Open the document. Fill the bottle. Put the appointment on the calendar. Carry the trash outside. Send the honest sentence.

Stay with the chosen move until the timer reaches eight minutes.

### Minute nine: leave a marker

Write down what changed.

- The form is open.
- The voicemail is left.
- The question is written.
- The first box is packed.
- The help request is sent.

This is evidence. Your fear said you could not move. The marker shows that you did.

### Minute ten: choose the next checkpoint

Decide when you will return.

Maybe you keep going. Maybe you take a responsible break and return at 9:30. Maybe the next step depends on somebody else answering. Name the checkpoint instead of leaving the work in a fog.

## Asking for help counts as movement

Sometimes the smallest honest move is not solitary.

You may need information, accountability, transportation, professional help, or one calm person who can hear the truth without turning it into a spectacle.

Asking for help is not handing somebody your entire life. It can be specific:

- “Can you sit with me while I make this call?”
- “Can you tell me which document comes first?”
- “I am not doing well. Can you check on me at noon?”
- “I need professional help with this. Can you help me find the right place to start?”

The request is stronger when it names the need and the next checkpoint.

## Do not use ten minutes to lie to yourself

This plan is not permission to avoid the rest forever.

Ten minutes should create contact, clarity, or a safe pause. It should not become a ritual that lets you circle the same problem without touching it.

When the timer ends, tell the truth about what remains. If the problem is urgent, dangerous, or beyond your ability, escalate it. Call the professional. Tell the supervisor. Reach the safe person. Use the emergency resource.

Courage includes knowing when the next necessary thing is bigger than a private promise.

## The day can arrive one piece at a time

You may still feel afraid after ten minutes.

That does not erase the work.

You made contact with something real. You replaced one blurry threat with one defined next step. You gave the day a checkpoint instead of a verdict.

When the whole day feels impossible, stop trying to carry the whole day.

Take the next ten minutes back.
