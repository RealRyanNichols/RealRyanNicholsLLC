Andy Serkis directed an animated adaptation of *Animal Farm*. It stars Seth Rogen, Glenn Close, Steve Buscemi, Kieran Culkin, Woody Harrelson, Gaten Matarazzo, and Iman Vellani. It releases in theaters on July 11, 2026.

https://x.com/ToonHive/status/1914805607984488489

And in the days since the first real reactions started circulating, it has managed something I genuinely cannot remember another movie pulling off recently: it made everyone angry, for completely different reasons, at the same time.

## Two Furious Camps, Zero Overlap

One camp says the film waters down Orwell's original politics. One post that captured this side of the reaction directly accused the movie of cynically inserting "woke politics in place of Orwell's original thesis," reducing a sharp political allegory about totalitarianism to a question of "what if pigs could talk."

https://x.com/RayKump/status/2050658401860845583

The other camp isn't arguing about the politics at all — they're reacting to the film's bizarre theatrical gimmick. According to reporting on the release, the movie ends with an on-screen QR code asking viewers to leave a review and, separately, offering to let viewers donate up to $15,000 to buy tickets for other people. That's not a marketing beat anyone asked for. That's a fourth-wall break most audiences will find genuinely strange sitting in a theater.

Put those two complaints side by side and you get a film being criticized simultaneously for being too soft on its politics and too weird about its business model. Those are not the same complaint. They don't even live in the same conversation. And yet X is having both arguments about the same movie, often in the same threads, talking past each other.

One X user summed up the whole phenomenon better than any review I've read:

https://x.com/maenadea/status/2050640330450518367

"There's something sort of weirdly compelling about making an Animal Farm movie that somehow managed to make *everyone* mad."

## When Everyone's Mad, What Does That Actually Tell You?

Here's my honest read: a piece of art that makes one side furious usually tells you which side it picked. A piece of art that makes every side furious, for unrelated reasons, usually tells you it's confused about what it's trying to be — not that it's bravely offending everyone in service of some unified point.

Making the left mad and the right mad isn't automatically a badge of balance. Sometimes it just means nobody involved in the adaptation agreed on why they were adapting the book in the first place, and the seams show once it's in front of an audience deciding what to be mad about.

That doesn't mean the film is bad — I haven't seen it, and neither has most of X. It means the reaction itself is more interesting right now than the movie is, which is its own kind of warning sign for anyone who greenlit this.

## The Part That Actually Matters: Manufactured Outrage vs. Real Reaction

This is where I want to be careful, because "everyone's mad online" gets treated as a single phenomenon when it's usually two very different things stacked on top of each other.

Some of this backlash is a real, substantive critique — people who read the book, understand what Orwell was doing with the Soviet allegory, and have a specific, arguable complaint about the adaptation softening it. That's legitimate film criticism, and it deserves to be engaged with on the merits.

And some of it is the internet doing what the internet does with anything unfamiliar: reacting to a five-second clip of a QR code and a $15,000 donation prompt with the kind of instant, context-free outrage that spreads faster than anyone bothering to watch the actual film. That's not criticism. That's the same reflex that turns a joke into a conspiracy theory or a crossed arm into a national incident — decide first, gather context never.

The trouble is those two reactions look identical in a quote-tweet. Both show up as anger. Both get treated by the algorithm as engagement. And by the time anyone sorts out which complaints are substantive and which are just noise, the movie's actual merits are buried under both.

## What I'll Actually Be Watching For

Not whether people are mad — they clearly are, from multiple directions, before most of them have seen it. I'll be watching whether the reviews that come out after July 11, from people who actually sat through the film, land anywhere close to the pre-release outrage. If they don't, that tells you something about how much of this was ever about the movie at all.
