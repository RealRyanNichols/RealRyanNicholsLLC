Ryan Nichols has spent years building things in public: businesses, a case archive, articles, technology projects, and a record of a life that cannot be summarized by its hardest chapters.

Public rebuilding can become noisy. Every post can feel like it needs to prove something. Every unfinished task can begin to look like a verdict. Every outside opinion can compete for attention that is already stretched thin.

That is why the next part of rebuilding may need some places where nothing is being performed.

The outdoors does not have to become another brand, achievement, or dramatic transformation story. It can simply be a setting where Ryan—or anyone carrying a difficult season—steps away from the feed long enough to decide what deserves attention next.

This is not a claim that a lake, trail, fishing rod, or sunrise cures trauma, depression, anxiety, exhaustion, or any other condition. It does not replace medical care, counseling, medication, faith community, or trusted human support. It is a smaller and more honest idea: a safe outdoor routine can provide movement, fewer digital interruptions, and a physical place to practice paying attention.

## The public theme Ryan has already established

Ryan's published writing repeatedly returns to water and quiet as places for perspective. [The Water Doesn't Care What They Said About You](/posts/the-water-doesnt-care) and [I Go to the Water When the Noise Gets Loud](/posts/i-go-to-the-water-when-the-noise-gets-loud) are public expressions of that theme.

This article does not add a new private story to them. It does not claim Ryan took a particular trip, caught a particular fish, stood on a particular dock, or experienced a specific emotional breakthrough.

Instead, it asks what that public theme can become as a repeatable practice: less symbolism, more routine; less pressure to feel transformed, more room to identify the next responsible action.

## What the evidence supports—and what it does not

The [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/health-benefits/adults.html) states that a single session of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity can provide immediate benefits such as improved sleep quality and reduced short-term feelings of anxiety. The CDC also emphasizes that some physical activity is better than none and that activity can be accumulated in smaller portions.

The [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/health-and-wellness-benefits-of-parks.htm) summarizes research associating time in nature with restored attention, reduced mental fatigue, and improved well-being. A [systematic review indexed by the National Library of Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33066853/) found that the studies it reviewed generally linked nature exposure with lower perceived or physiological stress, while the methods and strength of individual studies varied.

Those findings support modest language:

- Walking, paddling, gardening, shoreline cleanup, or other outdoor movement can count as physical activity.
- A quieter setting may make it easier for some people to notice their thoughts without constant alerts and demands.
- A repeatable routine can create structure during an unsettled season.

They do **not** prove that nature will treat a particular diagnosis, resolve a legal or financial problem, repair a relationship, or produce the same response for every person. Anyone facing severe or persistent symptoms should seek appropriate professional care. Outdoor time can be one supportive practice inside a larger plan, not the whole plan.

## The 20-minute Quiet Water Reset

This tool does not require a boat, expensive equipment, a perfect lake, or even water. A porch, safe road, public park, yard, or patch of shade can work. If weather, mobility, safety, or health conditions make outdoor time impractical, the same steps can be used beside an open window or in another calm setting.

### Minutes 0–3: Arrive without producing

Put the phone on silent, while keeping emergency access available. Do not take a picture for social media. Do not open analytics. Do not begin by explaining the moment to anyone.

Notice the temperature, light, wind, sounds, and the ground under your feet. The goal is not to force calm. The goal is to arrive where your body already is.

### Minutes 3–8: Move at an honest pace

Walk, stretch, cast a line, sweep the porch, carry a small bag for litter, or do another safe and ordinary task. Choose movement that fits your current health and surroundings.

Nothing has to be impressive. Five honest minutes count.

### Minutes 8–13: Separate the load

On paper or in a note, make two short columns:

**Mine today**

- decisions I can make;
- work I can finish;
- people I can contact respectfully;
- facts I can verify;
- boundaries I can keep.

**Not mine today**

- another person's reaction;
- an algorithm's judgment;
- a rumor I cannot verify;
- a problem that requires information I do not yet have;
- the demand to repair everything at once.

This is not denial. It is triage.

### Minutes 13–18: Choose one proof-of-direction action

Pick one action small enough to complete after leaving the quiet place:

- answer one important message;
- organize one folder;
- finish one page;
- make one appointment;
- take one healthy next step;
- write one accurate paragraph instead of ten angry ones.

The action should prove direction, not perfection.

### Minutes 18–20: Re-enter on purpose

Before reopening the feed, write one sentence:

> The next right thing I will do is ________.

Then do that thing before asking the internet what it thinks.

## A seven-day version for Ryan—and for readers

The point is consistency, not a dramatic result.

### Day 1 — Find the place

Choose one safe, accessible outdoor setting. No need to make it meaningful yet.

### Day 2 — Remove one interruption

Silence one nonessential notification during the 20 minutes.

### Day 3 — Move gently

Add five minutes of movement that fits your health and ability.

### Day 4 — Write the two columns

Name what belongs to today and what does not.

### Day 5 — Complete the proof-of-direction action

Choose something that can be finished before the day ends.

### Day 6 — Invite connection without performance

Ask a trusted person to walk, fish, sit, or work outside with you. Conversation is optional. Presence is enough.

### Day 7 — Review without grading yourself

Ask three questions:

1. What setting made it easiest to pay attention?
2. What interruption kept taking over?
3. What small action helped the following hour go better?

Keep what helped. Adjust what did not. Do not turn one missed day into proof that the practice failed.

## Rebuilding is allowed to look ordinary

Ryan's public life has contained large events and large claims. His rebuilding does not have to match that scale every day.

Sometimes rebuilding is a finished article. Sometimes it is a new product, an organized record, or a difficult truth stated accurately. Sometimes it is twenty quiet minutes, followed by one task completed without applause.

That kind of ordinary work fits the same principle behind [keeping one small promise](/posts/how-to-rebuild-self-trust-keep-one-promise-today) and the [Carry, Cut, Create seven-day reset](/posts/how-to-keep-moving-forward-seven-day-reset): confidence is not manufactured by saying everything is fixed. It grows through evidence that a person can still choose a direction and act on it.

The outdoors can support that process because it gives the practice a place. It does not do the rebuilding for Ryan. It can give him room to do a little of the work himself—quietly, honestly, and without needing the moment to become content.
