*By the Real Ryan Nichols Editorial Team*

Texas students already receive instruction that can matter in the minutes before an ambulance arrives.

State law requires cardiopulmonary resuscitation and automated external defibrillator instruction for students in grades seven through twelve. It also requires public school systems to maintain bleeding control stations and annually offer instruction on their use to students in grade seven or higher.

But instruction is not the same as certification.

Texas law specifically says the CPR and AED instruction does not have to result in certification. The bleeding control law makes the same distinction.

That leaves a reasonable question for families, educators, first responders, and taxpayers.

Should every Texas student graduate with a recognized CPR, AED, and bleeding control credential, or is practical instruction without a certificate the better statewide standard?

## What Texas law requires now

[Texas Education Code Section 28.0023](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/ED/htm/ED.28.htm#28.0023) requires school districts and open enrollment charter schools to provide CPR and AED instruction to students in grades seven through twelve. Each student must receive the instruction at least once before graduation.

The instruction must include hands on practice. It can be provided as part of any course, and districts may use qualified firefighters, emergency medical technicians, paramedics, police officers, teachers, school employees, and representatives of nationally recognized training organizations.

The law also says the instruction is not required to produce certification. If a district chooses certification, the instructor must be authorized by the American Heart Association, American Red Cross, or a similar nationally recognized organization.

The State Board of Education updated its rules after the Legislature added AED use to the existing CPR requirement. The [official SBOE record](https://sboe.texas.gov/state-board-of-education/sboe-2024/sboe-2024-april/april-2024-committee-on-instruction-item-2) says the AED requirement began with students entering grade seven in the 2024 to 2025 school year.

## Texas also requires bleeding control readiness

[Texas Education Code Section 38.030](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/ED/htm/ED.38.htm#38.030) requires districts and charter schools to maintain bleeding control stations in accessible campus locations. The protocol includes training for school personnel who may reasonably need to use the equipment.

Schools must also annually offer instruction on using a bleeding control station to students in grade seven or higher. The course must use recognized evidence based guidance and include physical skills needed to use the equipment.

Again, certification is optional rather than mandatory.

The [enrolled text of House Bill 496](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/86R/billtext/html/HB00496F.htm), which created the program, lists supplies such as tourniquets, chest seals, gauze, gloves, scissors, and markers. The point is practical: major blood loss can become fatal before professional help reaches the patient.

Texas has already decided that these skills belong in public schools. The disagreement is about how far the state should go.

## The strongest case for certification

A credential creates a clear finish line.

“Received instruction” can describe a strong hands on course or a brief lesson that a student barely remembers. Certification requires a defined curriculum, an authorized instructor, skills demonstration, and documentation that the student met the standard.

Supporters can argue that a Texas diploma should represent more than academic credits. A graduate may encounter a cardiac arrest at work, a serious vehicle crash, an accident at home, or a traumatic injury during recreation. The first person beside the victim is often not a professional responder.

Certification could also become a practical employment asset. Child care, fitness, public safety, health care, construction, outdoor work, and many service jobs value employees who can respond before help arrives.

There is a fairness argument too. Families with money can pay for private courses. A statewide school based credential would give the same basic opportunity to students in rural districts, low income communities, and families that would never purchase the course separately.

## The strongest case against a mandate

Certification costs more than instruction.

Districts would need enough authorized instructors, course materials, equipment, scheduling time, records, and a way to handle students who are absent or need another attempt. A small rural district may face a different staffing problem than a large suburban system.

Opponents can also ask whether a credential proves long term readiness. Skills fade when they are not practiced. A student certified early in high school may still hesitate years later unless the school provides refreshers.

There is a graduation policy concern. Texas already requires instruction. Turning the credential into another condition for a diploma could delay a student because of scheduling, access, disability, absence, or a district's failure to provide the right course.

A more flexible approach could keep the current instruction requirement, fund voluntary certification for every interested student, and let districts partner with local fire departments, hospitals, emergency medical services, and community colleges.

## Editorial analysis: make the credential available to every student

Texas should make CPR, AED, and bleeding control certification available at no charge to every high school student.

The state should set a strong target for completion, publish district results, and fund the instructors and equipment. But a student's diploma should not be withheld when the district failed to provide a reasonable certification opportunity.

That position keeps the standard serious without turning an administrative failure into a punishment for the student.

The state already requires the instruction and much of the equipment. Certification is not a leap into a new subject. It is a question of turning required exposure into a portable, accountable skill.

Texas is already debating what public schools should guarantee. We recently asked whether [school meals should be free for every student](https://realryannichols.com/posts/should-texas-make-school-meals-free-for-every-student), whether [A through F school ratings tell families enough](https://realryannichols.com/posts/should-texas-keep-grading-public-schools-a-through-f), and whether [student phones should be locked away during the school day](https://realryannichols.com/posts/should-texas-schools-lock-student-phones-away-all-day).

Lifesaving skills belong in that same practical conversation. The question is not whether preparation matters. Texas law already answered that. The question is what level of proof should come with a diploma.
