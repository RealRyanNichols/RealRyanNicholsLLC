Daniel Roch Cholowsky waited his whole life for one sentence: his name, called first, at the MLB Draft.

When the moment came, Commissioner Rob Manfred said it wrong. Not close-wrong — full mangled, something in the neighborhood of "Cha-loo-sky" instead of "Chill-OW-skee."

X did what X does. Clips circulated within minutes. Draft analysts piled on. The general sentiment, paraphrased from about a hundred versions of the same post: *don't tell me you care about this draft if the commissioner can't say the first pick's name right.*

Fair criticism. Manfred runs the event. Get the name right — it's one job, and it's the top of the list.

But the funniest, most human detail in the entire story almost got buried under the discourse: Cholowsky didn't even hear the mispronunciation.

{{share}}

## What actually happened in that room

By his own account, Cholowsky already knew the White Sox were about to take him. He'd kept it to himself all morning — didn't tell his family, didn't tell anyone, let everybody experience the pick in real time with him.

So when Manfred opened his mouth and the name came out mangled, Cholowsky wasn't parsing syllables. He heard "Roch," heard the pick was his, and — in his own words — "kind of lost it." He smiled, put his face in his hands, and cried. His mom and sister were in tears next to him. His dad, by multiple accounts, had the most stone-faced reaction of the entire draft — the kind of deadpan that becomes its own meme.

That is the actual story. A kid gets the news he's waited twenty-some years for, surrounded by the people who got him there, and the mispronunciation is a footnote he didn't even register.

## Two true things, held at once

I want to be careful here, because I think this is a place where the internet's instinct to pick one side flattens something that's actually got two layers to it.

Layer one: Manfred should get the name right. It's disrespectful to stumble that badly on the single most personal moment of a draft prospect's night, and the criticism of the commissioner's office is legitimate. Preparation is not optional when you're the one holding the microphone.

Layer two: the moment itself — the actual human experience Cholowsky had — was not diminished by Manfred's flub. It happened around him, not to him. He got exactly what he came for: his name, his pick, his family in the room.

We are very good, collectively, at fusing those two layers into one flattened narrative — "the draft was ruined" or "Manfred embarrassed him" — when the truth is more like: an executive did his job poorly, and a 21-year-old had one of the best nights of his life anyway, almost entirely undisturbed by the executive's mistake.

## Why this distinction matters beyond baseball

This is a pattern I notice constantly: someone in a position of institutional authority fumbles — a name, a fact, a procedure — and the fallout gets narrated as if it happened to the person most affected, when often that person barely absorbed it. Meanwhile the actual headline — a family in tears of joy, a kid who kept his composure enough to let everyone else have the surprise — gets a fraction of the attention the executive's blunder gets.

Both things are worth talking about. But if you only remember the mispronunciation and not the kid crying into his hands next to his mother, you got the story backwards.

Cholowsky is now a Chicago White Sox farmhand with a name every broadcaster in that organization will now say correctly, guaranteed, for years. Manfred will answer for the flub for exactly as long as this news cycle lasts and not a day longer.

The kid's night was still perfect. That's worth remembering when the next executive fumbles a name in front of a camera.
