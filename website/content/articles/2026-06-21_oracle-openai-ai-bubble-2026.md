Let me show you a post that didn't age well.

When Oracle announced its Stargate joint venture with OpenAI and SoftBank in January 2026, committing to $500 billion in U.S. AI infrastructure investment, Gary Marcus — an AI researcher who has been consistently skeptical of the sector's valuations — posted this on X:

> *"Mark my words, this week marks peak bubble. Oracle just went up $300B on what appears to be a non-binding deal with OpenAI. OpenAI doesn't have $300 billion dollars. They don't have anywhere near $300 billion dollars. By their own (presumably optimistic) projection, they won't turn a profit until 2030."*

At the time, many people dismissed that take. The AI bull case was dominant. Nvidia was stratospheric. Oracle was riding the wave.

Five months later, the Wall Street Journal reported that OpenAI missed its internal revenue and user growth targets for 2026. Oracle shares fell 4.2% in premarket trading. Cloud infrastructure companies that had hitched their futures to OpenAI's momentum dropped alongside. And suddenly Marcus's "peak bubble" post was getting passed around X all over again.

## What's actually happening with Oracle

Let me lay out what's in the ledger, because the numbers are striking.

Oracle signed a five-year, $300 billion commitment with OpenAI — an arrangement where OpenAI agreed to use Oracle's cloud infrastructure for its AI workloads at a staggering scale. OpenAI represents more than $300 billion of Oracle's remaining performance obligations. That means if OpenAI slows down, stumbles, or restructures, Oracle absorbs the impact directly.

And OpenAI is showing signs of slowing down.

Yahoo Finance reported that Oracle was becoming a "poster child for AI bubble fears." The analysis from finance writers wasn't subtle: Oracle has the lowest credit rating among major tech companies. It also carries a significant debt load — and that's *before* the full infrastructure buildout comes into play. They don't yet have all the chips they would need to fulfill the contracts. And they don't have all the cash to buy them.

Meanwhile, Oracle announced it may pursue mass layoffs as it shifts operations toward AI-driven automation. That's the kind of move companies make when growth projections aren't materializing the way the market expected.

## This isn't about whether AI is real

I want to be clear about something before people read this as an "AI is a scam" argument.

AI is real. The technology is real. Generative AI has already changed how millions of people work. It's not going away.

What's being tested right now is not whether AI exists — it's whether the *valuations* built on top of it are real. Those are two very different questions.

The AI bull case that sent Oracle's stock soaring, that fueled the Stargate announcement, that justified hundreds of billions in data center infrastructure commitments — that case was built on projections. Projections about how fast OpenAI would grow, how quickly enterprise customers would spend, how soon the AI era would generate the kind of revenue that justifies the capital being deployed.

When OpenAI misses its internal targets, those projections come under pressure. And when projections come under pressure, the valuations built on top of them get questioned.

That's not a scandal. That's how markets work. The problem is the scale at which this particular stack was built.

## Bloomberg mapped the loop — and it's dizzying

Bloomberg published a graphic earlier this year mapping what they called the "AI money loop": how Microsoft, OpenAI, and Nvidia keep paying each other. It went viral on LinkedIn and X because it illustrated something people had been sensing but hadn't seen laid out clearly.

The circular nature of the deals — tech giants investing in OpenAI, OpenAI licensing from tech giants, tech giants selling chips and cloud to OpenAI — looks, when you draw it out, less like an ecosystem and more like a closed circuit where the same money cycles through different hands.

That doesn't mean the technology doesn't generate real value. But it does raise a legitimate question: at what point does the *financial architecture* supporting AI need to generate returns from *outside* the loop? Real revenue from real customers who aren't also investors in the system?

That's the question the Oracle situation is forcing into the open.

## What Gary Marcus got right — and what still isn't settled

Marcus has been calling this a bubble for years, and he's been wrong about the timeline multiple times. The technology kept advancing. The revenue kept growing, just not as fast as the promises suggested. The crash he predicted in 2023 didn't come. The crash he predicted in 2024 didn't come.

He may still be wrong about the ultimate outcome.

But the specific mechanism he identified — Oracle, as the single most exposed publicly traded company in the AI supply chain, as the canary in the mine for whether the bubble is bursting — that part is getting tested in real time right now. Oracle stock slides when OpenAI numbers disappoint. That's the correlation Marcus described, playing out on schedule.

The question isn't whether AI has a future. It almost certainly does. The question is who's going to be holding the bill when the gap between what was promised and what's being delivered finally gets priced in.

Right now, a lot of that bill has Oracle's name on it.
