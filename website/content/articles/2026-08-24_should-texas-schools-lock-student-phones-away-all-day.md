Texas has already answered the easy version of the school-phone question.

Students are not supposed to use personal communication devices on school property during the school day.

The harder question is what “not using” means in a building full of backpacks, smartwatches, buzzing notifications, worried parents, and teachers who do not want to spend class time policing pockets.

Should a phone stay powered off in a student's bag?

Or should Texas require every campus to place student devices in a locked pouch, locker, or storage system until dismissal?

The first approach gives families and districts flexibility. The second makes the rule visible and enforceable.

Both carry real costs.

## What Texas law requires now

[House Bill 1481](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/html/HB01481F.htm) requires Texas school districts and open-enrollment charter schools to maintain a written policy prohibiting students from using personal communication devices while on school property during the school day.

The definition includes smartphones, flip phones, tablets, smartwatches, radios, pagers, and other devices capable of digital communication. School-issued devices are excluded.

The law requires exceptions when a device is necessary for an individualized education program, a Section 504 plan, a documented physician directive, or a health or safety requirement imposed by law or school safety protocols.

It also leaves an important choice to local schools. A district may prohibit students from bringing devices to campus, or it may designate a storage method. The statute does not command one statewide storage system.

[Texas Education Agency guidance](https://tea.texas.gov/taa-letters/implementation-texas-house-bill-1481-student-use-personal-communication-devices-school) says the law took effect June 20, 2025, and required local policies by September 18, 2025. TEA also identifies $20 million in state grant funding to help schools buy secure storage and enforcement infrastructure.

So the debate is no longer whether Texas schools can have a phone-free day. They must.

The debate is whether the state should standardize the physical method.

## Why the phone in the backpack may not be enough

A rule that depends on every student resisting every vibration all day is difficult to enforce.

The phone is still within reach. The watch is still on the wrist. The teacher must notice the violation, interrupt instruction, determine what happened, and apply discipline consistently.

National data show why school leaders see a problem. In February 2025, the [National Center for Education Statistics](https://nces.ed.gov/learn/press-release/more-half-public-school-leaders-say-cell-phones-hurt-academic-performance) reported that 77 percent of public schools prohibited student phone use during class. Fifty-three percent of school leaders said phone use negatively affected academic performance, while 73 percent reported a negative effect on attention spans.

Those are school-leader assessments, not proof that one storage method fixes every outcome. But they show the scale of the operational problem.

[UNESCO reported in March 2026](https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/articles/phone-bans-schools-are-spreading-worldwide-policy-debate-rages) that 114 education systems, representing 58 percent of countries worldwide, had a national school-phone ban. The trend reflects concerns about distraction, cyberbullying, and student well-being.

Texas is part of a much larger policy movement. The unresolved part is enforcement.

## The strongest case for locked storage

The strongest argument is clarity.

If every phone goes into a numbered locker or secured pouch at arrival, students know the line, teachers know the line, and parents know the line. The device is not forbidden forever. It is physically unavailable during the hours set aside for school.

Secure storage can also protect instructional time. A teacher should not have to decide whether a glance at a watch was checking the time, reading a message, or opening an app. The device has already been handled before class begins.

Supporters can also argue that a uniform state rule prevents unequal enforcement. One campus should not treat a violation as a reminder while another turns the same conduct into repeated confiscation or suspension.

The state has already funded storage systems. Requiring them could turn a broad prohibition into a practical routine.

## The strongest case for local flexibility

Texas has more than one kind of school.

A small rural campus, a large suburban high school, an elementary school, and an alternative program do not have identical buildings, staffing, transportation, or safety plans.

Locked storage costs money to purchase, maintain, replace, and administer. Devices can be lost or damaged. Arrival and dismissal can become bottlenecks. A system that works for 300 students may create a daily mess for 3,000.

Parents also raise a serious safety concern. During an emergency, they want a direct line to their child. School leaders can respond that hundreds of simultaneous calls and rumors can complicate an incident, and that safety protocols should control communication. Still, telling a frightened parent that a locked device is better requires trust in the school's plan.

The statute's existing exceptions matter too. Medical, disability, and safety needs are not loopholes. A rigid statewide mechanism could create needless disclosure or delays for students who lawfully need access.

Local flexibility lets elected boards choose a method, explain it publicly, and change it when the method fails.

## Ryan's Take: Require the result, publish the method

Texas should keep the full-day prohibition. A phone-free school day is a defensible boundary, and teachers should not be turned into notification referees.

But the state should not require one storage product or one physical system for every campus.

Require each district to publish four things in plain language:

1. Where devices remain during the school day.
2. How exceptions are approved without publicly identifying a student's private need.
3. How families receive verified information during an emergency.
4. What the district learned from violations, loss claims, and instructional interruptions.

If powered-off phones in backpacks produce constant violations, the district should move to secure storage. If a locked system creates dangerous delays or repeated property problems, the district should have authority to repair the method without waiting on Austin.

State law should define the result and transparency. Local schools should own the mechanics and the evidence.
