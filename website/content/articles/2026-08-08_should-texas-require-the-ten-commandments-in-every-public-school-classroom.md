*By the Real Ryan Nichols Editorial Team*  
*Facts last checked: August 8, 2026, 5:20 a.m. Central*

When Texas students walk into public school classrooms this year, many will see a state-prescribed version of the Ten Commandments on the wall.

Supporters call the display a recognition of history and a basic moral tradition. Opponents call it the government's selection and promotion of a religious text inside a compulsory public institution.

The argument deserves more than a slogan from either side.

The law is specific. The latest federal appellate ruling is consequential. The constitutional and community questions are real.

## What Senate Bill 10 requires

The enrolled text of [Texas Senate Bill 10](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/html/SB00010F.HTM) says every public elementary or secondary school classroom must display a durable poster or framed copy of the Ten Commandments.

The display must be at least 16 by 20 inches. The commandments must be legible to a person with average vision from anywhere in the classroom. The law also prescribes the exact English wording and says the text must be the focus of the display without additional content.

Schools must accept and display qualifying posters that are privately donated. They may also use public funds to buy them, although the statute does not require a purchase when no donation is available.

The requirement applies classroom by classroom, not only in a history course, library, or common area.

The [Texas House committee analysis](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/analysis/html/SB00010H.htm) presented supporters' view that the commandments have influenced American legal and educational traditions. The same analysis recorded concerns that the bill could violate constitutional protections, marginalize students from different traditions, and expose school districts to litigation.

## Where the court fight stands

Legal challenges produced an injunction against enforcement in a group of districts. On April 21, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, sitting en banc, reversed that injunction by a 9-7 vote.

The [Texas attorney general's official announcement](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/news/releases/attorney-general-ken-paxton-successfully-defends-sb-10-and-ensures-ten-commandments-are-displayed) links the appellate opinion and describes the ruling as a successful defense of Senate Bill 10.

The decision matters because the court concluded the challengers had not shown the facial unconstitutionality required for the injunction before the law was implemented in those districts. That does not mean every imaginable dispute about every display has disappeared. It does mean the broad order blocking the requirement was reversed.

As of this fact check, the law is in effect and school districts are preparing for the 2026-27 school year under that legal posture.

This article is not legal advice. It is an editorial explanation of the public record and the policy choice Texas has made.

## The strongest case for the requirement

The strongest argument for the law is not simply that many Texans are Christian.

It is that a short text with deep influence on Western moral and legal thought can be displayed as part of the historical inheritance of the state without forcing a student to worship, pray, recite, or profess belief.

Supporters can reasonably point to principles against murder, theft, false testimony, and coveting as moral ideas that remain recognizable across religious and nonreligious communities. They can also argue that removing religious influence from the account of American history would create its own distortion.

The Fifth Circuit's ruling gives supporters a powerful legal answer to the claim that the statute is automatically unconstitutional on its face.

There is also a local values argument. Texas voters elect lawmakers to make education policy. If those lawmakers believe the display communicates an important civic inheritance, supporters say the state should not have to treat every religious reference as forbidden.

## The strongest case against it

The strongest objection begins with government power, not hostility toward faith.

Texas did not require schools to teach a neutral unit comparing moral or religious traditions. It selected one exact religious text and ordered its permanent display in every classroom, including classrooms serving young children and families who are Jewish, Muslim, Hindu, Buddhist, Christian in a different tradition, or not religious.

Critics can reasonably ask why the state should choose the wording, placement, and minimum size of a sacred text at all. A public school has compulsory attendance rules and authority over students. That makes a classroom different from a private home, church, or voluntary religious school.

The text also contains explicitly religious commands, not only widely shared rules against murder and theft. Opponents argue that calling the whole display merely historical avoids the religious claim the document actually makes.

Even if the current appellate decision permits this statute, families may still believe the state is occupying a role that belongs to parents and faith communities.

Texas has already made another statewide classroom choice by restricting student phone use. ## Ryan's Take

*Editorial analysis prepared by the Real Ryan Nichols Editorial Team. This is not presented as a firsthand quotation from Ryan.*

Faith is not strengthened by pretending the government has no history with religion. It is also not strengthened by pretending state power becomes harmless when it promotes words we personally respect.

The Ten Commandments deserve more than treatment as classroom decor. Believers should be able to explain what they mean, why they matter, and how faith changes conduct. A required poster cannot do that work by itself.

At the same time, opponents should address the actual law and ruling rather than claim that students are being ordered to pray or recite the text. Senate Bill 10 requires a display. It does not require religious participation.

The honest question is whether a mandatory, state-selected display is a legitimate recognition of historical moral influence or an unnecessary use of government authority in matters of faith.

That question deserves the same discipline we bring to other policy debates, including [who should control the devices Texans own](https://realryannichols.com/posts/should-texas-make-it-easier-to-repair-the-devices-you-own). State power should be judged by the rule it actually creates, the evidence supporting it, and the limits it respects.

**Should Texas require this exact Ten Commandments display in every public school classroom, or should that decision remain with families, teachers, and local communities?**
