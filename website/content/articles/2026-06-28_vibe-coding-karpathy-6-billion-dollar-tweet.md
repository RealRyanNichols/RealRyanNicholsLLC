On February 2, 2025, Andrej Karpathy fired off a post on X that he later admitted he did not think would go anywhere.

Here is what he wrote:

> *"There's a new kind of coding I call 'vibe coding', where you fully give in to the vibes, embrace exponentials, and forget that the code even exists."*

He described just talking to an AI, running what it gave him, copy-pasting errors back in, and mostly getting to something that worked. No deep debugging. No architecture diagrams. Just *vibes* and a working product.

Karpathy later said, looking back a year later, that he "still can't predict tweet engagement basically at all" — and that this was a "shower of thoughts throwaway tweet" he just fired off.

That throwaway tweet has since been viewed millions of times. Collins Dictionary named it Word of the Year. MIT Technology Review listed it among the 10 Breakthrough Technologies of 2026. And the market it described grew to an estimated $4.7 billion this year.

Not bad for a shower thought.

## What Vibe Coding Actually Is

Here is the simple version: instead of writing code line by line, you describe what you want in plain English to an AI, it writes the code, you test it, you describe what is broken, it fixes it, and you keep going until you have something real.

The most honest description remains Karpathy's original one: *"I just see stuff, say stuff, run stuff, and copy-paste stuff, and it mostly works."*

What changed that made this possible is that the underlying models — particularly Anthropic's Claude and the AI tools built on top of them, like Cursor — got good enough at code generation that the gap between "what a human thinks" and "what a computer needs" became small enough to bridge through conversation.

The result? 63% of vibe coding users today are not professional developers. And 92% of US developers now use AI coding tools daily.

The gatekeepers at the door — the years of CS education, the memorized syntax, the arcane debugging knowledge — are dissolving.

## The Success Stories Are Insane

Lovable, one of the flagship vibe coding platforms, raised $330 million at a $6.6 billion valuation. It is on pace to cross $1 billion in annual recurring revenue before the end of 2026.

Medvi, an AI-powered medical documentation tool built largely through AI-assisted development, pulled in $401 million in its first year and is projected to reach $1.8 billion this year.

These are not side projects. These are real companies, with real revenue, built by people who would not have been able to build them five years ago.

That is one of the most genuinely democratizing things I have seen in tech in a long time — and I do not say that lightly.

## The Part the Hype Gets Wrong

I have to give you the honest picture, not just the viral one.

Developer enthusiasm for AI coding tools has actually *dropped* since the early excitement. Favorability went from 77% in 2023 to 60% in 2026. And only about a third of developers say they trust AI-generated code for accuracy.

Research shows that AI co-authored code contains 1.7 times more major issues than human-written code.

What that means in practice: vibe coding is phenomenal for prototyping, for getting from zero to something-testable, for solo founders exploring an idea. It is substantially more dangerous when you are building anything that handles real money, real health data, or real security.

The people who are winning with vibe coding understand the tradeoffs. They use AI to build fast, then bring in engineers to audit and harden what the AI generated. The people who get burned are the ones who shipped straight to production without understanding what they were shipping.

## What This Means If You Are Not a Developer

Here is what I keep coming back to.

For most of the history of software, having an idea was worth almost nothing. Execution required capital, technical co-founders, years of learning, or expensive contractors. The idea-to-product gap was wide enough that it filtered out all but the most resourced or most committed builders.

Vibe coding is compressing that gap. Not eliminating it — you still need judgment, taste, the ability to identify what users actually want, and eventually real engineering rigor. But the barrier to testing an idea has dropped dramatically.

If you have a business idea that requires software, the question is no longer *do I know how to code?* The question is *can I describe clearly enough what I want?*

That is a different game. And the people who learn to play it are going to have a serious advantage over the next five years.

Karpathy himself just joined Anthropic — the company whose models underpin much of what makes vibe coding possible in the first place. The man who named the movement is now building the tools that power it.

I do not think that is a coincidence. I think it is a signal about where the action is going to be.
