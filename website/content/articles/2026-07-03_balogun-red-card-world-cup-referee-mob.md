In the 64th minute of the USMNT's World Cup match against Bosnia and Herzegovina, Folarin Balogun went up for a 50/50 ball, lost his balance, and came down on Tarik Muharemovic's ankle.

In real time, it looked like nothing. A collision. The kind of thing that happens forty times a game.

https://x.com/tombogert/status/2072493475262767313

The referee didn't card it. Then VAR sent him to the monitor. Then, staring at a slowed-down, frame-by-frame version of a moment that happened in a fraction of a second, he reversed himself and showed red.

Balogun is out for the Round of 16 against Belgium. Under FIFA rules, there is no appeal.

## The internet didn't stop at the call. It went after the man.

Analysts across the sport — including former FIFA officials — said the review misapplied VAR protocol, which exists specifically to overturn *clear and obvious* errors, not to relitigate a 50/50 challenge in super-slow-motion until it looks worse than it was. That's a legitimate, sourced criticism of a system.

What followed on X wasn't that. It was a demand that the referee, personally, lose his job over one call in one match. Not "the protocol failed." **Him. Fired. Now.**

I want to be precise about where I land, because the two critiques get flattened into one mob, and they're not the same thing.

## Slow motion is a different sport than the one that was played

Here is the trap, and it isn't just a soccer problem: **slow it down enough, and anything can be made to look intentional.**

A stumble becomes a stomp. A glance becomes a stare. A word said once, fast, in a room full of noise, becomes a headline when it's typed out cold, three years later, with no tone and no context.

I have spent the better part of my adult life on the other side of exactly that mechanism — watching a chaotic, real-time moment get frozen, zoomed, and re-narrated by people who were never in it, until the freeze-frame version became the only version anyone was allowed to believe. I don't get to litigate my own case in this post, and I won't. But I know the shape of this machine well enough to recognize it instantly when I see it happening to a 24-year-old striker on a soccer pitch.

The protocol that let a real-time non-call become a monitor-review red card deserves every bit of the scrutiny it's getting. The referee, as a person, deserves the same thing due process gives anyone: judged on what he could actually see, in the moment he had to see it — not refought in slow motion by a crowd that gets to watch the replay as many times as it wants.

## Fix the system. Leave the man alone.

If FIFA's VAR protocol is producing calls this inconsistent on the sport's biggest stage, that's a real story worth pressure and reform. Say that loudly. I will say it with you.

But "he should lose his job" is not accountability. It's the same online reflex that turns one clip into a career-ending verdict before anyone has asked what the person actually intended, in real time, with the information they actually had.
