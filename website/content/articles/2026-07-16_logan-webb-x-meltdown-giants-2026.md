Wednesday night, the San Francisco Giants lost 10-0 to the Blue Jays. Logan Webb, their ace, gave up a first-inning grand slam and never really recovered.

That's a bad day at work. It happens in every sport, every season. What happened next is the part everyone's actually talking about.

## The post that started it

KNBR's Jack Loder posted a video on X reacting to the loss, saying Webb has "always been a great Giant" but that "there's always been a little bit lacking. I wish he was more of an ace."

Fair or not, that's a media take. Athletes get criticized after bad outings every day of the season.

Webb didn't let it go. He replied directly: "You know what's sad is they allow people like you in the locker room." When another account jumped in with a joke, Webb shot back, "Who are you?" To a critical fan, he fired: "Honestly you probably don't know anything about anything, some loser on the couch that couldn't make his little league team."

By late Wednesday night, his account was gone. Deactivated.

## I know exactly what that midnight deletion feels like

I'm not writing this to pile onto a guy who had one bad night at the ballpark and one bad night on a keyboard. I'm writing it because I've lived the exact shape of that spiral — frustration boiling over into a reply you can't unsend, the replies stacking up faster than you can process them, and eventually staring at the delete button because it feels like the only way to make it stop.

Webb told reporters afterward: "I was just frustrated with the way my day went. Frustrated with how the season's gone." That's an honest answer. It's also the same explanation I've given for my own worst posts — the ones written in a moment I didn't have the distance to see clearly yet.

Here's what nobody tells you before you hit send at 11 p.m. after the worst day of your professional life: the internet doesn't grade on context. It grades on the clip. Webb will be remembered for one night of replies longer than he'll be remembered for the seven solid innings he's thrown in a hundred other games this year.

## The part worth sitting with

I'm not defending what he wrote. Loder didn't deserve to be told he shouldn't be allowed in a locker room for doing his job, and the fan who got told he "couldn't make his little league team" didn't do anything except post a tweet.

But I also think there's something broken about a culture where the only two options after a bad moment are "get mobbed forever" or "delete every trace of yourself and disappear." Neither of those things helps anyone learn anything. One just makes the internet meaner, and the other makes it emptier.

Webb owned it afterward instead of blaming the platform, the reporter, or "haters." That's more than a lot of people — public figures and private citizens alike — manage to do after a bad night online. I'll give him that much credit.

## What I'd want people to take from this

Before you fire off the reply that feels good for four seconds, ask whether you'll still stand behind it in the morning. And before you pile onto someone else's bad night, ask whether you've ever had one of your own that you're just lucky nobody clipped.
