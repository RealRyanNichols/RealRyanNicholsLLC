On July 6, Pepsi posted this:

https://x.com/pepsi/status/2074161453045788823

"Pepsi Wild Cherry is what happens when regular cherry stops asking permission."

Somebody in a marketing meeting thought that line was clever. Within hours it wasn't clever — it was a consent joke from a company with a market cap most countries would envy, and the internet made sure everyone knew it. "Pepsi, what the hell? Promoting r*pe culture? This is shameful," read one of the calmer replies.

Ten hours later, the post came down. The next day, the apology: "Our recent Wild Cherry post landed in a way we never intended. We hear you, we're sorry, and the post has been deleted."

{{share}}

## Ten hours. Then it was over.

I'm not here to defend the ad. It was bad, and the backlash was earned — a global brand's legal and marketing review process let a consent joke through, and that's worth asking hard questions about.

What I want you to notice is the shape of the recovery.

Pepsi had lawyers, a comms team, and a playbook drafted before the sun came up the next day. They controlled the timeline. They chose the words of their own apology. Within 48 hours, most of the internet had moved on to the next thing, and Pepsi's stock didn't blink.

I don't get to compare that to a hypothetical. I've lived the other version — where one post, one video clip stripped of context, one moment gets *your* name turned into a headline, and there's no comms team drafting your statement, no legal department running interference, and no 48-hour news cycle that resets. The pile-on doesn't come down in ten hours for a private citizen. Sometimes it doesn't come down at all. It just goes quiet for a while and comes back the next time someone wants to dig it up.

## The outrage was real. The accountability gap is the actual story.

Here's what nobody's saying out loud: the same energy that took down a Pepsi tweet in ten hours could, in theory, be pointed at accountability that actually costs something — a settlement, a review process, a person losing their job over the decision, not just the words. Instead it burns hot for a day, gets an apology statement, and moves on. The corporation absorbs the hit and keeps selling soda. It has the resources to weather a mob that would flatten an individual.

I've said this before about other pile-ons and I'll say it again here: volume isn't the same thing as justice. A trending topic isn't a verdict. When the target is big enough to survive the wave, the wave crashes and recedes and nothing structural changes. When the target is a single person without a PR department, the wave doesn't recede — it just moves in with them.

## What actually deserves scrutiny here

Not the joke. The process. How did that line clear legal review at a company with Pepsi's resources? Who signed off? That's the accountability question worth asking, and it's the one least likely to get answered, because "we're sorry, the post has been deleted" ends most people's curiosity right there.

It shouldn't. An apology that costs a brand nothing but ten hours of bad press isn't accountability. It's a very well-rehearsed way of making the story stop.
