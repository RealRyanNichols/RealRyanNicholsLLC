A local Facebook group can show you what a town is talking about. That does not make the member list yours.

The tempting shortcut is to scrape names, profiles, or contact details, turn them into an audience, and call it precise local marketing. The problem is not just that the tactic feels wrong. Meta’s own help page says scraping is automated collection of data from its products, and it says using automation to collect data without permission violates its terms.

You do not need that shortcut to learn whether an offer works in one city.

Build the test around geography, relevant creative, consented first-party data, and a page you control.

{{callout: key | Target the place, earn the response, and keep the lead path on property you control.}}

## Start with the question, not the audience trick

A useful local test answers one business question.

- Will Longview homeowners request an estimate for this service?
- Will Marshall business owners download this checklist?
- Will people within a practical service radius book a consultation?

Choose one city or service area, one offer, and one conversion. Do not test five cities, three services, and four calls to action in the same small campaign. You will spend money and still not know what worked.

## Use location targeting for the first test

Meta provides [location targeting options](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/365561350785642) for reaching people in selected places. Depending on the available campaign settings, that may include people who live in or were recently in the selected area. Read the current option carefully because a visitor is not the same as a resident.

Use the smallest geography that matches the business’s real service area. A contractor who only serves a thirty-minute radius should not buy attention statewide. A destination business may reasonably include visitors. The map should follow the operation, not somebody’s desire to make the potential audience look large.

Do not claim that geographic targeting reaches every resident or only perfect prospects. It does neither. It gives the platform a boundary for delivery.

## Make the creative unmistakably local

The ad should explain why the offer matters in that place without pretending to be a news story or using a fake testimonial.

Name the service area. Show the actual type of work. Use a real photograph you own or have permission to use. State the eligibility, price condition, or appointment limit plainly. If there is no deadline, do not invent one.

Create two or three honest versions that test a real difference:

1. Problem-first: name the costly or frustrating problem.
2. Outcome-first: show the practical result the service is built to produce.
3. Process-first: explain the first simple step and what happens after it.

Change one main idea at a time. If the image, headline, offer, and audience all change, the results cannot tell you which choice mattered.

## Use first-party data only when you have the right to use it

A customer list can be useful, but it is not automatically fair game. Meta’s [Custom Audience terms](https://www.facebook.com/legal/terms/customaudience/update) require the advertiser to have the necessary rights, permissions, and lawful basis for the data used.

That means a list collected for one purpose should not quietly become an advertising audience for another. It also means buying, copying, or scraping a group member list is not a substitute for consent.

Meta has described scraping as an ongoing security and privacy problem in its explanation of [how it combats scraping](https://about.fb.com/news/2021/04/how-we-combat-scraping/). The clean operator decision is to avoid building a system that depends on taking data you did not earn.

## Send the click to an owned landing page

Do not send paid traffic to a generic homepage and hope visitors find the right button.

Create one page for the city and offer. It should include:

- a plain headline that matches the ad;
- the service area and who the offer is for;
- a short explanation of the next step;
- a form that asks only for information you will use;
- a privacy statement and truthful consent language;
- a confirmation page that tells the lead what happens next.

[A Form Error Should Tell the Lead What to Do Next](/posts/a-form-error-should-tell-lead-what-to-do-next) covers a detail that costs businesses real opportunities. If the form fails, the message should help the person recover instead of making them guess.

Ownership changes the test. The platform can deliver attention, but the business keeps the page, the consent record, the lead status, and the learning.

## Run a seven-day clean test

Choose a budget you can lose without harming payroll or operations. Keep it steady for seven days unless the ad is clearly broken, rejected, misleading, or producing unsafe responses.

Track the path:

1. Ad impression
2. Landing-page visit
3. Form start
4. Qualified submission
5. Contact attempt
6. Appointment or clear loss reason

Clicks are not the final score. A campaign with fewer clicks and more qualified conversations may be the better campaign.

[Every Lost Lead Needs a Reason Code](/posts/every-lost-lead-needs-reason-code) shows how to preserve the lesson when a lead does not become a customer. “Bad leads” is not analysis. Wrong city, no answer, no budget, duplicate, outside service scope, and not ready are different problems.

## Decide with evidence you can explain

At the end of the test, ask:

- Which creative produced qualified submissions?
- Where did people leave the page?
- How quickly did the business respond?
- Which loss reason appeared most often?
- Should the next test change the offer, the page, the audience, or the follow-up?

Do not scale because a dashboard number turned green. Scale when the full path produces outcomes the business can serve and understand.

If the lead path is scattered across messages, spreadsheets, and memory, [The Lead Flow Pro](https://theleadflowpro.com) is a natural place to begin organizing the diagnostic. The point is not another dashboard. The point is knowing where a real opportunity entered, what happened next, and why it moved or died.

[I Never Owned the Customer List](/posts/i-never-owned-the-customer-list) names the deeper risk. A business can rent reach from a platform. It should not rent its entire memory.
