Texas opened its first Education Freedom Account application window on February 4, 2026.

It closed on March 31.

The school year is now underway, the program says more than 274,000 applications were submitted, and more than 100,000 awards have been made. Families who missed the window are directed to an interest list for 2027-28.

That creates a fair public question.

Should Texas open a second application window for the current school year, or should it keep the first-year rules fixed and focus on the families already awarded or waiting?

## What Texas has confirmed

The [official Texas Education Freedom Accounts website](https://educationfreedom.texas.gov/) says the Legislature created the program through Senate Bill 2 in 2025 and appropriated $1 billion.

The Texas Comptroller oversees the program. Odyssey serves as the certified educational assistance organization that helps administer it.

For the 2026-27 school year, the official site lists these award levels:

- $10,474 for an awarded child attending a participating private school
- Up to $30,000 for certain private-school students with a qualifying individualized education program on file
- $2,000 for an awarded child who is homeschooled or otherwise outside the listed private-school setting

The program says funding and waitlist notices have been posted in applicant portals. It also reports that approximately 25,500 applicants were found ineligible, most commonly because the program could not confirm an eligibility requirement or required documentation.

The state allows an ineligibility appeal within 30 days of notification, but says appeals seeking exceptions to deadlines or missing information are unlikely to be approved.

Those are the current rules, verified September 21, 2026.

## The strongest case for a second window

An eight-week application period does not reach every eligible family at the moment they need it.

Some parents may not have known the program existed. Others may have changed jobs, moved, received a diagnosis affecting a child’s educational needs, or encountered a school problem after March 31.

A family deciding in September that the current setting is not working cannot go back to February and apply.

Supporters of a second window can also point to normal first-year friction. A new statewide program will not reach every community evenly. Information travels faster through some school networks, advocacy groups, and neighborhoods than others.

If awarded families decline, withdraw, or fail to complete enrollment steps, a second window could help Texas use available funds for eligible children instead of leaving capacity idle.

The strongest argument is simple: a program built around family choice should have a path for families whose circumstances change during the year.

## The strongest case for keeping the window closed

The state received far more applications than it could fund.

Texas already has eligible applicants on a waitlist. Opening the door to new applications before resolving that list could feel like changing the line after people followed the published rules.

Schools and families also need certainty. Award amounts, enrollment confirmation, approved expenses, and payment schedules are not instant decisions. A late cycle could create confusion about tuition commitments, vendor capacity, and which expenses qualify from what date.

There is a budget problem too. A second application period does not create a second billion dollars.

If the program has replacement awards to issue, administrators may be able to move through the existing waitlist using the priority order in state law. That is cleaner than asking new families to invest time in an application when the available number of awards may be small or unknown.

The strongest case against reopening is not hostility to family choice. It is that predictable rules and an orderly waitlist are part of fairness too.

## Editorial analysis

Texas should not announce a broad second application window unless it can first answer three questions in public:

1. How much 2026-27 funding remains uncommitted?
2. How many eligible applicants remain on the current waitlist by priority tier?
3. Would new applicants go behind the current waitlist or enter the same selection process?

Without those answers, “reopening” could create hope without a meaningful chance of an award.

The better immediate step is a published replacement-award process. Texas should explain how declined or unused awards move to the next eligible student, how often that process runs, and when families should stop expecting movement for the current year.

If the existing waitlist is exhausted and funds remain, then a limited second window makes sense.

If the waitlist is not exhausted, the state should serve that line first and make the 2027-28 application calendar public as early as possible.

## Ryan’s Take

Rules matter most when demand is bigger than the promise.

Texas should not move the goalposts for families who applied on time and are still waiting. It also should not treat March 31 as the end of the conversation for everyone whose life changed in April.

Publish the numbers. Work the existing waitlist in the order the law requires. Open a second window only if real funding remains after that.

Choice needs access, but access without a truthful picture of the available seats is just another form to fill out.
