Your website can load in a second and still feel broken.

The phone screen opens. The headline is tiny. The menu stacks on top of itself. A form field runs off the edge. The visitor pinches the page, drags sideways, misses a button, and leaves.

The analytics may call that a bounce.

The person calls it work.

{{callout: key | If a customer has to pinch, hunt, or rotate the phone to understand the next step, the page is not finished.}}

## Mobile is not a smaller desktop

Shrinking a desktop layout is not responsive design.

A phone changes the conditions of the visit. The screen is narrow. The visitor may be outside, moving, distracted, using one hand, or reading in glare. The keyboard covers part of a form. A sticky banner can consume a third of the useful space.

That means the page needs a clear order:

1. What is this?
2. Is it for me?
3. What should I do next?

If a desktop page presents five columns, three competing calls to action, and a long navigation bar, the mobile version cannot merely squeeze them together. It has to decide what comes first.

## Three standards worth testing

The World Wide Web Consortium gives builders useful, testable boundaries.

Under [WCAG 2.2 Reflow](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/reflow), ordinary horizontal content should reflow at a width equivalent to 320 CSS pixels without forcing two-directional scrolling, subject to exceptions for content that genuinely requires a two-dimensional layout.

The [Resize Text guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/resize-text.html) says text should be able to grow to 200 percent without losing content or functionality.

The [Target Size Minimum criterion](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html) sets a 24 by 24 CSS pixel minimum for pointer targets or requires sufficient spacing, with defined exceptions. Larger targets can still be the better practical choice for an important button.

These are accessibility requirements and explanations, not a complete conversion formula. They give you three plain tests: narrow it, enlarge it, and try to tap it.

## Run the seven-screen phone audit

Do not start with every page. Start with the seven screens closest to a customer decision.

### 1. The homepage opening

Can a person understand what you do before scrolling?

Remove vague claims. Put the useful noun on the screen. “Reliable solutions for modern needs” says nothing. “East Texas roof repair and storm inspections” tells the visitor where they are.

### 2. The main service page

Does the first screen name the problem, the service, and the next step? Check whether the most important sentence wraps naturally without becoming six short fragments.

### 3. The contact page

Can someone tap the phone number? Is the form label visible after the keyboard opens? Do errors appear beside the field that caused them?

### 4. The quote or booking form

Complete it with one thumb. Do not use saved autofill. If a field requires a format, explain the format before submission. If the form fails, preserve what the person already entered.

### 5. One article or proof page

Increase browser zoom. Look for clipped headings, floating share tools that cover text, and images wider than the screen. Long evidence pages need calmer spacing, not smaller type.

### 6. The menu

Open every level. Make sure the close control remains visible and that each link has room around it. A menu is not usable because it technically opens.

### 7. The thank-you page

Submit a safe test inquiry. The final screen should say what was received, what happens next, and when the person should expect a response. A blank success state destroys the confidence the form just earned.

## Test the real path, not a screenshot

A design screenshot cannot show keyboard overlap, focus order, loading shifts, error recovery, or whether a button is actually tappable.

Use a real phone. Use mobile data once. Try portrait and landscape. Increase text size in the device settings. Ask someone who did not build the page to find the service, price boundary, phone number, or form.

Record each failure as a plain sentence:

- Headline clipped at 200 percent.
- Submit button hidden behind keyboard.
- Menu close control too small.
- Form lost answers after one error.
- Thank-you page gave no response time.

Then give each failure one owner and one due date.
