One of Longview’s busiest north-side corridors is being studied, and the public questionnaire is still accepting responses.

The [City of Longview announced](https://www.longviewtexas.gov/m/newsflash/Home/Detail/6514) that it and the Longview Metropolitan Planning Organization are developing a corridor study for Fourth Street between Loop 281 and Eastman Road.

The study is looking at transportation, safety, and the overall vision for the corridor. The in-person open house happened in August, but the [online questionnaire remains active](https://us.maptionnaire.com/q/96fb29syb7da) as of 3:35 a.m. Central on September 20, 2026.

That gives residents, workers, property owners, customers, cyclists, and drivers another way to put specific concerns into the record.

## What area is being studied

The study covers Fourth Street from Loop 281 south to Eastman Road.

That stretch carries more than one kind of trip. It serves homes, offices, medical destinations, restaurants, retail, schools, and the larger network of roads that move people across Longview.

A corridor can work differently depending on who is using it. A driver may care about turn conflicts and signal timing. A person walking may notice where sidewalks disappear or crossings feel exposed. A business may care about access, visibility, deliveries, and whether customers can enter and leave safely.

The value of a public survey is not that every request will become a project. It is that planners can compare lived experience with traffic data and design constraints before a preferred plan hardens.

## What the city is asking for

The official notice says the agencies want feedback on transportation, safety concerns, and the overall vision for Fourth Street.

The online questionnaire begins with an introduction to the study area and moves through several pages of questions. Residents can use it to identify how they use the corridor and what they believe needs attention.

Useful feedback is usually specific.

Instead of writing “traffic is bad,” name the intersection, time of day, direction of travel, and movement that creates the problem.

Instead of writing “walking feels unsafe,” name the missing crossing, short signal, blocked sightline, dark segment, or point where the sidewalk ends.

Instead of asking planners to “help business,” explain whether the problem is confusing access, delivery space, driveway conflicts, construction communication, or something else a corridor plan can actually address.

## Who may be affected

The study can matter to people who do not live directly on Fourth Street.

It may affect:

- residents in neighborhoods that connect to the corridor
- people who commute through the area
- businesses and employees along Fourth Street
- customers reaching medical, retail, food, and service destinations
- people walking, biking, or using mobility devices
- emergency, delivery, and transit operations

Different users may want different things from the same block. Faster movement can conflict with safer crossings. Fewer driveways can reduce conflict points while changing how customers reach a business. A new turn treatment can help one direction and add delay to another.

That is why the record needs more than a list of general preferences.

## What is known and what is not

The city has clearly identified the study area and the subjects under review. The questionnaire is active, and the project page remains available.

The official notice does not publish a final survey deadline. It also does not announce a selected design, construction contract, budget, or construction schedule.

That distinction matters.

A corridor study is not the same thing as a funded construction project. It can document conditions, compare options, and establish priorities. Later decisions may still depend on engineering, right of way, environmental review, funding, and public approvals.

Residents should not assume that a questionnaire response guarantees a change. They also should not wait until a final design appears to explain a problem that could have been documented earlier.

## How to make one useful comment

Open the [official Fourth Street study notice](https://www.longviewtexas.gov/m/newsflash/Home/Detail/6514), then follow its link to the survey or go directly to the [active questionnaire](https://us.maptionnaire.com/q/96fb29syb7da).

Before you begin, write down four details:

1. The exact location.
2. The time and conditions when the issue happens.
3. Who is affected.
4. The outcome you want planners to test.

A strong comment might say that eastbound drivers at a named intersection cannot see a person entering the crosswalk during the evening rush. That gives planners a location, movement, user, and condition to examine.

If the questionnaire closes before you respond, use the contact information on the city notice to ask the Longview MPO how comments can still be submitted.

## Public input works best before the answer is final

People often pay attention to transportation projects when orange barrels arrive.

By then, many of the basic choices may already have been narrowed.

This is the quieter stage. It is also the stage when a careful observation can be most useful.

You do not need to be a traffic engineer to report what happens on the street you use. You do need to be precise enough that somebody else can investigate it.

What is the one location on Fourth Street that you believe the corridor team needs to see at the time it actually becomes a problem?
