“Closed” is not an answer.

Neither is “went cold.”

If a lead disappears and the CRM records only that the opportunity was lost, the business cannot tell whether the problem was speed, fit, price, timing, trust, competition, bad contact data, or a follow-up that never happened.

The record ends. The lesson disappears with it.

Every lost lead needs one clear reason code, one short note when needed, and enough evidence to separate what the customer said from what the team assumed.

{{callout: key | A reason code is not a verdict on the customer or the salesperson. It is a repair signal for the system.}}

## Stop using “cold” as a diagnosis

A lead can look cold for very different reasons.

The phone number was wrong.

The business replied after the customer hired somebody else.

The service was outside the company’s area.

The customer needed financing the company did not offer.

The estimate was never delivered.

The project was delayed for three months.

Those outcomes should not land in one bucket.

When they do, the owner sees a pile of lost opportunities and reaches for a general solution. More ads. More calls. A new CRM. A new employee. A discount.

The business may spend money treating the wrong failure.

## Start with six codes

Do not build a list of forty choices. Staff will stop using it or choose the first option that ends the task.

Start with six:

1. **No contact made.** The team attempted contact but never confirmed a conversation.
2. **Not a fit.** The service, location, timing, scope, or qualification did not match what the business can deliver.
3. **Price or terms.** The customer specifically identified price, financing, deposit, or payment terms.
4. **Chose another provider.** The customer confirmed a different choice.
5. **Customer delayed or canceled.** The need moved, paused, or disappeared.
6. **Internal failure.** The business missed a response, estimate, appointment, handoff, or promised next step.

Add “other” only if it requires a note. Review those notes monthly. If the same real outcome appears repeatedly, promote it into a code. If a code is never used, remove it.

That keeps the list connected to the business instead of a generic CRM template.

## Separate fact from inference

The customer said, “We hired someone who could start Friday.” That supports “chose another provider.”

The salesperson left two voicemails and assumed price was the issue. That does not support “price or terms.” It supports “no contact made.”

Do not turn a guess into data.

Use a short evidence note:

- **Customer stated:** hired another provider for earlier availability.
- **System shows:** estimate promised Tuesday, sent Thursday.
- **Team inference:** customer may have stopped responding after delay.

The first two lines are facts. The third is analysis. Keeping them separate makes the later review more honest.

That statement points to the operating question behind the code. If “chose another provider” keeps appearing after slow response times, the next repair is not a new ad. It is a faster answer.

## Pair the code with timestamps

A reason without timing can still hide the leak.

For every lead, preserve:

- when the inquiry arrived
- when the first acknowledgement went out
- when the first human attempt happened
- when a conversation was confirmed
- when an estimate or next step was promised
- when that promise was completed
- when and why the opportunity closed

Reason codes complete the picture by recording why the opportunity stopped.

## Run the 25-lead loss audit

Pull the last twenty-five lost leads. Do not start with the whole database.

For each one, answer four questions:

1. Is there a specific reason code?
2. Is the code supported by a customer statement or system event?
3. Was the next step completed when promised?
4. Could one change have prevented or clarified the loss?

Count the six codes.

Then choose the most common preventable category and repair one part of it.

If “no contact made” dominates, verify phone and email fields and test the first response.

If “internal failure” dominates, add a named owner, deadline, and escalation.

If “not a fit” dominates, change the website so the wrong customer can disqualify the offer before filling out a form.

If “price or terms” dominates, make the offer, range, or financing limits clearer before the estimate.

Do not use the codes to punish one employee. Use them to inspect the entire path from promise to outcome.

## Make the field impossible to fake

The CRM should require the code before a lead is marked lost. It should not require a long essay.

Keep the note optional for self-explanatory outcomes and required for “other” or “internal failure.” Preserve who changed the status and when. Allow a manager to reopen a lead when the code is wrong.

The field should help the person doing the work, not turn into surveillance theater.

[A Form Error Should Tell the Lead What to Do Next](/posts/form-error-should-tell-lead-what-to-do-next) applies here too. If the CRM rejects a status change, tell the operator exactly what is missing.

If the lead path crosses a website, inbox, phone, CRM, calendar, and estimate process, [The Lead Flow Pro diagnostic](https://www.theleadflowpro.com/diagnostic) is a natural way to map where the evidence disappears.

Run the twenty-five-lead audit first.

The answer may be uncomfortable. It will still be more useful than “went cold.”
