If you have been on X in the last 48 hours, you have seen Venezuela at the top of the trends.

Venezuela. La Guaira. Caracas. The names trend without context for anyone who does not already know what happened.

Here is the context.

On the night of June 24th, two of the most powerful earthquakes in modern Venezuelan history struck seconds apart — a 7.2 followed immediately by a 7.5 — off the coast near La Guaira, a port city that sits between Caracas and the Caribbean. Buildings collapsed in the capital. A Twitch streamer captured the moment mid-broadcast, and the clip went viral on X. Pool water poured down the side of a high-rise. People ran into the streets.

https://x.com/WeatherMonitors/status/2069925746831986803

As of this morning, at least 235 people are confirmed dead. More than 4,000 are injured. La Guaira has been declared a disaster zone. International rescue teams are already on the ground.

This is not a political story. It is a human one. I want to talk about it as a human — and as someone who knows what it means when the people controlling the information are not on your side.

## Why La Guaira

La Guaira is Venezuela's entry point. It is where ships dock, where goods arrive, where the main international airport is located. It is also one of the most densely populated coastal corridors in the country.

When a 7.5 hits a city like that — seconds after a 7.2 that has already cracked foundations and shattered windows — you are not looking at a disaster with clean edges. You are looking at infrastructure damage in a place that was already stretched thin, in a country that has been under economic collapse for the better part of a decade.

The buildings that fell were not just buildings. They were hospitals. Schools. The apartments of families who had already survived years of hyperinflation, food shortage, and political repression.

A natural disaster does not pause for context. But context matters for how the rest of the world responds.

## What the Government There Is Not Telling You

I do not have sources inside Venezuela. I am not going to pretend I have eyes on the ground in Caracas.

What I can tell you is what open-source reporting confirms: Venezuela has one of the most controlled media environments on the planet. More than 200 websites are blocked. Independent journalism is not tolerated. The government's damage reports are the government's damage reports — and the government of Nicolás Maduro has a long, documented history of managing information to serve its own political survival.

When the "official" government count is 32 deaths, and Al Jazeera is reporting 188, and ABC News is reporting 235 — I do not assume the government is telling me the full truth. I have been inside a system that managed information to shape public perception. I know what it looks like from the inside.

The people of Venezuela cannot assume their government's count is honest. The people watching from the outside should not assume it either.

## What the Marine in Me Has to Say

I served. I know what it is to watch civilian infrastructure collapse in a disaster zone, to see communities try to survive something that the headlines do not fully capture, to understand the difference between the official briefing and what is actually on the ground.

I am not calling for military intervention. That is not my point.

My point is that the response to a human crisis — whether it is government overreach or a 7.5 earthquake — should not be partisan. People are under rubble right now. Children are under rubble right now. The politics of the Maduro government, the politics of U.S.-Venezuela relations, the politics of foreign aid — none of that should be your first question when you are watching video of a high-rise shedding pool water from its shattered windows.

Your first question should be: what can I do?

Trump has already pledged a rapid U.S. response. That pledge needs to be tracked against what actually arrives and when. Promised aid and delivered aid are two different numbers. Hold both accountable.

## What You Can Do Right Now

**First: pay attention.** Venezuela trending on X does not mean the story is getting sustained coverage. Most American news cycles move on within 48 hours of a disaster like this. The death toll is still rising. The rubble is still being cleared. Stay with it past the trending window.

**Second: give if you can.** The International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies has emergency response operations already deployed. Direct donations reach faster in the first 72 hours than they will in three weeks when the cameras are gone.

**Third: push for accountability on the information.** Ask your representatives what the U.S. government knows about the actual scope of the damage. Demand independent verification of both death tolls and aid delivery. A government that controls its own disaster numbers cannot be the only source.

**Fourth: share this.** Venezuela is trending because the algorithm noticed volume. That is not the same as sustained coverage. When the algorithm moves on, the crisis does not. Be the thing that keeps it in the conversation for longer than a news cycle.
