On June 8, 2026, U.S. District Judge Leo T. Sorokin of Massachusetts did something that triggered immediate reactions across X and every major newsroom in the country.

He threw out the Trump administration's $100,000 fee on new H-1B visa applications.

The administration had put this fee in place in September as part of its immigration enforcement strategy. The argument was simple: raising the cost of sponsoring a foreign worker would push companies to hire Americans instead.

Twenty states, led by California, filed suit. Judge Sorokin ruled that the fee functioned not as a regulatory charge but as a *tax* — and under the Constitution, a tax requires authorization from Congress. The President cannot impose a tax by executive action alone.

The White House says it intends to appeal.

X, predictably, exploded.

## What X Is Actually Arguing About

The reactions split fast, and both sides are making their case loud.

The anti-fee camp — technology companies, immigration attorneys, and states with large tech economies — is celebrating. They argued from day one that $100,000 per application would make it economically impossible for smaller companies to sponsor foreign workers. Only the largest corporations with bottomless legal and HR budgets could absorb that cost. It did not level the playing field. It handed the field to the biggest players.

The pro-fee camp — immigration restrictionists, domestic-worker advocates, and a vocal section of the Republican base — is furious at the ruling. Their argument is straightforward: American workers are competing against foreign workers who are cheaper to employ even with significant fees. The $100,000 number was a feature, not a bug. It was designed to be prohibitive.

Both camps are talking past each other on X. They almost always are.

## What the Ruling Actually Says

Judge Sorokin's ruling is not about whether the H-1B program is good policy. It is not a ruling about whether foreign workers take American jobs or whether tech companies import cheap labor. It is not a political opinion from a federal judge.

It is a structural constitutional argument.

The Taxing Clause of the Constitution gives Congress — not the President — the authority to levy taxes. When the executive branch imposes a fee that functions like a revenue-raising tax (rather than a regulatory charge that covers administrative costs), it oversteps its authority.

Sorokin found that the $100,000 fee did not reflect the actual cost of processing an H-1B petition. It was designed to generate revenue or discourage behavior. That makes it a tax. A tax requires an act of Congress.

You may hate the H-1B program. You may love it. This ruling does not resolve that debate. It says the method used to attack the program was unconstitutional.

Those are different things. X tends to collapse them into one.

## The Real Conversation We Are Not Having

The H-1B debate has been happening at full volume for years. The volume has never resolved anything because both sides are arguing from fundamentally different premises.

One side believes the H-1B program, as currently administered, enables corporate abuse: companies use it to import workers from abroad at lower wages, suppress domestic wages, and avoid training American workers. This is a real documented problem in specific industries, and it deserves serious policy attention.

The other side believes the program allows companies to hire the most qualified people in the world for specialized roles where domestic supply is genuinely thin. This is also real in specific sectors.

Both things can be true simultaneously. Badly administered programs get abused. Well-administered versions of the same program can serve a legitimate purpose.

A $100,000 fee solves neither problem. It does not fix the documented abuses. It does not protect domestic workers in meaningful structural ways. It is a blunt instrument applied to a complex problem.

Congress — which actually has the authority to restructure immigration law — has consistently failed to address the genuine concerns on both sides. Instead, administrations from both parties reach for executive action, get blocked by courts, and everyone declares victory or defeat based on the ruling.

The cycle continues.

## What Comes Next

The Trump administration will appeal. The case will move to the First Circuit. The underlying constitutional question about what counts as a tax versus a fee will receive further judicial attention.

In the meantime, the $100,000 fee is vacated. Applications proceed under the old structure.

If you are following this on X, the reactions over the next 48 hours will tell you something real about where each side of this debate actually stands — not on the H-1B program, but on whether they believe the Constitution's separation of powers matters when the outcome aligns with what they want.

Watch that carefully. People reveal a lot about their actual values when the rules produce outcomes they oppose.
