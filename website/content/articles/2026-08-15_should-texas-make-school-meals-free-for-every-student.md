*By the Real Ryan Nichols Editorial Team*

The school day asks children to pay attention, solve problems, control impulses, and learn things they did not know yesterday.

It also assumes they ate.

Texas does not have one statewide rule making breakfast and lunch free for every public-school student. Some students qualify because of household income or participation in assistance programs. Some attend schools that use the federal Community Eligibility Provision and serve meals at no charge to everyone. Other families pay full or reduced prices.

That creates a direct policy question at the start of another school year.

Should Texas treat a basic school breakfast and lunch like textbooks and classroom instruction, available to every student, or should public money remain targeted to families and campuses that meet need-based rules?

## What Texas students have now

The [National School Lunch Program](https://www.fns.usda.gov/nslp/national-school-lunch-program-nslp) is federally assisted and provides balanced, low-cost or free lunches through participating schools. The School Breakfast Program operates alongside it.

Families may apply for free or reduced-price meals under federal income rules. The [USDA's family guidance](https://www.fns.usda.gov/nslp/household) says children in households receiving SNAP automatically qualify, and participation in certain other assistance programs can also establish eligibility.

The other major route is the [Community Eligibility Provision](https://www.fns.usda.gov/cn/cep). CEP allows eligible high-poverty schools, groups of schools, or districts to serve breakfast and lunch at no cost to every enrolled student without collecting household meal applications. Federal reimbursement is calculated from the share of students directly certified through programs such as SNAP and TANF.

The Texas Department of Agriculture administers these programs in the state. Its SquareMeals system told eligible school authorities that the deadline to elect CEP for the 2026-27 program year was June 30, 2026.

So universal meals already exist in some Texas schools. They do not exist as a guaranteed statewide benefit for every student.

## The strongest case for universal meals

The simplest argument is that a child should not have to prove poverty in the lunch line.

If the state requires students to be in school, supporters say the state should provide the basic fuel needed to participate in the day. The same meal for every student removes the visible divide between who qualified, who paid, whose application is missing, and whose family income changed last month.

A universal model can also remove paperwork. Families do not have to complete a separate income application for meals, and school staff spend less time tracking eligibility and unpaid balances. CEP already uses that simplified model in eligible communities.

There is a working-family argument too. A household can earn too much for free meals and still feel every grocery increase, rent payment, utility bill, and morning time crunch. Universal meals would reach families who are not poor on paper but do not have much margin in real life.

Supporters can also say that access is not the same as waste. Schools can track participation, improve menus, and adjust production without making a child carry the financial test.

## The strongest case against it

Universal does not mean free. It means the cost moves.

Federal reimbursement already covers much of the cost for eligible students and CEP campuses. A statewide promise to every student would require Texas to pay whatever federal programs and local revenue do not cover.

Opponents can reasonably ask whether that is the best use of limited education dollars. Money spent subsidizing meals for families able to pay cannot also be spent on teachers, special education, transportation, campus security, or classroom materials.

There is also a local-control argument. A high-need rural district, a fast-growing suburban district, and a small charter school may have different participation levels, kitchen capacity, food vendors, and staffing. One statewide mandate could create costs that look very different from place to place.

Critics may prefer a targeted approach: automatically enroll every eligible child, expand CEP where the numbers work, erase meal debt for families in hardship, and make applications easier without paying for every meal statewide.

They can also demand that the debate include food quality and waste. A universal tray has little value if students will not eat it or if schools lack the staff and equipment to serve it well.

## Editorial analysis: make the meal part of the school day

Texas should provide a basic breakfast and lunch to every public-school student who wants one.

That does not require pretending the cost disappears. The Legislature should publish the expected state share, preserve federal reimbursements, measure participation and waste, and give districts enough flexibility to buy and prepare food responsibly.

The line should be universal. The accounting should be strict.

Targeted programs make sense when the benefit is separate from the public institution. School meals happen inside a compulsory school day. We already accept that a child does not bring a household income statement before receiving a worksheet, using a library book, or sitting at a desk.

Meals should be treated with the same practical logic.

That position does not settle every education question. Texas still has to decide how it measures results, how much local control matters, and what families should be able to choose. Our recent article on [A-through-F school ratings](https://realryannichols.com/posts/should-texas-keep-grading-public-schools-a-through-f) asks whether clarity becomes distortion when too much is compressed into one symbol.

The debates over [full-time virtual public schools](https://realryannichols.com/posts/should-texas-move-faster-on-full-time-virtual-public-schools) and [student phone restrictions](https://realryannichols.com/posts/should-texas-schools-lock-student-phones-away-all-day) raise the same larger tension: what should the state guarantee, and what should a local community decide?
