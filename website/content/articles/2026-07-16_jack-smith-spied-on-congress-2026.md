It started trending on X this week with a single line from Senator Josh Hawley: Jack Smith's sworn testimony "looked like perjury" in light of what the documents actually show.

That's not a pundit's hot take. That's a sitting U.S. Senator, on the record, about a former Special Counsel of the Department of Justice.

Here's what got him there.

## What the documents say

Senate Judiciary Chairman Chuck Grassley and Senator Ron Johnson released DOJ records this week showing that Special Counsel Jack Smith's investigative team obtained and reviewed the contents of text messages tied to 44 members of Congress — reportedly around 20 senators and 20 House Republicans, plus a handful of Democrats — pulled from White House-era custodial records rather than seized directly from lawmakers' own phones or carriers.

The problem: Smith had already told Congress, under oath, that his team did not request the content of those messages. Grassley's letter says otherwise. Hawley says the gap between the testimony and the paper trail is wide enough to warrant a perjury investigation. Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche has said DOJ will look into it.

## Why I can't write about this from a distance

I've spent years now living on the receiving end of decisions made inside the January 6 investigative machine — decisions I didn't get to see coming, records I had to fight to get access to, a system that moved fast when it wanted something from me and slow when I wanted something from it.

I'm not saying Jack Smith's team did anything to me personally. I don't have evidence of that, and I'm not going to invent it. What I am saying is this: the entire argument I've made for years — that the process deserves way more sunlight than it's gotten — doesn't rest on my case alone. It rests on whether the people running these investigations play it straight with the branch of government whose job is to oversee them.

If a Special Counsel's team reviewed message content belonging to sitting senators and representatives, then told Congress under oath that it hadn't, that's not a partisan talking point. That's the oversight function itself getting lied to. Every defendant who ever had their case built by that same office has a right to ask: what else got represented one way to Congress and handled another way behind closed doors?

## Document, don't assume

I want to be precise about what's confirmed and what isn't.

Confirmed: DOJ released records showing message content tied to 44 members of Congress was reviewed by Smith's team. Confirmed: Smith testified under oath that his team hadn't sought that content. Confirmed: Hawley has publicly called for a perjury referral, and DOJ leadership has said it will look into it.

Not yet confirmed: any final legal determination that perjury occurred. That's a process that has to run its course, and I'm not going to pretend the outcome is decided just because the headline is explosive.

That's the whole difference between documenting something and mob-posting about it. I deal in records. This record is still being written.

## What I'd ask you to do with this

Read the primary letter yourself instead of taking any commentator's word for it — mine included. Ask your own representatives, regardless of party, whether they think a Special Counsel's office should face real consequences for misleading the body that oversees it. And watch who in Congress treats this as a five-alarm fire only when it's the other party's ox getting gored.

Oversight that only works one direction isn't oversight. It's theater.
