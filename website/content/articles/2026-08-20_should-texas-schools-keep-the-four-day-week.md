*By the Real Ryan Nichols Editorial Team*

The four-day school week is no longer a strange experiment at the edge of Texas education.

It has become a real operating choice, especially for rural districts trying to recruit teachers without matching the salaries available in larger systems.

The attraction is easy to understand. A three-day weekend can be a valuable benefit. Districts may gain planning time, reduce some absences, and offer families a schedule they come to prefer.

The warning is also real. Fewer days can mean less total time in school, child-care problems on the fifth day, missed meals, longer classroom days, and weaker academic results.

Texas does not need a slogan about this question. It needs an honest standard for deciding when a four-day calendar is helping and when it is costing students too much.

*Texas statutes, Texas Education Agency guidance, and the state campus analysis cited here were verified August 20, 2026.*

## What Texas law requires

[Texas Education Code Section 25.081](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/GetStatute.aspx?Code=ED&Value=25.081) requires a school district to operate for at least 75,600 minutes each school year, including instruction, intermissions, and recesses, unless another authorized provision applies.

That minute-based requirement gives districts room to arrange the calendar in different ways. A four-day district may use longer school days, a longer year, occasional fifth days, or a hybrid calendar to reach its required time.

The label alone does not tell parents how much instruction a child receives.

The Texas Education Agency has also tightened one part of the tradeoff. Its current [staff development waiver guidance](https://tea.texas.gov/school-and-district-information/waivers/state-waivers/state-waiver-types-expedited) says districts and charter schools operating on a four-day week are not eligible for the staff development waiver that can substitute professional learning time for student instruction.

The state is allowing calendar flexibility, but it is not treating a shorter week as permission to keep removing instructional time.

## What the Texas academic analysis found

TEA published a [February 2025 campus analysis](https://tea.texas.gov/data-reports/program-evaluations/program-evaluations-research-reports/4dsw2022-23.pdf) comparing students at four-day campuses with matched students at five-day campuses during the 2022-23 school year.

The agency identified 137 four-day campuses representing 76 districts. After matching campuses and students on relevant characteristics, the analysis included 21,260 students at four-day campuses and 70,673 students at five-day campuses.

Students at four-day campuses had lower performance in grades 6 through 8 reading language arts and grades 4 through 6 mathematics. The share meeting grade level was 6 to 8 percentage points higher in reading at five-day campuses and 5 to 8 points higher in mathematics.

Those differences remained statistically significant after controlling for prior-year achievement.

That is a serious warning. It is not a final verdict.

TEA also listed important limits. The agency had to estimate which campuses used four-day calendars because districts did not register under a standard statewide definition. Calendar designs varied. Some campuses had used the model for one year and others for longer. Post-pandemic testing disruptions limited long-term analysis. Unmeasured factors, including preexisting difficulty finding qualified teachers, may have affected the results.

The responsible reading is not “four days always fail.” It is “Texas found a measurable risk that every district must answer.”

## The strongest case for local four-day calendars

The strongest case begins with staffing.

A rural district cannot teach children well if it cannot recruit and keep capable teachers. When salary competition is limited by a small tax base and a tight budget, time can become part of compensation.

A four-day week may also reduce commuting days for families spread across large geographic areas. Teachers can use a fifth day for planning, appointments, second jobs, or family responsibilities. Older students may use it for work, career training, college courses, or targeted tutoring.

Local control matters because Texas districts are not interchangeable. A calendar built for a large suburban system may not fit a small East Texas district where transportation distances, staffing, and community routines are different.

The model can also be designed responsibly. The TEA analysis notes research finding no significant achievement difference when four-day systems preserved adequate total time by extending the day or year. Some districts offer meals, child care, enrichment, or academic help on the fifth day.

The best argument is not that every district should switch. It is that a local board should be able to test a calendar that solves a local staffing problem, provided it publishes the results.

## The strongest case against the shorter week

The strongest case against it begins with the purpose of school.

A recruiting benefit is not enough if students learn less.

Longer days do not automatically replace the attention and practice lost when a full day disappears. Younger students may tire. Families may need to pay for care. Students who depend on school meals or a safe supervised place can bear a cost that is invisible in a staff survey.

The fifth day can also widen inequality. A family with transportation, internet access, and money for activities can turn Friday into enrichment. Another family may have no safe option beyond leaving an older sibling responsible for younger children.

The calendar can become difficult to reverse. Once employees and families arrange their lives around the three-day weekend, a district may keep the schedule even if the academic evidence turns negative.

Texas already has a state analysis showing lower results in several grades and subjects. Ignoring that finding in the name of local control would turn flexibility into avoidance.

## Editorial analysis: allow the choice, require the receipts

Texas should not impose one calendar on every district.

It should require a public test before a four-day calendar continues year after year.

Each participating district should publish a simple annual dashboard with:

1. Total instructional minutes compared with the prior calendar.
2. Teacher vacancies, applicant numbers, and retention.
3. Student growth and grade-level performance by campus and student group.
4. Absences, discipline, and course failures.
5. Family access to meals, transportation, child care, tutoring, and career programs on the fifth day.
6. The total cost or savings, including fifth-day services.

If the schedule improves staffing and maintains student progress, the district has evidence to continue.

If learning falls, the district should add instructional time, redesign the fifth day, or return to five days.

That is the same facts-first principle behind [Should Texas Keep Grading Public Schools A Through F?](https://realryannichols.com/posts/should-texas-keep-grading-public-schools-a-through-f). A label cannot replace the underlying information.

It also connects to [Should Texas Require Middle and High Schools to Start at 8:30 or Later?](https://realryannichols.com/posts/should-texas-require-middle-and-high-schools-to-start-at-830-or-later). A schedule may offer a real benefit while moving costs onto transportation, activities, staff, and families.

## The question

Texas has enough experience to move beyond guessing.

Districts should not lose flexibility simply because one calendar is traditional. They also should not keep a popular schedule without proving that students are still receiving the time and support they need.

Should Texas districts keep the freedom to operate four-day school weeks, or should the state require five days unless a district proves the shorter calendar is not hurting students?
