Iran just closed the Strait of Hormuz again.

On Saturday, June 21, the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps declared the waterway shut — for the second time in a matter of weeks. The stated reason: Israeli attacks on Lebanon and Washington's failure to rein them in, which Iran says violates the terms of the recently extended ceasefire deal.

X lit up immediately.

`#HormuzClosed` was trending within hours. Energy traders, geopolitical analysts, veterans, and everyday Americans were posting everything from oil price charts to "this is how it starts."

They are not wrong to pay attention.

## What the Strait of Hormuz is and why it matters to you

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway between Iran and Oman at the mouth of the Persian Gulf. At its narrowest navigable point, it is approximately 21 miles wide.

Every day, roughly **20 percent of the world's oil and liquefied natural gas** moves through it.

That is not a minor supply line. That is the jugular vein of the global energy market. When it gets pinched, prices move. When it gets closed, economies notice.

According to maritime intelligence firm Windward, ship transits through the strait dropped from 35 crossings on Saturday to just **12 on Sunday** after Iran's announcement. Al Jazeera reported that shipping had stalled across the area.

## The dispute over whether it's actually closed

There is a significant gap between what Iran is saying and what the US is saying.

Iran's IRGC declared the waterway shut. US Central Command pushed back within hours, stating that safe passage through the Strait "remains intact" and that 55 merchant ships transited on Saturday alone.

Those two statements cannot both be fully true at the same time.

What appears to be happening: Iran is enforcing a de facto closure through threat and signaling — not a physical naval blockade. Ships are choosing not to move rather than being physically stopped. The effect on energy markets is real regardless of which statement is technically accurate.

CNBC reported Monday that energy traders and shipping companies are watching closely for further escalation.

## How we got here

This is not the first closure. According to reporting on the broader 2026 Strait of Hormuz crisis, shipping through the strait has been disrupted since late February 2026, when US and Israeli forces launched operations against Iran and Iran's supreme leader was killed.

A 60-day ceasefire extension was supposed to create a pathway to a permanent peace deal. US and Iranian negotiators met in Switzerland as recently as Sunday, June 22, trying to hold that framework together.

Then Netanyahu ordered strikes on a Hezbollah position in Beirut. Iran called it a violation. The strait closed again.

It had just reopened. Now it is closed again. That is not a sign of a situation that is stabilizing.

## What this means at the pump

If the Hormuz closure extends — or escalates into a genuine physical blockade — oil markets will react hard. A 20 percent disruption to global supply creates immediate upward pressure on benchmark crude prices, which filters through to fuel costs at the pump within weeks.

We are in a period of active military confrontation, ceasefire negotiation breakdown, and contested control of the world's most critical oil shipping lane. That combination has historically ended at the pump.
