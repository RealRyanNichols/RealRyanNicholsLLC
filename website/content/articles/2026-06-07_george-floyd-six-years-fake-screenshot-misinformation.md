George Floyd was murdered on May 25, 2020.

This week marked six years since a Minneapolis police officer kneeled on his neck for nine minutes and twenty-nine seconds while bystanders filmed it and begged for it to stop.

Six years. And the conversation about who Floyd was — what he represented, what his death meant for this country — is still being fought on social media. Still being distorted. Still being weaponized.

This week, a viral fake screenshot spread across X purporting to show a post by Floyd — dated years before the account even existed — making inflammatory statements that he never said and could not have said.

It was fabricated. Fact-checkers confirmed it quickly. The account handle it was attributed to was not created until more than two years after Floyd died, and the post carried a date from over a decade before the account existed.

It was not even a sophisticated forgery. It just did not matter. It spread anyway.

## Why Fake Posts About the Dead Are Especially Dangerous

There is a specific cruelty in fabricating statements from people who cannot defend themselves.

When someone puts words in the mouth of a living person, that person can respond. They can post a denial, go on camera, show documentation. The lie can be contested in real time by the person being lied about.

George Floyd cannot do that. He has been dead for six years.

And that is exactly the point.

When someone creates a fake post attributed to him — designed to make him look like a hypocrite, or a bad person, or someone whose death "wasn't worth what it became" — they are counting on the fact that the lie will travel faster than the correction. They are counting on the reality that most people who see the screenshot will not read the fact-check. They will just absorb the image, the name, and the message.

And they will carry it forward, consciously or not.

This is not a neutral act. This is character assassination of a dead man who became the center of a national reckoning about police violence and racial justice. And the timing — on the anniversary of his death, as Minneapolis Mayor Jacob Frey was posting remembrances and the nation's attention returned briefly to Floyd's legacy — was not accidental.

## What the X Reaction Looked Like

The fake screenshot moved fast. It got retweeted and quote-posted by accounts looking to use it as evidence of something — exactly what the person who created it hoped it would do.

The corrections followed, but they always follow at a slower pace than the original lie. That is the physics of misinformation on platforms like X. Outrage travels faster than clarification. Content that confirms what people already believe spreads faster than content that challenges it.

By the time [Snopes](https://www.snopes.com), fact-checkers, and the original reporters caught up, thousands of people had already seen the fake. Many shared it sincerely. Many others shared it knowing it might be fake, because it served a point they wanted to make.

That is the worst part. Not the people who were fooled — they are victims. It is the people who do not particularly care whether it is real, as long as it is useful.

## What I Think About This

I have been very public about my own experiences with misinformation and platform manipulation. I have had my words twisted, taken out of context, and used as the basis for coordinated harassment campaigns. I know what it feels like to be in a fight against a narrative that has already left the station before you even knew a train was coming.

George Floyd does not get to fight that fight. His family does. His memory does.

And his memory matters — not because every policy debate that grew out of his death has been resolved cleanly, not because every protest that followed was peaceful, not because there are no legitimate discussions to be had about policing reform and its implementation.

His memory matters because a human being was killed on camera in a way that shook the conscience of the country. Because his name became a shorthand for a conversation America had been avoiding for a long time. Because a lot of people who had never felt the weight of that conversation suddenly had to sit with it.

You can disagree with how the aftermath was handled. You can debate the policy outcomes. Those are real conversations with real stakes.

But you do not have to fabricate posts from a dead man to make your argument. If your position cannot stand on real facts, that is a problem with your position.

## The Anniversary We Should Actually Be Having

Six years after George Floyd's murder, the most honest version of this anniversary is complicated.

Some things changed. Laws were passed in some states. Police departments reformed some practices. The phrase "I can't breathe" became a cultural marker with real weight.

Some things did not change. Incidents of police violence against Black Americans have continued. The political will to pass comprehensive federal reform stalled and never recovered. The national conversation that briefly felt urgent in 2020 has been slowly buried under the weight of political exhaustion and short attention spans.

And now, instead of a real reckoning on the anniversary of his death, part of what is trending on X this week is a fake post with his name on it.

I find that heartbreaking and infuriating in equal measure.

The fake screenshot is not just misinformation. It is a signal about where we are. It is someone saying: *the truth about who this man was is not enough for what we want to do with his memory.* So we will rewrite it.

That is not acceptable. Not on the anniversary. Not ever.

## What You Can Do Right Now

If you see that fake screenshot — or any fake screenshot attributed to George Floyd — report it. Do not share it. Respond to the person sharing it with the fact-check. The correction matters even if only a few people see it.

Misinformation spreads because we let it move unchallenged. It does not have to.
