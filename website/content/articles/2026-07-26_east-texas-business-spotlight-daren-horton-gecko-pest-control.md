*By the Real Ryan Nichols Editorial Team*  
*Last verified: July 26, 2026*

MARSHALL, Texas — Pest control is easy to notice when something has gone wrong. The better work is often quieter: inspecting a building carefully, explaining what was found, choosing an appropriate response, documenting the visit, and helping a customer keep the problem from returning.

That is the practical lane occupied by **Gecko Pest Control**, a locally owned company based in Marshall with a second published location in Longview. Current public records identify **Daren G. Horton** as an owner and principal contact. A 2024 company case study from software provider FieldRoutes identifies **Becki and Daren Horton** as the owners.

The company's official website lists residential and commercial services for ants, bed bugs, spiders, roaches, rodents, fleas and ticks, mosquitoes, stinging insects, and termites. It also emphasizes inspection, property-specific treatment, prevention, and communication rather than a one-size-fits-all visit.

The constructive story is bigger than removing pests. A Greater Marshall Chamber of Commerce record documents that Horton donated a year of pest-control service to the Trifecta Place Teen Advocacy Center after learning the center needed help. That is a useful example of a skilled business contributing what it actually knows how to provide.

## The licensing record—and how customers can verify it

Structural pest-control work is regulated in Texas.

The [Texas Department of Agriculture](https://texasagriculture.gov/Regulatory-Programs/Pesticides/Structural-Pest-Control-Service) says its Structural Pest Control Service licenses and regulates professionals who apply pesticides in and around structures. Its [current-license reports page](https://texasagriculture.gov/Regulatory-Programs/Pesticides/Structural-Pest-Control-Service/Structural-Pest-Control-Reports-Current-Licenses) provides downloadable business, applicator, technician, and apprentice lists.

A search of the current commercial-business report returned Gecko Pest Control entries in Harrison and Gregg counties under Daren Horton. BBB separately lists a structural-pest-control license record for the business with a May 31, 2027 expiration date.

The company's website footer publishes another license number. Because public listings can use different identifiers for a business, branch, responsible certified applicator, or reporting system, this article does not declare the numbers interchangeable.

A careful customer can:

1. search the live TDA commercial-business report;
2. ask the company for the business license and the name of the responsible certified applicator;
3. ask what credential the person performing the service holds;
4. confirm that the proposed work falls within the relevant license category; and
5. contact TDA if a record appears expired, duplicated, or inconsistent.

That is healthy consumer practice for any pest-control provider. It is not an accusation against Gecko.

## What other owners can learn from the model

### Teach customers how the work is evaluated

Pest control involves decisions most customers cannot observe directly.

An owner earns trust by explaining:

- what evidence was found;
- whether the target is confirmed or only suspected;
- where activity is concentrated;
- what can be corrected without chemicals;
- what product or method is proposed;
- what preparation is required;
- what follow-up is included; and
- what would count as success.

The company's official process emphasizes inspection and explanation. That is a sound business principle even apart from any single provider.

### Turn customer feedback into an operating system

The FieldRoutes case study says Gecko adopted new communication and routing software and increased its emphasis on asking customers how the company performed.

Because that account comes from the software vendor, this article does not treat its revenue, staffing, or review-growth figures as independently audited facts.

The operational lesson remains useful: asking “How did we do?” only matters if someone reviews the answer, identifies a pattern, and changes the work.

A small business can build a simple version:

1. ask one consistent question after service;
2. separate praise from repairable problems;
3. assign a person to respond;
4. track repeat issues by technician, service type, or location; and
5. tell the customer what was corrected.

### Donate the skill, not just the logo

The Trifecta Place example shows a practical form of community support.

A plumber can repair a nonprofit's leak. A printer can produce event materials. A landscaper can restore an overgrown entrance. A bookkeeper can help an organization set up a basic reporting system.

The work should be freely agreed upon, properly scoped, and honestly documented. A donation should not become leverage over the recipient or a substitute for fair wages and safe practices.

## A practical customer checklist

### Before the inspection

Write down:

- where the activity was seen;
- the date and time;
- photographs or video, if safe;
- whether children, pets, food, gardens, aquariums, or sensitive equipment are present;
- previous treatments;
- known leaks, crawl-space issues, or entry gaps; and
- any deadline, such as a real-estate inspection or health review.

Do not touch an unknown animal, nest, chemical, or contaminated material simply to collect evidence.

### During the estimate

Ask:

- What pest or evidence did you confirm?
- Is this one-time service, recurring service, exclusion work, or a separate termite program?
- What areas are included and excluded?
- What preparation must the customer complete?
- What product or nonchemical method is proposed?
- What re-entry, ventilation, food, pet, and aquarium precautions apply?
- How many visits are included?
- What triggers an additional charge?
- Is there a guarantee, and what are its written limits?
- How can the agreement be canceled?

Get pricing, scope, renewal terms, and guarantee language in writing.

### After service

Keep the service report and follow the preparation and prevention instructions. If activity continues, document the date and location rather than relying only on memory.

For a recurring plan, periodically ask whether the service still matches the actual pest pressure. More treatment is not automatically better; the goal is appropriate control with clear reasons for each step.

### If the problem involves termites

Ask whether the visit is a general pest inspection, a wood-destroying-insect inspection, or a treatment proposal. Those are not the same thing.

For a real-estate transaction, confirm which form and credential the lender, insurer, buyer, seller, or inspector requires. Do not assume a routine household service visit satisfies a transaction-specific inspection requirement.

## Editorial perspective

Gecko Pest Control is a useful East Texas Business Spotlight because the record connects three kinds of constructive work: a regulated technical service, an effort to make that service understandable to customers, and a documented donation that helped a local youth organization maintain its space.

The strongest part of the story is not an award or a marketing slogan. It is the repeatable method:

**Inspect carefully. Explain plainly. Follow up. Use the trade to help the community.**

That standard is useful whether a customer hires Gecko or another licensed provider. It gives residents a better way to compare companies, and it gives other local owners a model for turning specialized knowledge into public value.
