In February 2026, Jack Dorsey posted on X and set the business world on fire.

Not because of a product launch. Not because of a scandal. Because of a single, chilling admission: *Block was cutting nearly 4,000 jobs — almost half its entire workforce — and AI was why.*

Here is what he wrote, and it has not stopped ringing in my ears since:

> *"I believe most companies will reach the same conclusion and make similar structural changes. I'd rather get there honestly and on our own terms than be forced into it reactively."*

Most companies. Within a year. Structural changes.

That is not a CEO talking about efficiency. That is a CEO giving you a preview of the future — whether you asked for one or not.

## What Actually Happened at Block

Block, the fintech company behind Square and Cash App, announced it was slashing its workforce from over 10,000 employees down to just under 6,000. That is not a trim. That is a gut.

And Dorsey's explanation was deliberately, almost brutally honest: *the business is strong, gross profit is growing*, and AI tools have simply changed what it takes to run a company at scale.

Block's stock went up 24% on the news.

Let that sink in. The market *rewarded* a company for firing half its workforce because investors believe the AI-driven model is more profitable. The workers who lost their jobs are collateral damage in a spreadsheet that Wall Street is celebrating.

## The "Doom Loop" That Went Viral

This announcement did not happen in a vacuum. Around the same time, a research note from Citrini titled *"The 2028 Global Intelligence Crisis"* went massively viral on X.

The premise: if white-collar AI displacement accelerates at the rate Dorsey is describing, you get a feedback loop. Displaced workers spend less. Companies selling to consumers see revenue drop. They lay off *more* workers. Consumer spending contracts further. And so on.

It is not science fiction. It is basic macroeconomics applied to an unprecedented technological disruption.

The post was a thought experiment — but X treated it like a warning shot. Thousands of replies from engineers, marketers, analysts, and managers asking the same uncomfortable question: *Am I next?*

## The People Pushing Back

It would be easy to write this as a pure horror story, but the truth is more complicated.

Some of Block's own former employees pushed back hard. Naoko Takeda, a data scientist from Cash App, posted on LinkedIn saying the AI tools at Block were nowhere near capable enough to justify eliminating that many specialized roles. Her post also went viral — a reminder that corporate narratives about AI capabilities are often ahead of actual AI capabilities.

BCG research released around the same time landed on a more measured conclusion: over the next two to three years, 50–55% of US jobs will be *reshaped* by AI — not necessarily eliminated. The distinction matters. Task automation is not the same as job elimination. Most roles will change. Far fewer will disappear entirely.

And as of early 2026, even the most advanced AI agent being tested — Claude Opus 4.6 Cowork — was completing complicated remote tasks at a rate of just 4.17% at human-level standards.

That is not a workforce replacement. That is a very powerful assistant.

## What I Think Is Actually Happening

I will be honest with you: I think Dorsey is right about the direction and wrong about the timeline.

AI is going to displace a significant number of white-collar jobs. That is coming. But the "within a year, most companies will do the same" framing is designed to serve a narrative — the narrative that makes investors comfortable with the decision Block already made.

The reality is that most companies are not run by founders with Dorsey's risk tolerance and public positioning. Most CFOs are not going to announce a 40% headcount cut and credit it to AI unless the tools are actually delivering. Right now, for most businesses, they are not there yet.

But *eventually* is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

The question is not *if* this disruption is coming. The question is *how fast* and *who absorbs the cost*.

History says the workers absorb the cost. Capital captures the gain. That is the pattern, and nothing I have read about this current AI cycle gives me reason to believe this cycle will be different.

## The Harder Question Nobody Is Asking

Every article about AI and jobs focuses on the jobs being lost. Almost none of them focus on *who owns the systems doing the replacing*.

If AI tools replace 10% of white-collar labor, and those tools are owned by three companies in San Francisco and Seattle, then productivity gains accrue almost entirely to shareholders in those companies while the displaced workforce gets a 60-day severance package and a LinkedIn recommendation.

That is not progress distributed. That is productivity extracted.

I am not anti-technology. I am not anti-AI. I use these tools and I find them genuinely useful. But I think we need to be clear-eyed about who wins and who loses in this transition — and stop letting corporate press releases do our economic analysis for us.
