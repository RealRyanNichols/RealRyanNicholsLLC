A resident walks outside for work and finds an empty parking space.

The car was not stolen. It was towed from the apartment property where the resident lives.

Maybe the permit fell from the mirror. Maybe the registration changed. Maybe the car sat in the wrong numbered space. Maybe the tow was lawful and the resident missed a sign.

Texas already regulates private-property towing. The question is whether signs are enough in a place where the property owner often has the resident's name, vehicle information, phone number, and email address.

Should Texas require one direct warning before an apartment complex orders a routine tow of a resident's registered vehicle?

## The verified starting point

The [Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/towing/consumerinfo.htm) says cars parked in a tow-away zone or on property without permission may be towed at the owner's expense. It explains several consumer rights after a private-property tow, including the right to request a tow hearing in a Justice of the Peace court within 14 days if the owner believes the vehicle was wrongfully towed, stored, booted, or overcharged.

Texas also sets detailed requirements for many [tow-away signs](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/towing/compliance/tow/signage.htm). TDLR says signs must be conspicuously visible at entrances, include the towing symbol and enforcement language, explain who may park, state enforcement hours, and provide a 24-hour number for locating a vehicle.

After a private-property tow reaches a vehicle storage facility, the facility must notify local law enforcement within two hours and provide identifying and location information, according to the state's [vehicle storage notification guidance](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/towing/compliance/vsf/notifications.htm).

Those rules create notice, recovery, and challenge procedures. They do not amount to a universal promise that every apartment resident will receive a text, call, email, or door notice immediately before every tow.

## The strongest case for a direct warning

The best argument for a warning is proportionality.

Towing a car can disrupt work, child care, medical appointments, and family logistics. The owner may face a towing charge, daily storage costs, transportation expenses, and lost time. TDLR currently lists a statewide maximum of $272 for a private-property tow of a vehicle weighing up to 10,000 pounds, although local limits may be lower.

When an apartment manager already knows the vehicle belongs to a resident, a short warning can solve many nonemergency problems without moving the car at all.

“Your permit is not visible. Move or correct the vehicle by 8 a.m.” is cheaper than a tow, faster than a court hearing, and more likely to fix the underlying parking issue.

A direct notice would also create a timestamped record. The property could show that it warned the resident. The resident could not honestly claim the first notice came from an empty parking space.

## The strongest case against a universal warning

Some vehicles need to move now.

A car blocking a fire lane, accessible space, gate, loading zone, hydrant, travel lane, or another resident's assigned space can create immediate safety or access problems. A mandatory delay could leave the property unable to protect people who followed the rules.

Managers may not know who owns every car. Plates change. Guests arrive. Contact information goes stale. A resident may register one vehicle and park another. A text can fail without the property knowing it failed.

There is also a fair argument that conspicuous signs and lease terms already provide notice. Requiring a customized warning for every violation could create administrative cost, encourage repeat violations, and produce new disputes over whether a message was sent, received, or sufficiently clear.

The strongest opposition is not a defense of surprise. It is a warning that one rigid statewide procedure may fit a fallen permit but fail badly in a blocked fire lane.

## Ryan's Take

*The following is editorial analysis from the Real Ryan Nichols Editorial Team, not a claim that Ryan supplied a personal statement for this article.*

Texas should require one direct warning before a routine apartment tow when three facts are present:

1. the property can reasonably identify the vehicle as belonging to a current resident
2. the violation does not create an immediate safety or access problem
3. the resident has not received a warning for the same issue within a defined period

The rule should allow delivery by text, email, resident portal, or a weather-resistant notice on the vehicle. It should state the violation, the deadline to correct it, and the number to call with a question.

It should not delay a tow from a fire lane, accessible space without authorization, blocked gate, travel lane, hydrant, or another location where waiting causes a real problem. It should not protect an abandoned, dangerous, or clearly unauthorized vehicle indefinitely.

The point is not to make parking rules optional.

The point is to make the consequence useful. If a six-hour warning gets the permit fixed and the space cleared, the parking rule worked without creating a second crisis.

## What residents can do under current rules

Until any law changes, residents can reduce confusion with a short parking record:

- photograph the current parking permit in the place where it must be displayed
- save the lease page and parking addendum that describe towing rules
- update the property when the plate, vehicle, phone number, or email changes
- photograph the entrance signs and assigned space numbers
- call the number on the posted sign if the vehicle is missing
- request the tow ticket and keep every receipt
- review TDLR's consumer guidance promptly if the tow appears improper

A tow hearing deadline can move faster than an angry phone call. TDLR says the request must be made within 14 days of the tow. Anyone facing a real dispute should read the current rules and seek qualified legal advice when needed.
