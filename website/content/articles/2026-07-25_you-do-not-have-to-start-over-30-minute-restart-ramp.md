*By the Real Ryan Nichols Editorial Team*

An interruption can make yesterday’s progress feel farther away than it is.

The project is still there. The documents still exist. The phone call still needs to be made. The plan may still be sound.

But the working memory is gone.

You no longer remember which version was current, what decision came next, why one option was rejected, or where you stopped. The first few minutes back feel awkward. You open the file, look around, and realize you have to reconstruct the road before you can move down it.

That feeling creates a dangerous sentence:

> “I guess I have to start over.”

Usually, you do not.

You may need to recover context. You may need to update the plan because something changed. You may need to repair a mistake. You may even need to admit that part of the old approach no longer works.

But none of those things automatically erases the useful work already completed.

Today’s exercise is a **30-Minute Restart Ramp**. It is built for a responsibility that mattered, got interrupted, and now feels difficult to re-enter.

The goal is not to finish the entire project in half an hour.

The goal is to move from **disconnected** to **working again**.

## A pause is an event, not an identity

Life interrupts work for ordinary and serious reasons.

A customer calls. A child needs attention. A meeting runs long. Equipment fails. Money gets tight. A health concern changes the week. An emergency takes priority. Another person does not deliver what was promised. You become overwhelmed, make a poor decision, or simply run out of energy.

The interruption may be your fault, someone else’s fault, no one’s fault, or a mixture.

Responsibility still matters. If you missed a commitment, the honest response may include an apology, a correction, a new deadline, or a consequence. A restart method should never be used to avoid accountability.

But accountability is not the same as declaring all previous effort worthless.

“I stopped” describes what happened.

“I am incapable of finishing anything” turns one event into a permanent identity.

Those are not the same statement.

The first can lead to a decision. The second often leads to shame, hiding, and another lost day.

A practical restart begins with facts:

- What was completed?
- What remains usable?
- What changed during the pause?
- What is the next honest action now?

That is enough to build a ramp back into the work.

## Choose one responsibility worth returning to

Pick one paused responsibility that still matters.

It should be specific enough to name and small enough to touch today.

Good examples:

- Finish and send the proposal that is half written.
- Return to the application that is missing two sections.
- Reopen the budget and reconcile one account.
- Resume the repair after getting the correct part.
- Continue the course or certification you paused.
- Organize the evidence or records for one defined issue.
- Reconnect with a customer, partner, or friend after a delay.
- Return to a neglected spiritual practice with one passage and one prayer.
- Resume the first usable version of a website, lesson, product, or service.

Avoid choosing “fix everything,” “catch up on life,” or “get my whole business back on track.”

Those may be real needs, but they do not identify a re-entry point.

Write:

> **The responsibility I am returning to is:** __________

Then set a timer for 30 minutes.

## Minutes 0–5: Recover the last true point

Do not begin by reading every note, email, message, or file connected to the project.

Find the **last true point**.

That is the most recent part of the work you can verify as complete or accurate.

Examples:

- The proposal has an approved scope, but the price and delivery date are unfinished.
- The application’s first three sections are complete, but the supporting document was never uploaded.
- The repair was diagnosed, but the replacement part still needs to be installed.
- The customer agreed to review numbers, but no follow-up time was set.
- The lesson outline exists, but the first exercise is missing.
- The records are downloaded, but they have not been sorted by date.

Use evidence, not mood.

Open the latest reliable file. Look at the last sent message. Check the most recent dated note. Inspect the physical work safely. Confirm what actually exists.

Then write one sentence:

> **The last true point is:** __________

This sentence prevents two common mistakes.

The first is restarting too far back and wasting completed work.

The second is pretending you are farther along than the evidence shows.

A restart ramp must begin on solid ground.

## Minutes 5–10: Build the four-line Restart Card

Take an index card, sheet of paper, or plain note and write four lines:

> **LAST TRUE POINT:** What is already complete and usable?  
> **CHANGED SINCE THEN:** What fact, deadline, condition, or assumption is different now?  
> **NEXT VISIBLE ACTION:** What physical action would prove the work has resumed?  
> **RETURN TIME:** If it is not finished today, when exactly will you come back?

Keep each answer short.

Here is an example:

> **LAST TRUE POINT:** Draft has an approved scope and opening.  
> **CHANGED SINCE THEN:** The original Friday deadline passed.  
> **NEXT VISIBLE ACTION:** Confirm the new delivery date and add it to the proposal.  
> **RETURN TIME:** Today at 2:30 p.m. for 45 minutes.

Another:

> **LAST TRUE POINT:** All statements are downloaded.  
> **CHANGED SINCE THEN:** Two newer statements are now available.  
> **NEXT VISIBLE ACTION:** Download the two new files and place all statements in date order.  
> **RETURN TIME:** Monday at 9:00 a.m. for reconciliation.

The card is not a project plan.

It is a bridge between the interrupted version of you and the working version of you.

## Minutes 10–15: Decide what must be kept, updated, or released

Draw three headings:

> **KEEP | UPDATE | RELEASE**

### Keep

What remains useful?

- A completed section
- A correct decision
- A working tool
- A customer’s stated need
- A source document
- A tested process
- A promise that still should be honored
- A lesson already learned

Do not discard useful work because the project no longer feels fresh.

### Update

What changed during the interruption?

- The deadline
- The price
- The responsible person
- The available budget
- The facts
- The customer’s needs
- The order of operations
- Your capacity
- A legal, safety, or technical requirement

An old plan is not sacred. If reality changed, update the plan honestly.

### Release

What no longer belongs in the project?

- A deadline that is impossible and has not been renegotiated
- A feature no one needs
- A duplicated file
- A task that belongs to someone else
- A perfect first version
- An assumption that the evidence disproved
- A private punishment you invented because you are behind

Releasing is not automatically quitting.

Sometimes it is removing an outdated demand so the real responsibility can continue.

Choose no more than one or two items for each column. The timer is still running.

## Minutes 15–25: Complete one re-entry action

Now do the **Next Visible Action** from your Restart Card.

It should leave evidence.

Examples:

- Send the honest update.
- Add the missing price.
- Upload the document.
- Put the records in date order.
- Install the correct part using the proper instructions.
- Complete the next lesson exercise.
- Write the first 200 useful words.
- Make the appointment.
- Ask the decision-making question.
- Put the next work session on the calendar.

This is where a restart becomes real.

Reading about the project is not always re-entry. Thinking about the project is not always re-entry. Reorganizing every tool around the project is not always re-entry.

The action should alter the state of the responsibility.

Before, the customer had no update. Now the update is sent.

Before, the document was missing. Now it is uploaded.

Before, the records were scattered. Now the first month is ordered.

Before, the next step was vague. Now the decision question is in front of the right person.

Ten focused minutes will not complete every task. They can prove that the work is alive again.

If the next action is unsafe, illegal, outside your authority, or dependent on professional advice, do not force it. Choose the proper action: stop, document the question, and contact the qualified person.

Movement does not mean ignoring boundaries.

It means taking the next responsible step within them.

## Minutes 25–30: Leave a breadcrumb for the next return

Many projects are interrupted more than once.

That is why the final five minutes matter.

Do not stop with the work open and the next step trapped in your head.

Leave a breadcrumb:

- Name the current file clearly.
- Save it in the correct place.
- Write the next physical action at the top.
- Record the decision you just made.
- List the one fact still needed.
- Put the required material together.
- Set the return time.
- Close or store the work safely.

Finish this sentence:

> **When I return, I will begin by:** __________

Make the answer physical and visible.

“Work on the project” is not enough.

“Open the current proposal and write the payment terms” is clear.

“Continue organizing” is not enough.

“Sort March statements and mark missing pages” is clear.

“Get back into prayer” is not enough.

“Read Psalm 27, write one honest sentence, and pray for ten minutes” is clear.

You are giving your future self a doorway instead of another wall.

## The Restart Ramp checklist

Before the 30 minutes end, check your work:

- [ ] I chose one paused responsibility, not my entire life.
- [ ] I identified the last true point using evidence.
- [ ] I wrote what changed during the pause.
- [ ] I separated what to keep, update, and release.
- [ ] I completed one visible re-entry action.
- [ ] I recorded the next physical action.
- [ ] I set a specific return time if the work remains open.
- [ ] I addressed any missed commitment honestly.

If all you did was rebuild a perfect plan, you may still be standing beside the ramp.

The visible action is what puts your feet on it.

## What if the interruption was your fault?

Then truth is part of the restart.

If you avoided the work, admit it without turning the admission into theater.

If someone was waiting on you, send a clear update.

If the old deadline cannot be met, propose a realistic one.

If you made a mistake, state what happened, what you are doing to correct it, and what will prevent the same failure next time.

Avoid two extremes.

The first is hiding because you are embarrassed.

The second is writing a long emotional explanation that makes the other person comfort you before the work can continue.

A responsible restart can be brief:

> I did not complete this when I said I would. The current status is ____. I have now completed ____. I can deliver the next part by ____. If that timing no longer works, please tell me.

Use your own words and tell the truth. Do not promise a new date you have no reasonable plan to keep.

An apology does not replace the work.

But honest communication can reopen the path so the work can continue.

## What if the old project should not be resumed?

Not every unfinished project deserves another hour.

During the five-minute **Keep, Update, Release** review, you may discover that the responsible decision is to close it.

Reasons may include:

- The need no longer exists.
- The cost now exceeds the value.
- The work would violate a current obligation or boundary.
- The facts changed.
- A safer or simpler solution replaced it.
- The responsibility was never yours.
- Continuing would only protect pride or sunk cost.

If that is the truth, close the loop responsibly.

Notify anyone affected. Archive the useful materials. Record why the decision was made. Return borrowed items. Cancel unnecessary costs. Preserve records that must be retained.

A deliberate closure is different from silent abandonment.

The purpose of a Restart Ramp is not to force every old idea back to life.

It is to replace fog with a responsible decision.

## Faithfulness after interruption

Faith is not proved by pretending the interruption never happened.

It can be expressed by returning to what is still yours to do with honesty, humility, and endurance.

[Ezra 5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezra%205&version=KJV) describes rebuilding work that resumed after it had been stopped. The workers did not erase the opposition, delay, or questions surrounding the project. They returned to the work while the record and authority were examined.

The details belong to that biblical setting; they are not a promise that every modern project has divine approval or will succeed.

The practical reflection is narrower:

- What worthy work have I treated as dead merely because it was interrupted?
- What part of the old plan is still true?
- What must be corrected before I continue?
- What is one faithful action available today?
- What outcome am I trying to control that I need to place back in God’s hands?

A simple prayer:

> Lord, help me tell the truth about where I stopped. Show me what is still worth carrying, what needs to change, and what I should release. Give me courage to take the next faithful step and humility to make right what I neglected.

Then complete the re-entry action.

Prayer and responsibility do not compete.

Prayer can steady the heart while responsibility moves the hands.

## Today’s 30-minute challenge

Before noon, or during the next honest opening in your day:

1. Choose one interrupted responsibility.
2. Find the last true point.
3. Write the four-line Restart Card.
4. Identify one thing to keep, update, and release.
5. Complete one visible re-entry action.
6. Leave a breadcrumb for the next return.
7. Set the return time.
8. Send an honest update if someone has been waiting on you.

At the end, do not ask:

> “Did I catch up on everything?”

Ask:

> **“Is this responsibility moving again, and can I prove it?”**

The proof may be small.

One sent message. One completed section. One organized month. One scheduled appointment. One corrected deadline. One page written. One next step left clearly for tomorrow.

That is not starting over.

That is returning with better footing.
