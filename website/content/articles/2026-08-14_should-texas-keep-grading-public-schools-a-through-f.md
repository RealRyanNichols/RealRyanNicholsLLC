*By the Real Ryan Nichols Editorial Team*

A single letter can shape how a family sees a school before anyone walks through the front door.

An A can sound like proof that everything works. An F can sound like proof that nothing does. Neither conclusion is safe.

Texas uses A through F accountability ratings because state leaders want school performance to be visible and comparable. The Texas Education Agency says the system is built from three domains: Student Achievement, School Progress, and Closing the Gaps. Those domains draw on more than one measure, including STAAR performance, academic growth, graduation and readiness indicators where applicable, and outcomes for different student groups.

That is more complete than saying the grade comes from one test score. It is still a lot of information compressed into one letter.

The question is not whether schools should be accountable. They should be. The real question is whether a letter grade is the clearest form of accountability or an oversimplification that can cause its own damage.

## What the current system measures

The [Texas Education Agency's accountability explainer](https://tea.texas.gov/school-and-district-leaders/accountability/academic-accountability/performance-reporting/how-accountability-ratings-work) identifies three statewide domains.

Student Achievement asks what students know and whether older students are ready for college, career, or the military. School Progress looks at growth over time and, in one part of the framework, compares performance with schools serving similar grade levels and percentages of economically disadvantaged students. Closing the Gaps examines whether different student groups are succeeding.

The [final 2026 accountability manual](https://tea.texas.gov/school-and-district-leaders/accountability/academic-accountability/performance-reporting/2026-accountability-manual) supplies the technical rules. TEA also adopted a 2027 manual in March 2026 so districts had the next year's methodology before the school year began.

There is another important fact in the background. Texas enacted House Bill 8 in 2025, and TEA says the law will replace STAAR beginning with the 2027-28 school year while also changing parts of future accountability. That means the testing system is moving, but the argument over public letter grades is not going away.

## The strongest case for keeping A through F

Parents should not need a statistics degree to find out whether a school is serving students well.

A clear grade forces the state to show its work in public. It gives families a starting point, helps local boards identify campuses that need urgent attention, and makes it harder for poor results to disappear inside a two-hundred-page report.

Supporters can also fairly argue that Texas does not look only at raw achievement. Including progress matters because a campus serving students who enter far behind can still receive credit for moving them forward. Closing the Gaps matters because a strong average can hide students who are being left behind.

There is a democratic value in a common measuring stick. Every district can describe its own strengths. A statewide system asks whether those descriptions match outcomes that can be compared.

And without a visible consequence, accountability can become a word everybody uses and nobody feels.

## The strongest case for changing it

A letter is easy to understand because it leaves so much out.

Two schools with the same grade may face radically different staffing conditions, student mobility, transportation burdens, course offerings, and community resources. A one-letter label can become a judgment about teachers, students, and neighborhoods even when the underlying data tell a more complicated story.

Critics can also reasonably worry about incentives. When a public grade carries reputational and financial consequences, schools may feel pressure to devote more time to what is measured and less to what is difficult to quantify. A test can measure something important without measuring everything important.

There is also a timing problem. Accountability data look backward. Families make decisions now. A campus may have new leadership, a stronger reading program, or a serious vacancy problem that the latest grade does not yet capture.

The answer does not have to be no accountability. Texas could publish a dashboard that places achievement, growth, gaps, staffing, attendance, course access, and family survey results side by side without collapsing them into one symbol. The cost would be complexity. The benefit would be a more honest picture.

## Ryan's Take

Texas should keep a statewide accountability system, but the public-facing letter should not be allowed to stand alone.

If the state is going to put one character beside a school, the same screen should make the component scores, year-over-year movement, student-group gaps, and meaningful local context impossible to miss. A grade should open the report, not end the conversation.

The hard truth is that both sides can misuse simplicity. School systems can bury failure in context and jargon. State leaders can use one letter to pretend a complex institution has been fully explained.

Families deserve clarity without false certainty.

Read the grade. Then read what built it. Look at growth. Look at gaps. Visit the campus. Ask what changed. Ask what the school is doing next. Accountability should produce better questions and better decisions, not just a color on a map.

That same principle matters in culture and civic policy.
