For months, a certain corner of X was certain about one thing: Christopher Nolan's *The Odyssey* was going to flop.

The casting of Lupita Nyong'o as Helen of Troy wasn't "historically accurate," they said. The film went "woke," they said. Elon Musk himself weighed in suggesting nobody would show up. The trailer became, by some measures, the most disliked movie trailer of Nolan's career — 352,000 dislikes against 56,000 likes on YouTube.

Confident. Loud. Everywhere in your replies for weeks.

Then Thursday night happened.

[Watch on X →](https://x.com/NolanAnalyst/status/2077807198915149935)

$17.6 million in previews on 3,900 North American screens — the best of the year. A path to a $117 million-plus opening weekend, Nolan's biggest debut outside the Batman trilogy and the top R-rated opening of 2026. An audience score of 96%, higher than *Oppenheimer*, higher than *The Dark Knight*, higher than anything he's made except *Memento*.

Ticket sites were reportedly crashing under demand. One tweet making the rounds put it simply: *"But the online grifters were telling me that it was gonna flop and destroy Nolan's career."*

## I've watched this exact pattern before

I wrote recently about Elmo caving to an online pile-on over a neutral NBA Finals post — millions of reactions, real pressure, a real cave. That story was about volume mistaken for correctness. This one is the mirror image: volume that turned out to be flatly, measurably wrong.

Both stories teach the same lesson. Being loud on X is not the same as being right. It never was. The accounts predicting *The Odyssey*'s failure weren't basing that prediction on test screenings, or advance ticket data, or anything resembling evidence. They were basing it on a casting decision they didn't like, dressed up as a market prediction because "this will flop" travels further than "I don't like this creative choice."

It traveled. It just wasn't true.

## Why we keep falling for this

I don't think most people who repeated the flop predictions were lying. I think they were doing what all of us do when a claim matches what we already want to believe — we stop checking it against anything real.

A confident prediction feels like information. It has the shape of information — a number, a timeframe, a definitive verb. But confidence is not evidence, and neither is repetition. Enough accounts saying the same thing starts to feel like consensus, even when every single one of them is repeating the same unverified claim to each other in a closed loop.

The box office didn't care about the loop. Audiences didn't care about the loop. They bought tickets, watched a movie, and rated it 96%.

## The part that actually matters going forward

This isn't really about a movie. Box office predictions being wrong is not a crisis — Hollywood gets these calls wrong constantly, in both directions.

What matters is the pattern: a specific, loud, confident narrative formed on X well before any actual data existed, spread because it was engaging rather than because it was accurate, and collapsed the moment real numbers showed up. That happens with movies. It also happens with elections, with legal cases, with public figures, with pretty much anything people have a stake in being true.

The fix isn't complicated, even if it's inconvenient: wait for the data. Let the confident hot take earn its confidence instead of borrowing it from volume. The accounts that were sure *The Odyssey* would flop are still on X today, still posting with the same confidence about the next thing. Nothing about being wrong here will cost them their audience.

That's on us, not them.
