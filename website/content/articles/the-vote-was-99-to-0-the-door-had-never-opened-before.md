The number was 99 to 0.

That sounds like the easy part of the story.

It was not.

On September 21, 1981, the United States Senate confirmed Sandra Day O’Connor to the Supreme Court. The official [Senate nomination record](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/nominations/SupremeCourtNominations1789present.htm) shows the vote, the date, and the result.

Four days later, she took the oath and became the first woman to serve on the Court.

The vote was unanimous.

The door had still taken nearly two centuries to open.

## Before the vote came the closed offices

O’Connor graduated near the top of her class at Stanford Law School in 1952.

The Supreme Court’s [exhibition on her early career](https://www.supremecourt.gov/visiting/exhibitions/SOCExhibit/Section2.aspx) says no California law firm offered to hire her as a lawyer because of her gender. One firm offered a legal-secretary position.

She kept looking.

When the San Mateo County attorney said there was no money for another deputy, O’Connor wrote a letter explaining how she could be useful and offered to work without pay if necessary. The office found a way to give her a position.

That is not a romantic detail about “paying dues.”

It is evidence of what the barrier looked like.

A person could finish near the top of a major law school and still be told the professional door was not built for her.

## She learned government from three sides

O’Connor’s path did not move straight from law school to Washington.

She worked in public law, private practice, politics, and the courts. She served in Arizona’s executive branch as an assistant attorney general, in the legislative branch as a state senator, and in the judicial branch as a trial and appellate judge.

The Ronald Reagan Presidential Library’s [history of her appointment](https://www.reaganlibrary.gov/education/letters-and-president-close-reading/letters-constituents-appointment-sandra-day-oconnor) notes that experience across all three branches.

By the time President Ronald Reagan nominated her in 1981, the résumé was not symbolic.

It was built.

The first did not arrive because the standard disappeared. She arrived with a record strong enough to make the old exclusion look as indefensible as it had always been.

## The hearing was another first

O’Connor’s confirmation hearing lasted three days and became the first televised Supreme Court confirmation hearing, according to the Reagan Library.

The country was not only deciding whether one nominee would serve.

It was watching a woman answer questions for a seat no woman had ever held.

Every answer carried two burdens. She had to demonstrate her own judgment while the public treated her as evidence about whether a woman belonged there at all.

That is one of the hidden costs of being first.

The person is judged as an individual and a category at the same time.

## A unanimous vote does not erase the resistance

The 99-0 tally can make the ending look inevitable.

History rarely feels inevitable to the person walking through it.

There were offices that would not hire her as a lawyer. There were professional systems designed around men. There were political objections to her nomination. There was a Court that had existed since 1789 without a woman on the bench.

Then, on one September day, every senator present voted yes.

That vote did not rewrite the years before it. It did establish a new fact that could not be talked back into the old order.

A woman had been confirmed to the Supreme Court.

Four days later, a woman sat there.

## First should never mean last

The Supreme Court’s historical exhibit records O’Connor’s later line: “It’s fine to be the first, but you don’t want to be the last.”

That is the responsibility inside a first.

Breaking a barrier matters. Making the opening survivable for the next person matters too.

O’Connor served for nearly 25 years. The Court’s account says she authored 676 opinions, including 301 opinions of the Court. Her work could not be reduced to the biography of her appointment.

The historic door became a daily job.

That may be the most durable part of the story. A breakthrough gets remembered as a moment. The institution changes because somebody keeps showing up after the cameras leave.

## The number and the door

Ninety-nine to zero is a clean number.

Human progress is not clean.

It includes exclusion, preparation, compromise, pressure, timing, courage, and work that begins long before the public can see the result.

The Senate vote on September 21, 1981, did not prove that every barrier had fallen.

It proved that one old sentence was no longer true.

The Supreme Court had never had a woman justice.

Then it did.
