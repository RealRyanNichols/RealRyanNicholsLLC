Conor McGregor hadn't fought in five years. He hadn't even held a live press conference in five years.

So naturally, when he finally sat back down behind a microphone ahead of UFC 329 — his rematch with Max Holloway, thirteen years after their first fight — he didn't talk about takedown defense or fight IQ.

He sang Oasis.

The room asked him a throwaway question: whose comeback is bigger, his or the Gallagher brothers reuniting Oasis? McGregor answered by grabbing the mic and belting "Wonderwall" to a packed press room, his wife and kids in the crowd singing along with him. Minutes later, during the staredown photo op, he jogged up to Holloway and yanked the sunglasses clean off his face.

By the next morning, that was the story. Not the tale of the tape. Not the odds. A press conference karaoke bit and a pair of stolen shades.

## This wasn't a loss of control. It was the plan.

I've watched enough press conferences, campaign events, and courtroom-adjacent media circuses to recognize the difference between someone melting down in public and someone who understands exactly how attention moves through a room.

McGregor is the second kind.

A fighter who hasn't competed in half a decade needed one thing above all else heading into a pay-per-view: to be the most talked-about person in the sport for a solid week. Technical breakdowns of his striking don't do that. A grown man belting a 1990s Britpop anthem while his family cheers him on, immediately followed by a bit of theater with his opponent's sunglasses — that does it instantly, and it does it for free.

Every combat-sports outlet ran the clip. Every casual fan who doesn't follow MMA closely saw it cross their feed. That is a marketing achievement, not an accident.

## The line between promotion and disrespect

Where I'd push back on some of the online pile-on aimed at McGregor: there's a difference between theater and cruelty, and this stayed on the right side of it. Nobody got hurt. Nobody got humiliated beyond a pair of sunglasses and a captive audience. Holloway, by all accounts, gave as good as he got in the trash talk that followed.

Compare that to how often we watch actual character assassination — rumors, doctored clips, quotes stripped of context — get dressed up as "just promotion" or "just entertainment" and passed around as if intent doesn't matter. It does. A stunt aimed at hype is not the same thing as a smear aimed at reputation, even when both are loud.

McGregor's bit was loud, silly, and consensual theater between two guys who get in a cage together willingly. That's a very different animal from the manufactured outrage cycles that eat people alive over a misread sentence.

## What actually matters once the cage door closes

None of the pre-fight noise — the singing, the sunglasses, the trash talk — decides anything. Thirteen years is a long time. Five years out of competition is a long layoff at the highest level of a sport built on twitch reflexes and a chin that can take a punch. Holloway has been active, sharp, and dangerous the entire time McGregor has been away from the sport doing everything except fighting.

The press conference tells you who's better at getting attention. It tells you nothing about who's better at fighting.

That gap — between who dominates the narrative and who actually wins when it counts — is worth sitting with. It shows up far outside of MMA. Loudest doesn't mean rightest. Best promoted doesn't mean best. We should all get more comfortable holding those two truths at once: this was great theater, and theater is not the same thing as the result.
