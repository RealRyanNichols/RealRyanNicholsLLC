For most of July, Christopher Nolan's "The Odyssey" was trending on X for reasons that had nothing to do with the movie itself.

Casting choices sparked backlash weeks before release. Commentators picked fights over modern dialogue in a Homer adaptation. Nolan was asked about it directly and told The Telegraph that pre-release criticism is "always irrelevant, because no one having them knows what the film actually is yet." That answer became its own controversy.

Then, on July 17, 2026, the film actually opened — and the people whose job is to watch movies before anyone else finally got to weigh in.

Film journalist Simon Thompson posted this after seeing it:

https://x.com/ShowbizSimon/status/2074244611040739734

"Christopher Nolan's #TheOdyssey is flawless filmmaking, every inch as epic as you'd expect. Leading a stellar cast, Matt Damon gives Odysseus everything in a career-best powerhouse performance. Robert Pattinson is outstanding as Antinous and John Leguizamo's Eumaeus is sublime."

That is not a lone outlier. Critics across the trade press called it a "staggering achievement," a "colossal achievement of scale, even by Nolan's standards," and one of the best films of the year. Anne Thompson, one of the most trusted Oscar forecasters in the business, named it the current Best Picture front-runner and floated Damon for his first acting Oscar.

## Two Timelines, One Movie

Here's what strikes me about this whole cycle: the loudest conversation about "The Odyssey" and the most informed conversation about "The Odyssey" happened almost entirely without overlap.

The backlash cycle ran on casting announcements, trailer stills, and secondhand outrage. The review cycle ran on people who had actually sat in a theater for the runtime and watched what Nolan built. One of those groups had access to the thing being argued about. The other did not.

I'm not writing this to tell anyone how to feel about casting decisions — that's a real conversation people are entitled to have. What I'm pointing at is the order of operations. A huge amount of energy got spent forming a verdict on a film before the film existed to the public in any watchable form. Nolan's answer — that pre-release arguments are "irrelevant" until people can actually see the work — turned out to be a preview of exactly what happened next: the people who saw it came away stunned, and the loudest pre-release predictions didn't hold up against a single frame of actual footage.

## Why This Isn't Just a Hollywood Story

I see this pattern everywhere, not just in movie discourse. Verdicts get formed and broadcast before the evidence is actually in front of anyone. Once a narrative locks in online, it moves faster than the facts that are supposed to inform it — and by the time the real information shows up, half the audience has already moved on to the next argument.

"The Odyssey" got a rare second chance to be judged on the work itself, because the release date eventually arrived and people could actually watch it. Most disputes online don't get that clean of a resolution. There's no premiere night where the full picture drops all at once and settles the argument. Usually the noise just fades and a new fight takes its place, with the original question never actually resolved.

That's worth sitting with the next time a controversy trends before anyone involved has seen, read, or heard the thing being argued about.

## The Part Everyone Agrees On

Whatever side of the pre-release fight you were on, the reviews converged hard on one point: Matt Damon's performance as Odysseus is being called the best of his career, full stop. When critics who disagree on almost everything else land on the same praise for the same performance, that's usually a sign it's real.

Nolan set out to make his biggest film yet — by his own account, roughly triple the large-scale set pieces of any of his previous movies. Early box office projections had it opening between $80 and $100 million domestically. Whatever you think about the discourse that preceded it, the work now exists, and it's the work that's going to outlast the pre-release arguments.
